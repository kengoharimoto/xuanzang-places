# Notes on disputed places: juan 6–7

Range: Śrāvastī, Kapilavastu, Rāmagrāma, Kuśinagara (juan 6); Vārāṇasī, 戰主國, Vaiśālī, Vṛji, Nepal (juan 7).
Sources: `places.tsv`, `places_mizutani.tsv`, `coded/auth_*.tsv`, `coded/gaz_batch*.tsv` + `gaz_hits.tsv`,
`coded/asr_batch*.tsv`, `coded/ar_batch*.tsv`. Gazetteer volumes are cited by district and year. Where a volume exists
in two scans, it is counted once. Hit numbers are given only where a passage needs to be found again. The
Archaeological Survey reports are cited by volume and page: ASR I (1871), III (1873), VIII (1878), XI (1880), XII
(1879), XVI (1883), XVIII (1883), XXII (1885); authors are Cunningham unless named (Beglar VIII, Carlleyle XII, XVIII,
XXII, Garrick in XVI). "Mukherji 1901" is P. C. Mukherji's *Report on a Tour of Exploration of the Antiquities in the
Tarai, Nepal*; "Smith 1901" is V. A. Smith's prefatory note to it. The Survey's Annual Reports (1903–37) are cited as
AR with the year and page, by author (Vogel, Marshall, Konow, Oertel, Sahni, Hirananda Sastri, Pargiter, Page); "EC"
is the Bengal (Eastern) Circle report of the same year. Inscriptions are cited from *Epigraphia Indica* (EI, by
volume) and the *Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum* (CII 1, Hultzsch 1925; CII 3, Bhandarkar, revised by Chhabra and
Gai), and the excavations after 1953 from *Indian Archaeology – A Review* (IAR, by year and page), by excavator.

Not noted (no criterion met): 07.01, 07.01.02, 07.03.03, 07.03.04, 07.05. The survey confirms Mizutani for 07.01
(Benares, ASR I p. 115); Ghosh's summary of the Rajghat excavation (IAR 1957–58) dates the ancient city from NBP to
medieval times. For 07.03.04, the Bakhra/Kolhua lion pillar, Cunningham measured over 44 ft and put it at "about 50
feet" originally, as Xuanzang says (ASR I pp. 62–64, XVI pp. 13–15). Bloch (AR 1903–04, pp. 81–86; EC 1903–04, pp.
14–15) found that the Kolhua pillar, the stupa N of it and the tank S of it "tallies exactly" with Xuanzang's Aśoka
group. Later excavation exposed the group: two mud stupas by the pillar, the northern one with a relic vessel (Sita
Ram Roy, IAR 1959–60, pp. 14–16); a brick stupa 24.5 m across in front of the pillar (IAR 1973–74, pp. 67–68); and a
monastery, Gupta votive stupas and the tank (L. C. Singh and Muhammed K. K., IAR 1989–99; see "New", monkey tank).
Mizutani's 07.03.03 is itself the relic stupa that Altekar found by following Xuanzang (IAR 1957–58, pp. 10–11):
a mud stupa of NBP date, about half a mile NW of Raja-Visal-ka-garh, enlarged four times (first with Mauryan
polished-stone fragments), with a soapstone casket "only a quarter full" in a breach of the core and no
inscription. That fits the *Record of India*, which Xuanzang cites: Aśoka opened the stupa and took nine-tenths of
the relics. Page's remarks (AR 1922–23 p. 149, 1924–25 p. 86) only restate Rummindei for Lumbinī (06.02.11).

### 06.01 室羅伐悉底國 (Śrāvastī) — Sahet-Mahet, settled by inscriptions from the excavations of 1907–11

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sahet-Mahet on the Rāpti, Bahraich/Gonda border (agreed). Cunningham (1871, p. 469),
Beal (p. 1), Majumdar (1924, p. 711) and Dey: Sahet-Mahet. Watters (p. 378) would set it aside for V. A. Smith's Nepal site.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR I pp. 330–347) found a statue inscribed with "the name of Sravasti itself" (p. 331),
measured the ramparts at 17,300 ft against Xuanzang's 20 li, and had to emend the 500 li from Ayodhyā to 350. Smith
(1901, pp. 7–8, 19–20) put the city in Nepal NE of Nepalganj on bearings and hearsay.

**Excavations.** Vogel (AR 1907–08, pp. 90, 120, 131–32) re-surveyed Mahet at 17,250 ft, and a copper plate of
Govindachandra (A.D. 1130) from Sahet, granting villages to "the great convent of the holy Jetavana", "conclusively
settles the identification of Saheth with the Jetavana … and consequently that of Maheth with the city of
Sravasti". A Bodhisattva "in the Jetavana of Sravasti" followed (Sahni, AR 1908–09, pp. 137–38). The same seasons
rejected Cunningham's monuments inside Mahet (see "New").

**Inscriptions.** Bloch (EI 9) showed from the umbrella staff that the inscribed Bodhisattva was found in situ;
Sahni (EI 11, pp. 20–21) edited the Saheth plate. Harṣa's Madhuban plate of A.D. 631 grants a village "in the bhukti
of Śrāvastī" (Bühler, EI 1, pp. 71–72; Kielhorn, EI 7, p. 137): the name was a province in Xuanzang's day.

**Later excavations.** Sinha's trench across Mahet's N rampart (IAR 1958–59, p. 50) found Painted Grey Ware
occupation before the defences. The Survey–Kansai University seasons (IAR 1986–2001) exposed the Kandbhari gate, a
rammed road of pre-Mauryan or Mauryan date, and Kushan and Gupta houses inside Mahet, taking Mahet = Śrāvastī as
settled; nothing new names the city.

**Gazetteers.** Gonda 1905 reports Bloch's answer; Gonda 1989 adds Kushana inscriptions and seals; Oudh N–Z 1878
maps the city onto Mahet's mounds (see "New").

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 6's distances and bearings takes where
the Rapti leaves the hills NE of Nepalganj (Smith (Mukherji 1901)) here, not Mizutani's Sahet-Mahet (Sravasti ruins),
Uttar Pradesh (route/README.md §6). The figures are one kind of evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 6 takes where the Rapti leaves
the hills NE of Nepalganj (Smith (Mukherji 1901)) here, not Mizutani's Sahet-Mahet (Sravasti ruins), Uttar Pradesh
(route/README.md §7).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Sahet-Mahet, Mahet the city and Sahet the Jetavana, after Cunningham, with
Smith's Nepal site rejected for want of remains and for needing Faxian's distance emended (p. 484).

**Assessment.** Four inscriptions from the site, one naming the Jetavana outright, settle the identification and
dispose of Smith's Nepal site and Watters' doubt; the later excavations show a walled city occupied from before
the Mauryas. Mizutani is strengthened.

### 06.01.04 逝多林給孤獨園 (Jetavana) — Sahet, named on a copper plate and an inscribed Bodhisattva

**Identifications.** Mizutani names no site, though his remark that inscriptions show the monastery flourishing until
about the 12th century assumes the excavated site (unknown). Majumdar (p. 711): the Sahet monastery. Dey (after
Vogel): Sahet = Jetavana, Mahet = the city. Watters (p. 384): no site.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR I pp. 334–343) identified the Jetavana with the Jogini-jharia mound (Sahet), "just half a
mile distant from the south-west corner of the old city". In 1876 (ASR XI pp. 81–86) he excavated twenty mounds and
placed the Gandhakuṭī at temple 2 and the Kosamba-kuṭī at temple 3.

**Excavations.** Vogel (AR 1907–08, pp. 120, 131–32) found the Govindachandra plate of A.D. 1130 in a brick case under
a monastery cell, granting six villages to the friars "residing in the great convent of the holy Jetavana". A
Bodhisattva set up "in the Jetavana of Sravasti" by Kṣatriya brothers of Mathurā followed (Sahni, AR 1908–09, pp.
134, 137–38). Marshall (AR 1910–11, pp. 7, 11–12) found an early road running E from temple 2, "amply evident that
General Cunningham was right in his location of the east gate".

**Inscriptions.** Sahni (EI 11, pp. 20–21) edited the plate, "carefully packed in an earthen box" under a cell of
the monastery Hoey had partly dug, beside the Kushan Bodhisattvas inscribed "Sravasti" and "Jetavana of Sravasti".

**Later excavations.** The Survey–Kansai University seasons at the Jetavana mound (Raman and Aboshi, IAR 1986–87,
p. 76; 1987–88, pp. 106–108; Bhengra and Aboshi, 1988–89, pp. 82–84) found a Kushana terraced tank W of the
Gandhakuṭī, a circular stupa with cells, Kushana monasteries and Gupta temples, occupation from the 1st to the 12th
century, and a post-Gupta "ye dharmā hetu" sealing; no new inscription names the site.

**Gazetteers.** Oudh N–Z 1878 repeats Cunningham's ruin. Gonda 1989: Nagari inscriptions show that "Jetavana remained
a centre of Buddhism".

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Sahet is the Jetavana (pp. 484, 490).

**Assessment.** The site is named on the spot by two inscriptions eleven centuries apart, the excavated plan keeps
Cunningham's gate and shrines, and the later seasons show it in use through Xuanzang's time. Mizutani's implicit
identification is strengthened.

### 06.01.13 得眼林 (Andhavana) — Cunningham's grove at Rajgarh Gulariya fits the distance; Ikauna does not

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no site (unknown). Dey (after Führer): ruins near Ikauna, Bahraich district. Watters (p.
398) discusses the name only.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR I pp. 343–344) took Xuanzang's and Faxian's 4 li NW of the Jetavana to the village of
Rajgarh Gulariya, "situated in the midst of a very large grove of trees" about two-thirds of a mile NW. He found no
trace of Aśoka's stupa or pillar there.

**Gazetteers.** Oudh H–M 1877 and Bahraich 1921 derive the name Ikauna from "Arkhavana", "a place mentioned by Hwen
Thsang as being situated slightly to the west of Sahet Mahet". The evidence is the place-name alone.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji names no site; he gives the name as Āptanetravana (also Andhavana) and Faxian's 4 li
NW of the vihāra (p. 505).

**Assessment.** Ikauna is about 5 miles away against Xuanzang's 4 li, and "Arkhavana" is not attested as a form of
Andhavana. Rajgarh Gulariya at least fits the distance and bearing, but it rests on position and old trees, with no
remains. It is a weak candidate where Mizutani has none.

### 06.01.14 迦葉波佛本生城 (Kāśyapa's birthplace) — Tandwa at 60 li; Marshall's Bhitti only at Beal's 16 li

**Identifications.** Mizutani (disputed) reports Beal and Legge: Tandwa (Tadwa), 9 miles W of Sahet-Mahet. Giles:
Faxian's Nabhiga. Watters: a name like Topi. Mizutani gives no view of his own, and no coded authority names a site.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR I pp. 351–352) found a brick stupa at least 70 ft across at Tandwa, with a tank to its
north that "precludes the possibility" of another stupa N of the town. In 1876 (ASR XI pp. 71–75) he cleared the
casing and took the Bar-ki-bhari mound 3,700 ft WNW for the old town and a mound 300 ft ESE for the stupa of the
meeting with the father.

**Excavations.** Marshall (AR 1910–11, pp. 3–4) proposed a small early settlement near Bhitti, about 2.5 miles a
little N of W of Mahet, with the base of a brick stupa 56 ft square of Mauryan-size bricks to its north, as Xuanzang
puts the relic stupa. He offered it only "if [Beal's] reading is correct" (16 li) and noted that "the weight of other
authorities", Julien, Watters and Legge, gives 60 li. He did not discuss Tandwa, and found nothing south of Bhitti for
the stupa of the meeting.

**Gazetteers.** Oudh H–M 1877 and Bahraich 1921 repeat Cunningham and add a Buddhist statue worshipped as Sītā and a
mound of 800 × 300 ft; Bahraich 1921 notes that it "depends … on the acceptance of Sahet Mahet", now settled (06.01).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji names no site; he cites the Mahāsāṅghika Vinaya (都夷聚落) and Faxian's Duwei (都維), "50
li W of the city" (p. 505), a third figure beside the 60 and 16 li discussed here, closer to the majority 60.

**Assessment.** With Śrāvastī fixed, Tandwa fits the 60 li of most texts and has a stupa and town mound in the right
relation; Bhitti has an early stupa in the right relation but fits only the minority reading. Nothing inscribed
names either. Tandwa remains the better answer, with Bhitti as the rival.

### 06.02 劫比羅伐窣堵國 (Kapilavastu) — Piprahwa–Ganwaria, named on Kushan sealings; Tilaurakot the rival for Xuanzang's city

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Tilaurakot, Nepal Tarai (agreed). Cunningham (1871): Nagar, Basti. Watters (p. 17), Majumdar (pp. 711–12) and Dey: Tilaurakot; Majumdar reports
Fleet's Piprahwa.

**Survey.** Carlleyle's Bhuila Dih (ASR XII), accepted by Cunningham, fell (Basti 1907, after Führer). Bühler (EI
5, pp. 1–5), from the Rummindei pillar, moved the Śākya country north into the Tarai. Smith (1901, pp. 10–12, 21–22)
took Piprahwa, 9 miles from Rummindei, for "the Kapilavastu of Fa-hien" and Tilaurakot, 14 miles, for "the
Kapilavastu of Hiuen Tsiang". Mukherji (1901, pp. 20–21, 47–54) found at Tilaurakot a brick fort about 5,200 ft
round, and rejected Piprahwa.

**Later excavations.** *Tilaurakot:* Mitra (IAR 1961–62, pp. 73–74) found NBP-period habitation under a rampart
and brick wall, Mauryan–Śuṅga finds and one unread inscribed sealing, "nothing to establish or disprove the
identification". *Piprahwa–Ganwaria:* Srivastava found two more relic caskets below Peppé's (IAR 1971–72, p. 45).
In 1972–73 (p. 33) 31 sealings "in Brahmi characters of the first-second century A.D." from a verandah of the
eastern monastery gave "Om Devaputra Vihare Kapilavastu Bhikshu Sanghasa", the Kapilavastu monks' community in the
Devaputra (Kushan) vihāra; the second legend is not given. Ganwaria's brick
complexes became "the residential complex of … Sakya king Suddhodhana" (1974–75, p. 41), a "school" and shrines
over houses, matched to Xuanzang (1975–77); no level after the 4th century is reported.

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 6's distances and bearings takes
Piprahwa (Fleet (via Majumdar 1924)) here, not Mizutani's Tilaurakot, Nepal Tarai (route/README.md §6). The figures
are one kind of evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 6 takes Nagar-khas on a lake E
of the Ghagra (Cunningham 1871) here, not Mizutani's Tilaurakot, Nepal Tarai (route/README.md §7).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji reports Cunningham's Nagar Khas and Mansurnagar, Smith's Piprahwa and Rhys Davids'
and Mukherji's Tilaurakot, then gives the last word to the Piprahwa excavations of 1971–74: relic caskets, and over
fifty sealings "of about the turn of the era", some forty of them reading Kapilavastu in Brahmi, with a pot lid
bearing the same legend, from which "the excavators consider this the true site" (pp. 508–509). His source is K. M.
Srivastava, "Kapilavastu and its precise location", *East and West* 29 (1979), pp. 61–74 (Ji prints "West and East"),
new to this study; the count of about forty inscribed sealings, against 31 in IAR 1972–73, would include the later
seasons. Ji's own sketch is unreliable on one point: he makes Führer's Nigali Sagar pillar of 1895 the proof of
Lumbinī, which it is not (that pillar names Kanakamuni; Lumbinī is the Rummindei pillar, 06.02.11).

**Assessment.** The sealings are the only inscriptions naming Kapilavastu on any site, and 31 from one verandah (about
forty by the end of the 1971–74 seasons, with a pot lid bearing the same legend: Srivastava 1979, reported by Ji) are
more likely the monastery's own than letters received (the doubt once raised at Kasia, 06.04). So in the 1st–2nd
century the Piprahwa monastery was Kapilavastu's. But they name a community four centuries before Xuanzang, not his
city, and the palace is inference. Tilaurakot has a brick-walled city (smaller than his 14–15 li), and his route puts
Krakucchanda's pillar town 50 li S of the city: the in-situ Gotihawa pillar (06.02.05) lies S of Tilaurakot but on the
map NW of Piprahwa. On the evidence now, Piprahwa–Ganwaria is the better-supported Kapilavastu, at medium confidence;
Tilaurakot remains the rival for the city Xuanzang was shown (Smith's two-city view). The satellite notes
06.02.05–06.02.10 are reckoned from Tilaurakot.

### 06.02.05 迦羅迦村馱佛本生城 (Krakucchanda's town) — Gutiva, with an Aśoka-pillar stump, against Smith's Palta Devi

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no site (unknown). Cunningham (p. 480): Kakila or Kalwari Khas (Nagar frame). Watters (p.
18): Führer's ruins about 7 miles SW of Kapilavastu. Majumdar (p. 712) reports Vost: Sisanihava. Dey (after Mukherji):
Gutiva, 4 miles S of Tilaura.

**Survey.** Carlleyle (ASR XII pp. 177–182) put the town at Nagra, 8 miles NW of Bhuila, with one standing stupa. Smith
(1901, pp. 10, 13) measured Xuanzang's 50 li S from Lori-ki-kudan to Palta Devi, where a broken pillar is worshipped as
Mahadeo, or to a spot S of Chandapar. Mukherji (1901, pp. 54–56) reduced the 50 li to "about 20" and chose Gutiva: "here
is a brick Stupa, near which is the lower portion of an Asoka-pillar". He took the large mound NE of the village for
the birthplace. Smith (p. 5) holds that the Gutiva pillar "is in its original position" but assigns it to the
Nyagrodha grove (06.02.08).

**Inscriptions.** Bühler (EI 5, p. 6) passes on Führer's report of Krakucchanda's nirvāṇa stupa, "still eighty feet
high", 7 miles SW of Kapilavastu; Mukherji found Führer's identifications "full of contradictions" (see "New").

**Later excavations.** Mitra, revisiting Mukherji's sites (IAR 1961–62, p. 72), found at Gotihawa "in situ the
lower part" of an Aśokan pillar, as Smith had held.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji gives the name only (Krakucchanda, Pāli Kakusandha; p. 514).

**Assessment.** Carlleyle's Nagra falls with Bhuila. Gutiva has the field evidence Xuanzang describes, a relic stupa
with an Aśokan pillar beside it still in place, but only at an emended distance and only if Tilaurakot is the
city (06.02). Palta Devi fits the distance and has an uninscribed
pillar. Gutiva is the better candidate, at low confidence.

### 06.02.05a 迦諾迦牟尼佛本生城 (Kanakamuni's town) — near the Nigali Sagar pillar; whether it is in situ is the question

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Aśoka pillar at Nigali Sagar, 1 mile S of Nigliva (disputed: the pillar may have
been moved). Watters (p. 18): near Nigliva. Majumdar (pp. 711–12): "not in situ"; Vost: Gutanihava. Dey (after
Mukherji): Araura. Cunningham (p. 480): Subhay Pursa; Beal (p. 19): Kanakpur.

**Survey.** Carlleyle (ASR XII p. 185) placed the town at Khopoa Dih near Kanakpur, 6 miles W of Bhuila, on the village
name. Smith (1901, pp. 4, 13, 16) states that "the Nigali Sagar pillar is not in situ" and was moved 8 or 13 miles from
Sisania Panre or Palta Devi; he takes Sisania for the town. Mukherji (1901, pp. 29–30, 56–57) argues that a pillar
"about 3 feet in diameter and about 50 feet in length" could not have been moved far. He found no brick stupa near it,
derived the neighbouring village name Thamua from *stambha*, and took Araura-kot for the town.

**Inscriptions.** Bühler (EI 5, p. 6) reads the Nigliva edict: the stupa of Konākamana was enlarged in Aśoka's
reign. Hultzsch (CII 1, p. xxiii) takes the pillar for the one Xuanzang saw before Kanakamuni's relic stupa, but,
with Smith, "not in situ"; no stupa has been traced near it. Mitra (IAR 1961–62, p. 72) only names Nigali Sagar
among the sites revisited.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees with the caution here: Führer's Nigali Sagar pillar, 13 miles NW of Lumbinī,
may be Xuanzang's, but whether it is, and whether it stands where it was set up, is "not yet settled" (p. 515).

**Assessment.** The inscription fixes the pillar to Kanakamuni; the corpus editor sides with Smith's doubt, and
nothing since has answered it. If it stands where Aśoka set it, the town is close by (Araura-kot); if not, Sisania.
Mizutani's cautious formulation stands.

### 06.02.06 贍部樹蔭 (the Jambu-tree shade, ploughing stupa) — unidentified; Mukherji points to Jadi

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no site (unknown). No authority names one.

**Survey.** Mukherji (1901, p. 55) measured Xuanzang's 40 li NE of Tilaurakot to the neighbourhood of Jadi on the
Jamuar, whose name he derives from *jambu*, "which tree abounds its banks". But "I did not hear of a mound in the
neighbourhood".

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** A name and a distance, with no remains. The site remains unidentified; Jadi is a lead to test.

### 06.02.08 尼拘律樹林 (Nyagrodha grove / monastery) — Gutiva (Smith) or Lori-Kudan (Mukherji), whose dug mound was Brahmanical

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no site (unknown). Dey (after Mukherji): "the largest mound to the south of Lori-Kudan,
which is one mile to the east of Gutiva".

**Survey.** Mukherji (1901, pp. 53–54) puts the Nyagrodha monastery at Lori-Kudan, about 2 miles S of Tilaurakot, where
there is "a solid brick mound, very like a Stupa", and the related stupas on the other Lori-Kudan mounds. Smith (1901,
p. 12) takes Gutiva, with its pillar, for the grove "half a mile to the south" of the city.

**Later excavations.** Mitra (IAR 1961–62, p. 72) excavated at Kudan, 3 km NE of Gotihawa: a well with a carved
brick platform and two early medieval Brahmanical brick temples (liṅga, Mahiṣāsuramardinī), nothing Buddhist.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji gives the name only (nyagrodha; p. 519).

**Assessment.** Both proposals belong to the Tilaurakot frame (06.02). Mitra's dig at Kudan weakens Mukherji's
Lori-Kudan without excluding its other mounds; Gutiva is untested. Both are candidates only.

### 06.02.10 箭泉 (Śarakūpa, the Arrow Well) — Birdpur if Piprahwa is Kapilavastu; Sisania if Tilaurakot

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no site (unknown). Cunningham (p. 481): near Sarwanpur. Beal (p. 23) reports Carlleyle's
Sur-kuiâ but says "the bearing does not … correspond". Majumdar (p. 712) reports Vost: in Basti district.

**Survey.** Carlleyle (ASR XII pp. 111–112, 195–196) put the well at Sarkuhiya, 4.5 miles S of Bhuila, on the name and
distance. Smith (1901) leaves it unidentified. Mukherji (1901, pp. 56–57) prefers Sisania, SE of Tilaurakot with
"extensive mounds of ancient ruins", over Piprahwa: "Sisania may, therefore, be the more probable site". Mitra
(IAR 1961–62, p. 72) found the Sisania mound "rich in early pottery" and Kushan coins.

**Later excavations.** Srivastava dug the Birdpur mound, "9 km south of Piprahwa, which corroborates in distance
and direction" Xuanzang's Śarakūpa (IAR 1976–77, p. 49): under a modern liṅga temple, a brick platform and two
copper vessels at about 6 m, one under a brick carved with a lotus, and perhaps an earlier stupa of Kushan–Gupta
date. No spring or well is reported.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** Sarwanpur and Sarkuhiya belong to the abandoned Basti frames. Xuanzang puts the small stupa and
spring "more than 30 li" SE of the city: Birdpur fits the distance from Piprahwa but lies S, not SE, and stands only
if Piprahwa is Kapilavastu (06.02); Sisania is the equivalent lead from Tilaurakot. Birdpur, with a buried stupa
of about the right period, is the better of the two as the evidence now stands, but nothing marks the spring.

### 06.02.11 臘伐尼林 (Lumbinī) — Rummindei, confirmed by excavation

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Rummindei (agreed). Majumdar (p. 711): the pillar "locates the Lumbini grove beyond
doubt". Watters (p. 16) and Dey agree. Cunningham (pp. 477–78, pre-1896): Mokson on the Kohana.

**Survey.** Carlleyle (ASR XII pp. 195–196) put Lumbinī near Shiwpur, 7 miles E of Bhuila, on Faxian's distance.
Mukherji (1901, pp. 37, 57–58) exposed the plinth of the Māyādevī temple about 8 ft down, with the nativity group, and
found the split pillar 45 ft W of it, "as noted by Hiuen Tsiang". Smith (1901, pp. 5–7): the site "is therefore fixed
with absolute certainty".

**Inscriptions.** Bühler (EI 5, pp. 1–5) edited the pillar, dug out 3 ft below ground: "here Buddha Śākyamuni was
born", and the village Lummini was exempted from tax; it "fixes with absolute certainty the situation of the garden
of Lumbini". Hultzsch (CII 1, pp. xxii–xxiii) agrees.

**Later excavations.** Mitra's expedition, based at Lumbinī (IAR 1961–62, p. 65), found antiquities from the 3rd
century B.C. to at least the 9th century A.D., so the site was in use when Xuanzang came.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Rummindei, from Lumbinī-devī (p. 521).

**Assessment.** The inscribed pillar, the temple and the split Xuanzang mentions agree. Mizutani is strengthened.

### 06.02.12 臘伐尼林無憂王石柱・油河 (Lumbinī pillar and Oil River) — the pillar, and the Tilar Nadi for the river

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Rummindei pillar (agreed); no stream named. Cunningham (pp. 477–78): the Oil River =
the Kohana, from *mrakshan* "oil". Beal (p. 25): an unnamed stream.

**Survey.** Carlleyle (ASR XII pp. 195–196) took the Bairahwa and Gadi Nalas near Shiwpur for the Oil River. Smith
(1901, pp. 6–7): "That little river is to this day the Tilar Nadi" (*tel*, oil). Mukherji (1901, p. 58) found it a
furlong and a half E of the mound, and the pillar split "in the middle" as Xuanzang says.

**Inscriptions.** The pillar names the village Lummini (Bühler, EI 5, pp. 1–5; 06.02.11). Hultzsch (CII 1, pp.
xxii–xxiii) accepts the Tilar Nadi for Xuanzang's "river of oil" and notes the split shaft and lost horse capital
(Xuanzang: 上作馬像).

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the Rummindei pillar, recording Aśoka's visit in his twentieth year (p. 525);
his date of discovery, 1897, is a slip for December 1896. The Oil River note is textual only.

**Assessment.** The pillar is settled. The Tilar, by name and position beside the garden, is a good identification of
the Oil River, which Mizutani left unnamed.

### 06.03 藍摩國 (Rāmagrāma) — unsettled; Rampur Deoriya has the only surveyed stupa but belongs to the Bhuila frame

**Identifications.** Mizutani (disputed): Dharmauri (Dharmauli) on the Nepal–Gorakhpur border, or Rampur Deoriya.
Majumdar (p. 713): perhaps Dharmauli, where Hoey found a stupa; Vost: Bhuila-dih. Dey: Rampur Deoriya. Cunningham (pp.
482, 485): Deokali, 28 miles E of Nagar. Watters (p. 25) and Beal (p. 26): undetermined.

**Survey.** Carlleyle moved twice. In ASR XII (pp. 216–221) he chose the great mound of Korau Dih, wavering towards
Wardi Dih. In ASR XVIII (pp. 4–10) he retracted it for Rampur Deoriya near the head of the Marawa Tal (Gorakhpur district): a ruined brick stupa 20–25 ft high between two ancient
tanks, the lake as the Nāga tank, and the name. "In every single particular … Rampur agrees." Cunningham's preface to
ASR XXII accepts Carlleyle's site, calling it "Ramnagar". Smith (1901, pp. 19–20) reckons the distance from Lumbinī at
nearly 40 miles E and expects the capital in Nepal NNE of Nichlaul, "near Dharmauli", whose "name has a Buddhist look";
Bawangarhi in Champaran is "just possible" but too far. Mukherji (1901, p. 59) reports an Aśoka pillar said
to stand N of Nichlaul.

**Gazetteers.** Champaran 1907, copied in 1960, echoes Smith's "just possible" Bawangarhi (forts, tank, well with stone
alligators) but says "no trustworthy information can be obtained". Gorakhpur 1987 puts Rāmagrāma in
Gorakhpur district.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji takes Rampur Deoriya in Basti as the view of B. C. Law "and most scholars", after
reporting Cunningham's Deokali, Adachi's Nichlaul and Smith's Dharmauri (pp. 526–527). He brings no new site evidence.
His textual point is sound: Faxian's 5 yojanas from the birthplace fits the *Record*'s 200-odd li from Lumbinī, while
the Life's "over 500 li" from Kapilavastu (T50:236a, checked) is far out; the Life's figure is not a reason to look
further east.

**Assessment.** Rampur Deoriya is the only candidate with a surveyed stupa and tank, but Carlleyle reached it by
measuring 26 miles from Bhuila Dih, which fell (06.02); from Rummindei it lies to the south-west, not 200 li east.
Dharmauli fits the distance from Lumbinī but rests on a name and a stupa Hoey reported nearby; Bawangarhi is
Smith's own discarded alternative. The place stays disputed.

### 06.03.03 太子迴駕・剃髮窣堵波 (Chandaka's return; Anomā) — unidentified; Carlleyle's Kudawa sites depend on Rampur Deoriya, Mani's Nautanwa on a name

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Anomā bank in Moriya territory; no modern site (uncertain). Cunningham (pp. 489–91):
Anomā = Aumi; return stupa at Chandaoli. Beal (p. 29): Manêya (Carlleyle). Dey reports Aumi and Carlleyle's Kudawa
Nadi. Majumdar (p. 713): about 100 li E of Rāmagrāma.

**Survey.** Carlleyle first proposed Maniya near the Khiduwa Nadi and Chandbhani on the Ami (ASR XII pp. 220–226). From
Rampur Deoriya he then took the Anomā for the Kudawa nadi, "the river of the leap", 1 mile from the ruins at Tameswar
Nath (ASR XVIII pp. 12, 27–30), and placed the three stupas on high ground E of the Sirsarao Tal near Lachmipur: an
earthen conical mound about 10 ft high, a brick mound 300 ft NE once in a grove of jambu trees, and the Maha-than mound.
Cunningham called these "almost certain" (ASR XVIII preface; XXII preface).

**Excavations.** None at these sites. Sahni (AR 1906–07) toured Fleet's Anomā crossing near Majhauli and found the
village Anuaurpari ("across the Anuma"), but no stream of that name and no Buddhist remains: "rather of negative than
positive value".

**Later excavations.** Mani (IAR 1996–97, pp. 137–138), exploring Basti and Siddharthnagar, matched sites to Pāli
places on "size and antiquity of sites, similarities in the ancient and present names and their geographical
locations", among them "the township of Nautanwa with ancient
Nivartana chaitya from where Siddhartha returned Chhandaka and Kanthaka". Nautanwa was not excavated.

**Gazetteers.** Champaran 1907 (Smith's view) places the site of Chandaka's return in Champaran, on the Aśokan pillar
road, without a site.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees with Mizutani: the Anupiya grove on the Anomā in Moriya territory, after Hori,
with no modern site (p. 532).

**Assessment.** Carlleyle found low mounds where his reckoning put them, but the reckoning runs from Bhuila and Rampur
Deoriya, and "Kudawa" carries the argument. Fleet's Majhauli has a village name and nothing else. Mani's Nautanwa
follows the Pāli accounts and a Kapilavastu at Piprahwa, not Xuanzang, who puts the stupa over 100 li E of the
Śrāmaṇera monastery beyond Rāmagrāma, itself 200 li E of Lumbinī; Nautanwa, NE of Siddharthnagar, lies short of
that. It is a new proposal on name and region only. Mizutani's "uncertain" stands.

### 06.03.04 尼拘盧陀林灰炭窣堵波 (Ashes stupa, Pippalivana) — Lauriya-Nandangarh against Carlleyle's Rajdhani

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no site (unknown). Majumdar (p. 713): Lauriya-Nandangarh. Cunningham (p. 492): Sahankat
near Rudrapur. Dey: Barhi (Moriyanagara).

**Survey.** Carlleyle moved Cunningham's Sahankat a few miles NW (ASR XII pp. 227–228), then chose the Rajdhani or
Upadhaolia ruins on the Gorra, 14 miles SSE of Gorakhpur: a solid brick stupa 17–18 ft high and 75 ft across at the SE
corner of the great mound "might possibly be" the Ashes stupa, and the bar (banyan) forest round Rudrapur, Mithabel and
Rajdhani the Nyagrodha forest (ASR XVIII pp. 31–38, 52–54). Cunningham accepted Rajdhani (XXII preface). Smith (1901,
pp. 19–20) proposed the great stupa at Lauriya-Nandangarh instead.

**Gazetteers.** Champaran 1907, copied in 1960, repeats Smith's Nandangarh conjecture, describes the 80-ft mound, and
cites Bloch's view that it is a fortified citadel: "It is impossible … to affirm this identification positively".
Gorakhpur 1987 places Pippalivana in Gorakhpur district without a site.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji names no site; his note is textual (the Vinaya's ash stupa of the Pippala people; p.
535).

**Assessment.** Rajdhani belongs to Carlleyle's chain from Bhuila, and Nandangarh to Smith's Nepal-side scheme. Neither
is tied to the Moriyas by anything found. Later work, not in these sources, excavated a stupa at Nandangarh. Here
Nandangarh is a candidate rather than a result.

### 06.04 拘尸那揭羅國 (Kuśinagara) — Kasia; doubted by Bühler and on the sealings 1905–07, settled by Haribala's copper plate 1910–11

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Kasia, following "the common view", listed as disputed because the remains do not look
like the pilgrims' ruin. Wilson, Cunningham (p. 493), Vivien (p. 359), Beal (p. 32) and Dey: Kasia. Majumdar (p. 714):
the seals "support Cunningham". Watters (p. 44): not Kasia.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR I pp. 76–86) put the city at the Anrudhwa mound and the nirvāṇa vihāra at Matha Kuar.
Carlleyle (ASR XVIII pp. 57–96) excavated the Nirvāṇa statue with a Gupta inscription. Smith (1901, p. 19) still
put it in Nepal, Mukherji (1901, p. 60) at Lauriya.

**Excavations.** The hundreds of sealings of the "convent of the Great Decease" found in 1905–07 were read as
letters sent to Kasia from Kuśinagara (Konow, Marshall, AR 1905–06, pp. 57–59, 76–88), and Vogel took a seal-die
of the "Vishnudvipa" convent to make Kasia "highly probable" Veṭhadīpa (AR 1906–07, pp. 34–35). Hirananda Sastri
then found in the great stupa behind the Nirvāṇa temple a copper plate of Haribala, donor of the statue, deposited
in the "Nirvāṇa-caitya", with coins of Kumāragupta, and more Mahāparinirvāṇa sealings (AR 1910–11, pp. 65–69,
Pargiter p. 75; AR 1911–12, pp. 135–39). Sahni found nothing at Fleet's Pachrukhi (AR 1906–07).

**Inscriptions.** Bühler (EI 5, p. 6), on the Nigliva edict, followed Führer: "If the direction given by the
Chinese,— east of Lumbini,— is correct, Kusinara cannot be identical with Kasia". The objection is conditional,
rests on a bearing and on Führer's reports, and predates the plate. Sahni (EI 18, no. 16), editing a Kalachuri
inscription from the Matha Kuar shrine, which does not name the place, calls Kasia "proved by successive
excavations to represent in all probability the ancient site of Kusanagara". IAR records no later excavation.

**Gazetteers.** Gorakhpur 1909 answers Smith and reports Vogel's work; vol. VI 1881 repeats Cunningham.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Kasia, settled by the copper plate inscribed
*parinirvāṇa-caitya-tāmra-paṭṭa*, with Smith's and Watters' Nepal confluence and Adachi's Eta-anda reported and set
aside (pp. 537–538).

**Assessment.** The plate shows the stupa was the parinirvāṇa caitya by the mid-5th century, which reverses the doubt
of 1905–07; as Pargiter notes, it proves the tradition, not the historical spot. Bühler's objection was overtaken
by it. Mizutani's Kasia is strengthened and the reason for calling it disputed falls away.

### 06.04.03 娑羅林 (the sāla grove, nirvāṇa site) — Matha-kuar-ka-kot at Kasia, the "Parinirvāṇa-caitya" of the copper plate

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the nirvāṇa temple and stupa at Kasia (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 494–96):
Matha-kuar-ka-kot, W of the old channel of the Chota Gandak.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR I pp. 81–82) placed the vihāra at the W end of the Matha Kuar mound, 3–4 li NW of Anrudhwa.
Carlleyle (ASR XVIII pp. 57, 72–86) uncovered the recumbent statue in a vaulted chamber, reckoned the great stupa at
150–177 ft against Xuanzang's 200, and looked for the inscribed pillar without finding it.

**Excavations.** Vogel (AR 1904–05, pp. 33–44) found a Kushana temple under the Nirvāṇa temple, a stupa E of it and
a monastery, but no pillar on Carlleyle's eastern mound. Hirananda Sastri's shaft in the stupa behind the temple
(AR 1910–11, pp. 65–69, 75–77) reached a copper vessel under a plate recording that it was "in the [Pari]nirvāṇa
caitya", given by Haribala, the donor of the statue; an earlier small stupa lay inside. Mauryan remains round the
temple plinth and Mahāparinirvāṇa sealings followed (AR 1911–12, pp. 135–39). The 1927 restoration put a copy of the
evidence in the new relic chamber (AR 1927–28, p. 13).

**Inscriptions.** Sahni (EI 18, no. 16) edits a Kalachuri stone inscription that Carlleyle found by the door of the
Matha Kuar shrine, a separate brick shrine of a Buddha image; it does not name the site but shows the complex in
use in the medieval period.

**Gazetteers.** Vol. VI 1881 repeats Cunningham's distance from Anrudhwa. Gorakhpur 1909 reports the temple and the
20-ft image with Haribala's inscription.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the 6 m nirvāṇa image of the 5th century at Kasia, excavated in 1876 (p.
542).

**Assessment.** The statue and the plate together name the stupa and image as the nirvāṇa monument of the 5th
century. Mizutani is strengthened.

### 06.04.03a 阿恃多伐底河 (Ajitavatī / Hiraṇyavatī) — at Kasia, the old channel of the Little Gandak, locally "Hirana"

**Identifications.** Mizutani (disputed): Xuanzang merges three names; Mizutani takes the Little Rāpti as the
Aciravatī and the Gandak as the Hiraṇyavatī. Watters (p. 29): one river; Smith (via Watters): two streams. Beal (p.
32): the Little Gandak or a feeder. Dey: the old channel of the Chhota Gandak at Kasia. Cunningham (p. 495): the old
channel "or Hiranyavati" between Matha Kuar and Devisthan.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR I pp. 76, 81–82) traced old channels of the Little Gandak between the ruins and recorded
the local name Hirana for the stream W of Kasia.

**Gazetteers.** Vol. VI 1881 echoes this: the river "is now called the Little Gandak; but … a man of Padrauna styled it
Hirana". It adds that Tarai rivers shift their beds.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees with the reading here, against Mizutani: the Ajitavatī and Hiraṇyavatī are the
Little Gandak, and the Aciravatī is a different river, the Rāpti of Oudh (p. 541). He gives no site evidence beyond
the survey.

**Assessment.** If Kuśinagara is Kasia (06.04), the river by the grove is the Little Gandak or its old bed, and the
local name Hirana, recorded by Cunningham on the spot, is modest but direct evidence. Mizutani's main Gandak fits
Smith's Nepal site better than his own Kasia.

### 06.04.11 如來焚身窣堵波 (cremation stupa) — the Ramabhar stupa, Mauryan but without relics or a name

**Identifications.** Mizutani (disputed): the present cremation site is a mound "about ten miles" E of the nirvāṇa site;
he notes that Xuanzang (N of the city) and the sūtras (E gate, W gate) disagree. Cunningham (pp. 494–95): the great
stupa Devisthan, NE of Anrudhwa.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR I pp. 84–86) was "strongly inclined to identify the Ramabhar Tila" with the cremation
stupa. Garrick (ASR XVI p. 125) found it already dug. Carlleyle (ASR XVIII pp. 88–92) judged Ramabhar Aśokan but put
the pyre at a low mound NE of Anrudhwa, near the Mallas' palace, and Ramabhar perhaps at the relic division.

**Excavations.** Vogel found two sealings (c. 400 and c. 600) of the Makuṭabandhana convent, "the place where
Buddha's remains were cremated", among the Kasia seals (AR 1905–06, pp. 76–88). Hirananda Sastri (AR 1910–11,
p. 69) cleared the Ramabhar stupa, "nearly a mile south-west of Kasia" on the Ramabhar Tal: circular, of heavy
Mauryan bricks, a drum of 112 ft, an old shaft re-sunk to water level, no relics. Structures S of it were left
unexplained (AR 1911–12, pp. 135–39), which "may throw light" on the Makuṭabandhana identification.

**Gazetteers.** Vol. VI 1881 repeats Cunningham's Ramabhar and notes stupa remains on the mound.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji names no site. He adds that the *Dīrghāgama* (T1, juan 4) agrees with Xuanzang in
putting the cremation north of the city, while the Mahāsāṅghika Vinaya calls the place the Makuṭabandhana caitya,
across the Golden river (pp. 550–551); the Makuṭabandhana name is the one on the Kasia sealings.

**Assessment.** Ramabhar is now shown to be a large stupa of Mauryan date close to the nirvāṇa site, and the
Makuṭabandhana convent is attested in sealings at Kasia; nothing found at Ramabhar names it. It stays the main
candidate. Mizutani's "about ten miles" fits no excavated site and may be a slip.

### 06.04.14 大邑聚 (the large town of the Brahmin patron) — Khukhundo or Kahaon against Carlleyle's Rudrapur

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no site (unknown). Cunningham (1871, p. 497): Kahaon, preferred to Khukhundo on
distance. Majumdar (p. 714) reports Carlleyle's Rudrapur. Vivien (p. 360): perhaps Radjapore.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR I pp. 84–86) first chose Khukhundo, 27 miles from Kasia on the old road to the Ghāgra
crossing, with ruins "covering nearly a square mile" and temple mounds; Garrick (ASR XVI p. 127) followed, recording
Brahmanical sculptures and many small brick mounds. Carlleyle (ASR XVIII pp. 40–47) argued that Khukhundo and Kahaon
lie 11 and 14 miles too far E of the straight line to Benares, and chose Rudrapur, with about 25 mounds, "nearly all
Brahmanical" liṅga temples, or ruins near Baraon 6 miles S.

**Gazetteers.** Vol. VI 1881 echoes ASR I: the Kasia–Benares road crossed at the Mail ferry, which excludes Rudrapur,
and "the town must have been Khakhundu".

**Assessment.** All three are Brahmanical towns of some size, which suits Xuanzang's Brahmin patron. The choice turns
on the route: the old ferry favours Khukhundo, a straight line favours Rudrapur. Khukhundo, chosen by Cunningham's field
survey and Garrick, is marginally the better candidate. The town remains unidentified.

### 07.01.02a 鹿野伽藍 (Deer Park) — Sarnath, named Saddharmacakra on its own seals

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sarnath; the 200-ft vihāra is the Mūlagandhakuṭī (agreed). Vivien (p. 360), Cunningham
(pp. 501–2), Beal (p. 46) and Dey: Sarnath. Watters (p. 48) gives no modern name.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR I pp. 115–118) confirms Sarnath, citing statues of the Buddha "turning the wheel", and puts
the monastery at the Barahipur mound, half a mile NNE of Chaukhandi.

**Excavations.** Oertel's season of 1904–05 found a 5th–6th-century monastic seal with wheel and deer, "Śrī
sad-dharmma[cakre] bhikṣusaṅghasya" (AR 1904–05, pp. 90–91). In 1906–07 seals reading "Śrī-saddharmacakre
mūlagandhakuṭyām bhagavataḥ" gave "the name of the old Sarnath monastery" and of its chief shrine; Konow placed the
Mūlagandhakuṭī E of the excavated Main Shrine, where lamp-posts inscribed for it were found (AR 1906–07, pp. 98–99).
Marshall dated the Main Shrine late, with 9th-century inscriptions in its foundations (AR 1904–05, pp. 33–44). A
Sammitīya inscription on the Aśoka pillar matches Xuanzang's 1,500 monks of that school (AR 1907–08, pp. 74–76), and
10th–12th-century inscriptions name the Dharmacakra(jina) vihāra (pp. 74–76, 80–83).

**Inscriptions.** Vogel (EI 8, pp. 166–172) edits the Sarnath inscriptions, including the 4th-century record on
the Aśoka pillar to the masters of the Sammitīya (?) school; Hultzsch (CII 1, pp. xxi–xxii) places Sarnath N of
Benares and the edict pillar in the Deer Park.

**Later excavations.** Sinha's trial trenches behind the apsidal structure and towards the Aśoka pillar (IAR
1992–93, pp. 98–99) dated the apsidal temple to the late Mauryan period, over deposits of the 4th–3rd century B.C.

**Gazetteers.** Benares 1911 reports the Dharmacakrapravartana inscription and dates the Main Shrine after the 7th
century; Varanasi 1965 identifies it with Xuanzang's 200-ft vihāra.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Sarnath, about 4 miles N of Varanasi (p. 562).

**Assessment.** The seals name the monastery and its Mūlagandhakuṭī, so the site and Mizutani's name for the vihāra
are confirmed. Whether the Main Shrine is that vihāra is doubtful on the excavators' dating and Konow's placing.

### 07.01.03 初轉法輪石窣堵波 (stone stupa and pillar of the first sermon) — Dharmarājikā and the Aśoka pillar, with reservations

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Dharmarājika (Jagat Singh) stupa, with the broken Aśoka pillar W of the vihāra
(agreed). Cunningham (pp. 501–2): the Dhamek. Dey: the Aśoka pillar marks the spot.

**Excavations.** Oertel dug the pillar to its foot, 17 ft below the terrace: polished, with the lion capital and
wheel, about 37 ft above ground in all (AR 1904–05, pp. 60–69), against Xuanzang's 70 ft, "his estimates of height
cannot be relied upon"; Marshall put the column at about 50 ft and noted that the Jagat Singh stupa is brick, not
stone (pp. 33–44). Konow took the Dharmarājikā of the Mahīpāla inscription for Aśoka's stupa (AR 1906–07, pp.
98–99), and Marshall's section through the Jagat Singh stupa found an original of large wedge-shaped bricks "back …
to the time of Aśoka", with enlargements down to the 12th century (AR 1907–08, pp. 64–65).

**Inscriptions.** Hultzsch (CII 1, pp. xxi–xxii) takes Oertel's pillar for Xuanzang's 70-ft pillar before
Aśoka's stupa, noting its 37 ft; Vogel (EI 8, p. 166), with the lower shaft in situ, identifies it with the jade-bright
pillar where the Buddha first turned the Wheel.

**Gazetteers.** Benares 1911: the pillar "could not have been more than 60'", but "most people will regard it as more
than probable" that it is Xuanzang's column; the brick Jagat Singh stupa against his stone one calls for "hesitating";
Dhamek is "too far" from the column; "practically nothing definite can yet be predicated".

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the Dharmarājika stupa (laid open in 1794) and the Aśoka pillar broken in
five pieces, its lion capital in the Sarnath museum (p. 563).

**Assessment.** The excavations give the Jagat Singh stupa an Aśokan core and the name Dharmarājikā, and the pillar
beside it carries a Sammitīya inscription (07.01.02a). The height and the "stone" remain unexplained, but the
gazetteer's doubt is answered on date and name. Mizutani is moderately strengthened.

### 07.01.04 慈氏菩薩受記窣堵波 (Maitreya's prediction stupa) — Dhamekh, now with the name "Dharmekṣa"

**Identifications.** Mizutani and Dey: the Dhamekh (agreed).

**Excavations.** Oertel (AR 1904–05, pp. 60–69, 74–77) argued that Dhamekh is the Bodhisattva stupa: Jinaprabhasūri's
Tīrthakalpa (1612) calls the place Dharmekṣa, with a Bodhisattva sanctuary, and the main niche faces W. Konow
(AR 1906–07, pp. 98–99) found that "Dhamekh therefore regularly corresponds to an older dharmeksha". Marshall
(AR 1904–05, pp. 37–38) answered that Cunningham had used the same niche argument for a wheel-turning Buddha, a
theory with "as much to recommend it as the Maitreya one". Cunningham's shaft of 1835 had found no relics.

**Gazetteers.** Benares 1911 lists the Dhamekh among the monuments that "afford a chance of identification" and rejects
only Cunningham's equation of it with Aśoka's stupa (07.01.03).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the Dhamekh, the largest monument at Sarnath (p. 566).

**Assessment.** The excavators give the name a Buddhist etymology and a 17th-century Bodhisattva tradition; the
Maitreya attribution itself still rests on the niche argument, which Marshall doubts. Mizutani is mildly
strengthened.

### 07.01.06 三清池 (the three ponds) — the Sarnath tanks

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Naya, Sarang and Candra ponds (hedged, so uncertain). Dey (after Cunningham):
Chandra-tal, Saranga-tal and Kaya-tal.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR I pp. 120–121) adds the stone on which the Buddha dried his robe, beside the ponds in
Xuanzang: a squared block hollowed underneath, found at Sarnath and since lost, which he thought "highly probable" to be
it.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** The two lists of tanks agree except for Naya against Kaya, probably a reading or OCR difference.
Mizutani's identification stands.

### 07.01.10 五人迎佛窣堵波 (the five companions' stupa) — at Chaukhandi

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Chaukhandi mound with Akbar's tower (agreed). Dey: Chaukhandi.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR I pp. 117–118) identifies Chaukhandi with Xuanzang's 300-ft stupa 2–3 li SW of the Deer
Park monastery (the Barahipur mound, half a mile NNE). He sank a shaft 27 ft into the centre and found no relic chamber,
which suits a commemorative stupa; Kittoe had exposed buttress walls on its E and W sides.

**Excavations.** Oertel (AR 1904–05, pp. 74–77) cleared the north side: three square terraces each about 12 ft high
and broad on a concrete floor, with an octagonal plinth above. Marshall (pp. 33–44) found the identification "far more
convincing" than the others: the stupa lies "some 800 yards south-west" of the monastery and "there is no other mound
in this direction". The niched terraces "tended rather to disprove" it, since Xuanzang saw no niches, but the
monument may have been rebuilt since.

**Later excavations.** Muhammed K. K. (IAR 1997–98, p. 199; 1998–99, pp. 217–218) cut the SE corner: terrace walls
1.30 m thick with cross walls forming earth-filled boxes, a terraced brick structure of the Gupta period, and on
the E a 2-m brick path to the centre, at least one gateway, with traces of a circumambulatory path.

**Gazetteers.** Benares 1911: "The one structure about which there seems least uncertainty is the Chaukhandi stupa".
Benares, Mirzapur, Jaunpur 1884 echoes Cunningham's measurements.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the Chaukhandi mound with Akbar's tower of 1588 (p. 576).

**Assessment.** Position, distance and the absence of any rival mound support Chaukhandi for the great stupa, and
the later work dates the terraced structure to the Gupta period, before Xuanzang; the niches are a reservation, not
a counter-site. Mizutani's small stupa stood "beside" it, so Mizutani is strengthened.

### 07.02 戰主國 (Garjapatipura / Yuddhapati) — the country is Ghazipur; Sayyidpur is a serious rival for the capital

**Identifications.** Mizutani: capital at Ghazipur (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 502–3), Vivien (p. 362) and Beal (p. 61):
Ghazipur. Majumdar (p. 715): Ghazipur, noting that Watters objected and Smith agreed. Watters (p. 59) disputes
Cunningham's name argument ("Yuddhapati", an epithet of Śiva) and gives no site.

**Survey.** The survey does not take up the capital. Cunningham (ASR III pp. 64–66) visited Buxar, the Mahāsāla of
Oldham's western scheme, and found "a purely Brahmanical site" with "no remains of antiquity" (07.02.03).

**Gazetteers.** Azamgarh, Ghazipur, Ballia 1883: Oldham "identifies the country round Sayyidpur as having been the site
of a very ancient city, the capital", citing carvings, caityas reused as Muslim tombs, mounds and the Masaun fort.
Ghazipur 1909 reports that Cunningham's Ghazipur "was contested by Dr. Hoey but accepted … by Dr. Fleet", and that
Saidpur, with "numerous remains of Buddhist and early Hindu times", "has been suggested", but it is "not yet possible to
identify it definitely". No gazetteer cites remains at Ghazipur town.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees with Cunningham's Ghazipur (p. 581); he does not discuss Sayyidpur.

**Assessment.** The extent of the country is not in doubt. For the capital, the only physical evidence here is at
Sayyidpur, while Ghazipur rests on Cunningham's distance and name arguments. Oldham's scheme loses its Mahāsāla at Buxar
to the inscribed Masar, which weakens his reckoning, but not the remains at Sayyidpur (see also "New", Aonrihar).
Sayyidpur remains an equally supported alternative for the capital.

### 07.02.02 阿避陀羯剌拏僧伽藍 (Aviddhakarṇa monastery) — near Ballia; Carlleyle replaces Bikapur with mounds at Dharmaranya

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Bikapur, 1 mile E of Ballia (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 503–4) proposes it on the name,
equating it with Faxian's "Vast Solitude"; Beal (p. 62) follows. Vivien (p. 363): near the Sarju–Ganges confluence.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR III pp. 67–68) moved Faxian's "Vast Solitude" to near Bhojpur, which loosens his own link.
Carlleyle (ASR XXII pp. 81–87) reports Bikapura washed away by the river and puts the monastery at the Dharmaranya
tank, about 450 ft N of Ballia, and the names Dharmaranya and Dhamaoli, with measured mounds at Wazirapur, Dhamaoli
and Muhammadpur (the last 24 ft high, perhaps a stupa): "I think there cannot be any doubt". His "slit-ear" etymology
is weak.

**Gazetteers.** Azamgarh, Ghazipur, Ballia 1883 and Ghazipur 1909 report Oldham's Ghauspur/Kathot, 7 miles E of
Ghazipur, with stone, bricks, sculptures and a healing-tank legend. Ballia 1907 calls the Ballia proposal "merely
speculative".

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees with Mizutani: Bikapur near Ballia, after Cunningham (p. 583); he does not
know Carlleyle's mounds.

**Assessment.** The survey keeps the monastery at Ballia, on mounds and names rather than Cunningham's place-name.
Ghauspur fits only Oldham's western capital (07.02). Mizutani's locality stands at low confidence; Bikapur itself is
gone.

### 07.02.03 摩訶娑羅邑 (Mahāsāla) — Masar, confirmed by inscriptions

**Identifications.** Mizutani, Vivien (p. 363), Cunningham (p. 504), Beal (p. 63), Watters (p. 61), Majumdar (p. 716)
and Dey: Masar, 6 miles W of Arrah (agreed).

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR III pp. 64–72) visited both Buxar and Masar and rejected Oldham's Buxar. At Masar "no less
than seven inscriptions, nearly 500 years old" in the Jain temple of Pārśvanātha give the name Mahāsāra, one naming
"Raja Natha Deva, of Mahasara". He traced the old town at about a mile by half a mile, found no Buddhist trace, and
showed that Masar "stands upon the high bank of the old bed of the Ganges".

**Gazetteers.** Gaya, Shahabad 1875–77, Shahabad 1906 and Shahabad 1966 echo ASR III: the seven inscriptions and the
old high bank past Bihiya and Arrah. Shahabad 1906 notes "numerous images of Brahmanical gods", which fits Xuanzang's
town of Brahmins. Ghazipur 1909 alone floats Chaunsa ("possibly").

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Masar, 6 miles W of Arrah, on Cunningham's inscription (p. 584).

**Assessment.** The inscriptions name the place and the old bank answers Xuanzang's "near the Ganges". Mizutani is
strengthened.

### 07.02.03a 那羅延天祠 (Nārāyaṇa temple) — opposite Masar, not at Revelganj; Narainpur the survey's site

**Identifications.** Mizutani: probably Revelganj (uncertain). Cunningham (pp. 504–5) and Beal (p. 64): above
Revelganj, 16 miles N of Masar. Majumdar (p. 716) reports Carlleyle's Narayan, at the usual ferry N from Masar.

**Survey.** Cunningham himself gave up Revelganj in ASR III (pp. 72–73): the temple "stood on the opposite bank of the
Ganges within sight of Mahasara". Carlleyle (ASR XXII pp. 75–79) followed the old roads N from Masar to the ferry and
proposed Narainpur near Gangapur, where an old village of the name "with its temple" had been washed into the river.

**Gazetteers.** Saran 1905 repeats Revelganj, and echoes Carlleyle on the Narayanpur and Ghāgra island since washed
away, calling these attempts "at least speculative". Azamgarh, Ghazipur, Ballia 1883 and Ballia 1907 report Oldham's
different Narayanpur, "opposite to Buxar", which goes with his abandoned Buxar.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees with Mizutani: Revelganj, after Cunningham's first view (p. 585).

**Assessment.** Mizutani follows the 1871 view that Cunningham himself revised. A site opposite Masar fits the
inscriptionally fixed Mahāsāla, and Narainpur supplies a name and a reported lost temple, nothing more. It is a weak
alternative to Revelganj.

### 07.02.03b 伏鬼窣堵波 (the Āṭavaka stupa) — unidentified; Cunningham abandoned Chāpra for Arrah

**Identifications.** Mizutani: perhaps near Chāpra, with "no traces" (uncertain). Cunningham (1871, p. 505): Chāpra.
Beal (p. 64): Saran district. Majumdar (p. 716) reports Carlleyle's Godena.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR III pp. 71–74) moved the stupa to Arrah, S of the Ganges, on the legend of the man-eating
Asura Baka killed by Bhīma. Beglar (ASR VIII p. 17) took Arrah as the base for his search for the Droṇa stupa
(07.02.04). Carlleyle (ASR XXII pp. 77–78) put it 30 li E of Narainpur, on an island at the Ghāgra mouth since washed
away, or at the brick mound at Godena near Revelganj.

**Gazetteers.** Saran 1905 and Shahabad 1906/1966 echo the survey's Godena and Arrah, noting that "there are no ancient
remains" at Arrah. Azamgarh 1883 and Ballia 1907 report Oldham's Bharauli/Ballia sites.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji gives the name only (Āṭavaka; p. 586).

**Assessment.** The survey abandoned Mizutani's Chāpra. Arrah rests on a legend on the wrong bank; Godena, on the north
bank near Revelganj, has a brick mound and fits a temple opposite Masar. Nothing is identified, and Mizutani's
Chāpra is weakened.

### 07.02.04 瓶窣堵波 (the Droṇa / vessel stupa) — unidentified; Beglar's Bhagwanganj stupa or Chirand

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no site, after "various views" (unknown). Cunningham (p. 507) and Beal (p. 65):
Deghwara, on the name. Majumdar (pp. 716–17) reports Carlleyle's Chirand.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR III p. 77) read Xuanzang's SE as NE and suggested the Bitha mound, 16 miles E of Arrah.
Beglar (ASR VIII pp. 17–20) followed the bearing as written, 100 li SE from Arrah, and found "a small stupa at
Bhagwanganj" near Bhartpura on the Punpun: 35–40 ft across, about 20 ft high, with a square socket-hole at the top and
temple mounds around. He argued that the 140–150 li NE to Vaiśālī measures from the Ganges. Carlleyle (ASR XXII pp.
78–79) examined Deghwara and found no mound, and preferred "the mounds of ancient ruins at Cheran" (Chirand).

**Gazetteers.** Saran 1905 repeats Carlleyle's Chirand phrase, attributing it to Cunningham, and calls it
"speculative".

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: it "cannot yet be located" (p. 586).

**Assessment.** Deghwara is eliminated. Bhagwanganj is the only stupa found on Xuanzang's bearing, but it follows from
Arrah as the Āṭavaka stupa (07.02.03b); Chirand goes with the north-bank route. Bhagwanganj is a candidate no standard
authority records, at low confidence.

### 07.03 吠舍釐國 (Vaiśālī) — Basarh; Bloch's survey, plan and Vaiśālī seals

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Basarh, Raja-Bisal-ka-garh (agreed). Vivien (p. 364), Cunningham (pp. 507–8), Beal (p.
66), Majumdar (p. 717) and Dey: Basarh. Watters (p. 77): "we need not accept the
identification". Dey reports Hoey's Chirand.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR I pp. 55–57) measured Raja Bisal ka garh at 4,660 ft round against the 4–5 li of the palace
city.

**Excavations.** Bloch (AR 1903–04, pp. 81–110; EC 1903–04, pp. 14–15): the fort, which "still preserves the name
of Visāla", is "something less than 5,000 feet" round, as the 4–5 li require; the Kolhua
pillar, stupa and Rām-kund tank lie about 2 miles NW, as in Xuanzang (07.03.04). "It is this group which I think proves
conclusively that Vaisali is identical with Basarh." Trench D, where local opinion put the palace, gave hundreds of
4th–5th-century clay seals, one reading "[V]aiśāly-adhiṣṭhān-ādhikaraṇa", the government of Vaiśālī, and others
Tīrabhukti (pp. 91, 103–10). Spooner's season (1912) added over 1,200 inscribed seals
(Page, AR 1922–23, pp. 30–31).

**Inscriptions.** CII 3 (pp. 67, 70, 101, 260) edits the Basarh seals of Dhruvasvāminī, Govindagupta and
Ghaṭotkacagupta, and reads the official seals as making Vaiśālī the Yuvarāja's seat and head of a *viṣaya* in
Tīrabhukti.

**Later excavations.** Altekar and Sita Ram Roy (IAR 1958–59, p. 12; 1959–60, pp. 14–16) cut the fort's defences:
a brick wall on NBP deposit, an earthen rampart dated by a sealing of Agnimitra (2nd century B.C.), and a late
Kushan–early Gupta brick rampart. Outside it Altekar found the relic stupa by following Xuanzang (07.03.03; IAR
1957–58, pp. 10–11), Roy a Gupta shrine at Baniya (07.03.02), and the Kolhua group was cleared (07.03.04).

**Gazetteers.** Muzaffarpur 1907 reports Bloch's excavation; Darbhanga 1907 the seals; Saran 1905 rejects Hoey's
Chirand.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Basarh, after Cunningham, defended by Smith against Hoey (JRAS 1902) and
confirmed by Bloch's Raja Visal ka garh, where a room held some seven hundred sealings, some reading "of the
householder … of Vaiśālī", and the measured circuit of 4,600 ft answers Xuanzang's 4–5 li palace city (p. 588).

**Assessment.** A fort of the right circuit bearing the name, the Aśoka group in the right relation, and a seal of the
city's own administration found in the fort leave no room for Watters' doubt; a seal can travel, but not with the
rest. The later excavations add a relic stupa found where Xuanzang puts it. Mizutani is strengthened.

### 07.03.02 宮城西北伽藍 (the Sammitīya monastery NW of the palace city) — Bloch's spot E of Baniya, emptied by brick-robbing

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no site (unknown). No authority names one.

**Excavations.** Bloch (AR 1903–04, pp. 83–84; EC 1903–04, pp. 14–15) puts the Sammitīya monastery and its three
stupas "one mile (5 to 6 li) north-west from the palace", in the direction of the Kolhua group. Smith's site near
Pharawal rested on a mound Mukherji reported in 1897; Bloch could not find it, and Ufraul is too far. All that remains
is a silted tank E of Baniya, "which may have belonged to the monastery", with no bricks in the fields: "all traces of
these ruins have been removed long ago".

**Later excavations.** Altekar's relic stupa (07.03.03; IAR 1957–58, pp. 10–11) lies about half a mile NW of the
fort, NE of the Kharauna pokhar. In Xuanzang's sequence it stands SE of the Śāriputra stupa, which is E of this
monastery, so the monastery lay a little beyond it to the NW, towards Bloch's spot. Roy (IAR 1960–61, p. 6; 1961–62,
p. 7) found a Gupta brick shrine at Baniya over NBP-period levels.

**Gazetteers.** None that are specific. Muzaffarpur 1907 speaks only of "so many" stupas at Basarh.

**Assessment.** Bloch fixes the area on Xuanzang's bearing and distance from a securely identified palace, and the
relic stupa found since anchors the sequence just short of it. The monastery itself has not been found. It is a
located area rather than a site.

### 07.03.07 菴沒羅女園 (Āmrapālī's garden) — unidentified

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no site (unknown). No authority names one.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR I p. 57) calls the Mir Abdal brick mound, SW of the fort, "one of the stupas … of which so
many are described by Hwen Thsang", without naming which. Xuanzang's sequence does not put the garden there.

**Excavations.** Bloch (AR 1903–04, pp. 85–86) calls the same stupa, now the dargah of Mīrānjī, "not referred to by the

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji names no site, but notes that Faxian puts the garden 3 li S of the city, W of the

**Assessment.** The one candidate mound has been set aside by both surveyors. It remains unidentified. Xuanzang's text
puts it NW of the palace city, by the stupa of the last look (Ji's reading, against Bloch's "south"), so Faxian's
southern garden is a different tradition and the search area is the NW quarter.

### 07.03.12 栗呫婆子別如來處 (where the Licchavis parted from the Buddha) — unidentified

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no site (unknown); he notes Faxian's inscribed pillar. No authority names one.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** It remains unidentified. Faxian's inscribed pillar would be the thing to look for.

### 07.03.13 吠舍釐城西北故城 (the old city NW of Vaiśālī, Mahādeva jātaka) — Kesariya, confirmed by local tradition and a pre-7th-century stupa

**Identifications.** Mizutani and Cunningham (p. 511): Kesariya, about 30 miles NNW of Vaiśālī (agreed). The coded Dey
entry ("Hoey … town of the Monster Fish") belongs to another place.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR I pp. 65–66; XVI pp. 16–18) describes a stupa mound 62 ft high and 1,400 ft round, the
upper stupa built on a ruined older one, a practice Xuanzang records at Vaiśālī. Locally it is the
Deora of Raja Ben Chakravartti, and a gallery dug into it some forty years earlier found nothing. Its distance is "the
exact position" for Xuanzang's town "deserted for many ages".

**Excavations.** None before 1997. Page (AR 1922–23, pp. 30–31), on its conservation, describes a brick stupa on a
platform assigned to the 1st century A.D. and repeats the identification with the stupa Xuanzang describes.

**Later excavations.** Muhammed K. K. dug four seasons (IAR 1997–98, pp. 13–14; 1998–99, pp. 2–3; 1999–2000, pp.
9–15; 2000–01, p. 8): a terraced circular brick stupa over 100 m across at the base, with five terraces, cells
holding large stucco Buddhas, and a drum 9.38 m high. First dated to the 7th century, it was then given a
Śuṅga–Kushan core and late-Gupta terraces, so it stood in Xuanzang's day. No inscription was found.

**Gazetteers.** Tirhut, Champaran 1875–77 and Champaran 1907 (copied in 1960) echo Cunningham's Raja Ben tradition:
"It can hardly be doubted that the tradition of Raja Ben preserves the story recorded by Hiuen Tsiang".

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Kesariya, 30 miles N–NW of Vaiśālī, after Cunningham (p. 601).

**Assessment.** The local name keeps Xuanzang's cakravartin attribution, the distance fits, and the excavation shows
a great stupa of Śuṅga–Kushan origin enlarged in the late Gupta period, before his visit. Nothing names it.
Mizutani is strengthened.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** The dispute is between the sources, and the survey found nothing on Xuanzang's bearing.

### 07.03.15 濕吠多補羅僧伽藍 (Śvetapura) — north of the Ganges, near Hajipur; no site

### 07.03.16 阿難陀分身窣堵波 (Ānanda's half-body stupa) — at the confluence; the Sonepur region is suggested

**Identifications.** Mizutani: quotes Faxian's "confluence of five rivers" one yojana N of Pāṭaliputra (uncertain). No authority names a site.

**Assessment.** The Sonepur area at the Gandak–Ganges confluence is a new, tentative candidate with some early remains. It suits the
geography but is not tied to the stupa.

### 07.04 弗栗恃國 (Vṛji) — northern Tirhut and Darbhanga

**Identifications.** Mizutani: northern Darbhanga, near Nepal (agreed). Majumdar (p. 718) and Smith (via Watters): N Darbhanga and the adjacent Tarai.

**Gazetteers.** Darbhanga 1907 and Bengal 1902: "Tirhut comprised the territory of Vaisali to the south and the Vrijjian kingdom to

**Inscriptions.** Bhandarkar (EI 21) compares the Saṃvaṅgiyas of a Bengal plate with the Saṃvajji, Xuanzang's

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the strip between the Gandak and the Bagmati, roughly northern Darbhanga (pp.
608–609). He notes that the Life omits Vṛji (it omits Nepal too, checked), so that Xuanzang may not have gone there,
and that Wang Xuance did.

**Assessment.** The gazetteers agree with Mizutani's northern Tirhut/Darbhanga and add no evidence. Mizutani stands.

### 07.04.01b 大河 (the great river) — perhaps the Kamla

  cells on top, artificially raised like the Lauriya mounds; he does not discuss the Pūrvārāma. Fonia and Hasan dug
  the Orajhar "to trace the Purvarama" (IAR 1999–2000, pp. 171–174): Kushan monastic cells, then a Gupta temple
  plinth with pilasters like the Sahet temple, then later platforms. A Kushan–Gupta Buddhist site, but nothing names
  it.
- **Aśoka stupa monastery NW of the 戰主國 capital (≈07.02.01).** Azamgarh, Ghazipur, Ballia 1883 (hits 851/1070): Oldham's
  ruins at Aonrihar, 2 miles from Sayyidpur. Bricks, masonry wells and walls run towards the Masaun mound, and 29 sculptures were removed to
  Ghazipur. This goes with Oldham's Sayyidpur capital (07.02).
- **Monkey tank (獼猴池), Vaiśālī.** Muzaffarpur 1907 (hits 67/295) echoes Cunningham (ASR I pp. 62–63; XVI pp. 13–15): the
  brick-faced tank, 240 × 140 ft, S of the Kolhua pillar, now called Ram-kund, "evidently is the monkey tank". Well supported by its
  position next to the pillar and stupa (07.03.04). Bloch (AR 1903–04, p. 84; EC 1903–04, pp. 14–15) confirms the
  Rām-kund as the Markaṭahrada, measured at 200 × 100 ft: "As to their location no possible doubt exists." The
  Survey exposed it (IAR 1991–92, pp. 4–5; L. C. Singh, 1992–93, pp. 5–6): a Kushana brick-lined tank about 32.5 ×
  31.5 m in seven tiers, with two bathing ghats, S of the pillar, as Xuanzang puts the pond.
- **Monkey's offering of honey / bhikṣus' grove, Vaiśālī.** Saran 1905 and Saran 1960 (hits 334, 1591), after Hoey (JASB 1900):
  Bhikaban ("clearly the 'Bhikshu's Grove'") and two villages called Kapia (*kapi*, monkey) near Saran Khas. This depends on Hoey's
  placing of Vaiśālī in Saran, which 07.03 contradicts. A curiosity, not a candidate.
- **The 300-ft stupa 2–3 li SW of the Sarnath saṅghārāma** (coded under 07.01.10). Benares 1911 and Benares, Mirzapur, Jaunpur
  1884, after Cunningham (ASR I pp. 117–118): Chaukhandi.

From the Archaeological Survey and Mukherji 1901 (rows coded `new`, and sub-sites coded under their parent):

- **Śrāvastī, the three "gulfs" (Devadatta, Kokālika, Ciñcā), the stupa of Maudgalyāyana and Śāriputra's girdle, Virūḍhaka's
  tank.** Cunningham, ASR I pp. 342–347: three tanks by the Jetavana mound, the shrine of Pir Barana 700 ft NW of it, and a tank S
  of the Pūrvārāma.
- **Massacre of the Śākyas (釋種誅死處), Kapilavastu.** Carlleyle, ASR XII pp. 111–112, 172–173: Bhata (Kosahra), 1.5 miles NW of
  Bhuila Dih (Bhuila frame). Smith 1901 pp. 10–17 and Mukherji 1901 pp. 28–29, 54–56: Führer's 17 small stupas at Sagarwa, though
  Mukherji finds "Dr. Führer's identifications … full of contradictions".
- **Īśvara temple, Kapilavastu.** Smith 1901 and Mukherji 1901 pp. 29–30, 54–56: the Tauleshvara liṅga temple at Tauliva; Führer's
  temple at Bikuli rejected.
- **Royal precincts at Tilaurakot.** Mukherji 1901 pp. 20–21, 49–54: Śuddhodana's palace and the conception vihāra in the NW of the
  fort; the Asita stupa at a sixteen-sided brick structure NE of it; Yaśodharā's quarters and the school-house by the central tank;
  the saṅghārāma before the E gate; deva temples at Chitra-dei; the elephant stupa at a mound 500 ft S of the S gate, with the
  Hastigarta ditch beside it; Kanthaka's return stupa about 650 ft SE of the E gate; the kāṣāya and conversion stupas at Lori-Kudan;
  the tournament stupa at Derwa. All partly excavated or unexcavated; proposals only. The rival set is Srivastava's
  at Ganwaria, by Piprahwa (06.02): the larger brick complex as Śuddhodana's residence (IAR 1974–75, p. 41), a block
  of rooms and courtyards as the prince's school (1975–76, p. 49), and shrines built over houses, tested against
  Xuanzang's vihāras on the palace sites (1976–77, p. 51). Also proposals by analogy, with no inscription.
- **Śakra's and the four Devarājas' stupas, Lumbinī; Koli/Devadaha.** Mukherji 1901 pp. 57–58: among five small stupas excavated at
  Rummindei; Koli at the Koliha rivulet near Baghia. Smith (1901, p. 18) puts Koli on the Baghela, 17–18 miles E of Rummindei.
- **Kuśinagara: Subhadra's stupa, the deer stupa, the relic-division stupa.** Carlleyle, ASR XVIII pp. 88–91: Subhadra's at the
  Nat-ka-Than mound in the Khanua Nala, about 2,000 ft W of Anrudhwa; the deer stupa at a small grassy mound near it; the relic
  division perhaps at Ramabhar (see 06.04.11). He found no other large stupa near Kasia.
- **The monkey's honey-offering stupa (獼猴奉蜜窣堵波), Vaiśālī.** Cunningham, ASR I p. 62, took two low mounds W and S of the
  monkey tank; excavating W of the tank (ASR XVI pp. 15–16) he found only a small votive stupa and "can scarcely suppose" it the
  honey stupa. The later seasons found a Kushana–Gupta monastery of 12 cells 80 m SW of the pillar, a Gupta
  svastika-shaped monastery and over eighty votive stupas N and W of the tank, and a Gupta sherd "Buddha Nandasya"
  (L. C. Singh, IAR 1989–90, pp. 11–13; 1996–97, pp. 5–6; Muhammed K. K., 1997–99). None is singled out as the honey
  stupa.
