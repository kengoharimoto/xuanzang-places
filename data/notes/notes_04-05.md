# Notes on disputed places: juan 4–5

Places in juan 4–5 that meet at least one of the three criteria in `NOTES_INSTRUCTIONS.md`, in master-table order
(35 notes), with the evidence of the Archaeological Survey reports, the Survey's Annual Reports (1903–37), the
inscriptions edited in Epigraphia Indica and the Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum, and the excavations reported in
*Indian Archaeology – A Review* (1953–2001). Gazetteer volumes are cited as `district year`. Annual Reports are
cited as AR with the year and page, by author (Vogel, Sahni, Francke, Marshall). Inscriptions are cited by editor
with EI or CII volume and page; excavations as IAR with the year and page, by excavator. The IAR volumes 2007–14
are cited from `coded/iar_batch11.tsv` under **Recent scholarship.**
Survey reports are cited as ASR with volume and page, and are Cunningham's: I (1862–63), II (1863–65), III
(1871–72), V (1872–73), IX, XI (1875–78), XIV (1878–79), XVII (1881–82), XX (1882–83), XXI (1883–85). "Atkinson"
means the Himalayan Districts text, which exists as *Himalayan districts II 1884* and in the Cornell scan catalogued
as *unidentified 1874* (cu31924023947751). The two have the same wording and are counted as one text. Checked but
not noted, because the authorities agree, no gazetteer argues and the survey only confirms Mizutani: 04.02.03
(Tamasavana = Sultanpur; ASR XIV, pp. 55–59, measured the Badshahi serai on the old mound and suspected it stands on
the monastery's foundations), 05.04.04 (the Great Giving Field at the confluence; ASR I, p. 298, only notes that
the sandy plain has been eroded).

### 04.01 磔迦國 (Skt. Takka) — the country is the central Punjab; the capital is still open (Asarur or near Sialkot)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Punjab plain between the Beas and the text's "Indus" (taken to be the Chenāb);
capital unnamed, perhaps "new Śākala" (uncertain). Cunningham (1871, pp. 170, 207–8): the plains from the Indus to
the Beas; the capital Taki is the ruins of Asarur, 19 miles NE of Sangala. Majumdar (1924, p. 687): with Śākala at
Sialkot, the capital belongs c. 2 miles NE of Sialkot, not at Asarur. Vivien (p. 328): the Ravi–Beas doab, capital
near Amritsar. Beal (p. 165): Chenab/Ravi region. Watters (p. 291): no site. Dey: Madra/Takkadeśa between the
Ravi and Chenab.

**Survey.** Cunningham found Asarur in November 1863 (ASR II, pp. 191–203): ruins 15,600 ft round, which "agrees
sufficiently well" with the 20-li circuit, moulded and wedge-shaped bricks, and the Sodra mound near Thatta Sayyadan
for the Asoka stupa. The distance from Sangala (16 miles direct) does not fit the text's 15 li, so he reads 115 li.
Later volumes add only texts: the Takiya of the Chach-nāma (ASR V, p. 113, with moulded bricks from Asarur) and the
Thakkiya of the Rājataraṅgiṇī, "close to Sangala" (ASR IX, pp. 84, 103). The survey's view never moved.

**Gazetteers.** Gujranwala 1883 echoes ASR II at length: the circuit "≈ 20 li", moulded bricks, a 21-ft well, the
Sodra mound and the Ransi mound with its Nao-gaja tomb. Jhang 1884, Lyallpur 1905 and Punjab 1919 repeat the same
view. None of them argues independently.

**Inscriptions.** Only an editor's remark: Bhandarkar (CII 3, p. 9) reports Stein's Takka between the Chenab and the
Ravi in Xuanzang's time. No inscription names Takka or its capital.

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 4's distances and bearings takes 2 mi
NE of Sialkot (Majumdar 1924) here, not Mizutani's Punjab plain between Beas and Chenab (route/README.md §6). The
figures are one kind of evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 4 takes Asarur (Tse-kia)
(Cunningham 1871; Cunningham ASR II) here, not Mizutani's Punjab plain between Beas and Chenab (route/README.md §7).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji takes the country as the whole Punjab plain from the Beas to the Indus and from the
Himalayan foothills to below the Panjnad near Multan, reading the text's 信度河 as the Indus rather than Mizutani's
Chenāb (p. 353). For the capital he gives Cunningham's Asarur, 2 miles S of the Lahore–Pindi Bhattian road, whose
large bricks and Kushan coins show an old town (pp. 353–354). He does not say how Asarur stands to Śākala, which he
leaves open (04.01.02), so his Asarur keeps the dependence on Sanglawala Tibba noted below.

**The road from Rajauri.** Drew's route table (1875, App. IV, route 4) gives Rajaori to Bhimbar as 56 miles and
Bhimbar to Gujrat on the plain as "80" in the OCR, which Stein's 28 map miles show to be 30: about 86 road miles over
the hills. Measured on to Sialkot the 700 li come to about 181 km (0.26 km per li, the normal value), on to Asarur
about 223 km (0.32, just above the 0.175–0.30 found along the other juan 3 roads), so the distance leans slightly to
Sialkot but does not decide between them (route/README.md §10, Test 4).

**Name.** 磔 has a retroflex initial (知) with a -k coda, so the characters give Ṭakka, not the dental Takka of
Mizutani's romanisation, which is probably a dropped diacritic. The Sanskrit sources have the retroflex: Ṭakkadeśa in
the *Rājataraṅgiṇī* (v.150, with Stein's note) and ṭakkaja horses in the *Mahāsubhāṣitasaṃgraha* (7232). High
confidence for the form; the identification is unaffected (data/names/).

**Assessment.** The survey shows that a large early town lay at Asarur and that Takka was a real district name, but
its placing of the capital still depends on Cunningham's Sangala (Sanglawala Tibba) and on an emended distance. If
Śākala is Sialkot, as Mizutani holds, Asarur loses its anchor and Majumdar's site near Sialkot follows. Mizutani's
tentative picture stands.

### 04.01.02 奢羯羅故城 (Skt. Śākala) — Sialkot (Mizutani) against Sanglawala Tibba (Cunningham); the gazetteers only repeat Cunningham

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sialkot (disputed). Majumdar (p. 686) and Dey: Sialkot, rejecting Sangala as a
different place. Cunningham (p. 206) and Beal (p. 166): Sanglawala Tibba, west of the Ravi. Vivien (p. 329): near
Amritsar. Watters (p. 288): uncertain.

**Survey.** Cunningham surveyed the Sangala hill in 1863 (ASR II, pp. 37–38, 191–99): a rocky triangle, its north
side 215 ft high, brick ruins c. 1.5 miles round, bricks 15 × 9 × 3 in. He put the Asoka stupa on the Mundapapura
ridge to the NW, read the robbers' forest as the Pilu woods he walked through, and the Āpagā as the Ayak Nadi.
Against the classical writers who put Sangala east of the Ravi, he holds that "the detailed measurements of Hwen
Thsang are too precise … and the coincidence of name is too exact to be set aside" (p. 199). He did not examine
Sialkot, and the view never moved (ASR IX, p. 103).

**Gazetteers.** Jhang 1884, Lyallpur 1905 and Gujranwala 1883 (19 passages, three volumes) all repeat Cunningham.
Their local detail is the small rocky hill with brick-covered ridges and "hills of iron-bearing grey rock", which
they read as matching Xuanzang's note on iron. Gujranwala adds Cunningham's claim that Ransi = Nara-Sinha confirms
the identification.

**Inscriptions.** Two editors take Sialkot for Śākala in passing: Vogel (EI 16, p. 15: "Sial … probably derived
from Sagala") and Bhandarkar (CII 3, p. 24). Neither publishes an inscription that names the place.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji leaves the site open between Cunningham's Sānglawala Tiba (31°43′N 73°27′E) W of the
Ravi and Sialkot, for which he cites Fleet's note in the Proceedings of the Fourteenth Oriental Congress (p. 355); in
his note on Mihirakula he gives Sialkot as "one view" (p. 360). Fleet's argument is not reproduced.

**Kuwayama (2002).** In passing, "Sagala (present Sialkot)" (pp. 1, 109), without argument.

**Assessment.** The survey measured the hill but found nothing that names it; the case is still name and
itinerary, and the ruins (1.5 miles round) are small for a great city. Neither survey nor gazetteers weaken
Mizutani's Sialkot, and the epigraphists' asides favour it.

### 04.02 至那僕底國 (Skt. Cīnabhukti) — Chiniyari (Chine), as Mizutani holds; Cunningham himself abandoned Patti

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Chiniyari, on the Amritsar–Sialkot road (disputed). Majumdar (p. 687): Chine or
Chiniyari, tentatively. Cunningham (1871, pp. 230, 232) and Beal (p. 173): Patti (Haibatpur-Patti), 27 miles NE of
Kasur. Watters (p. 293): Patti "need not be seriously considered". Vivien (p. 332): the Katoch country (Kangra).
Smith (per Mizutani): Ferozpore.

**Survey.** In 1878–79 Cunningham gave up Patti (ASR XIV, pp. iv, 53–54). He visited "the lofty mound of China, 11
miles from Amritsar, which, I have no doubt, is the Chinapati of Hwen Thsang": the villages Unchakila, Bichlakila
and Shahbazpur on the road to Sialkot, a central mound 400 ft square and 46–50 ft high with a lower mound twice its
size on the north, large bricks (12.5 × 8.5 × 2.5 in), and Mirza Mughal Beg's "Chiniyari, also called Alexander's
mound". ASR XXI (p. 134) adds that a brick contractor digging the mound found a very old Viṣṇu image.

**Gazetteers.** Lahore 1883 (two scans) argues for Patti with the reasoning of the *Ancient Geography*: Lahore is
the "great Brahmanical city" on Takka's eastern border, and "midway between the two cities [Lahore and Jullundur]
is a village called Patti, to this day". It does not know the survey's revision.

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 4's distances and bearings takes Patti
(Cunningham 1871; Beal 1884; Lahore 1883) here, not Mizutani's Chiniyari, between Amritsar and Sialkot
(route/README.md §6). The figures are one kind of evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 4 takes Patti (Cunningham
1871; Beal 1884; Lahore 1883) here, not Mizutani's Chiniyari, between Amritsar and Sialkot (route/README.md §7).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji leaves the site open (p. 366). He rejects Cunningham's Patti as contrary to the Life
(50 li SE to Tāmasavana, then 140–150 li NE to Jālaṃdhara) and finds no strong evidence for Shastri's Chiniyari or
Smith's site near Ferozpore. The objection to Patti does not hold if Tāmasavana is Sultanpur, as Ji himself accepts
(04.02.03): Patti lies c. 33 km W of Sultanpur, Chine c. 73 km NW, so Patti is the nearer of the two to the 50 li. He
does not know Cunningham's withdrawal of Patti or the mound at Chine.

**Assessment.** The only argument for Patti was the name, and its author withdrew it after seeing a large ancient
mound at Chine that keeps the name and lies on the right road. Mizutani's Chiniyari is strengthened, though nothing
found there is specifically Buddhist.

### 04.03 闍爛達羅國 (Skt. Jālaṃdhara) — Jalandhar; the gazetteers argue only about how far the kingdom extended

**Identifications.** All authorities: Jalandhar. Cunningham (pp. 156–7) and Majumdar (p. 683): Trigarta, which
included the hills as far as Kangra.

**Gazetteers.** Kangra 1883 argues that the kingdom covered "the low hills from the Ravi to Simla" and that the
eleven hill principalities did not yet exist separately. Hoshiarpur 1884 and 1905 and Jalandhar 1884 and 1908
convert the text's dimensions to miles. Jalandhar 1884 adds that the old city was a little over 2 miles round and
that only two tanks (Gufa, Brahmkund) remain.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Jullundur, SE of Amritsar (p. 371).

**Assessment.** The site is not in question. The gazetteers support Mizutani and Cunningham and add the
Kangra-hills extent.

### 04.04 屈露多國 (*Kulūta) — Kulu; Atkinson offers Jagatsukh (Nast) as the old capital

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Kulu district of the upper Beas; old capital Nagar (agreed). Cunningham
(pp. 162–3): Kulu, old capital Makarsa or Nagar. Beal, Watters, Majumdar and Dey: Kulu.

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson (Himalayan districts II 1884) names "Nast or Jagatsukh in the upper valley of the Bias,
the ancient seat of the Pala Rajas of Kulu" and notes that "Kulu is still called Kulanta by the people". Kangra
1883 and 1899 argue that the kingdom took in Bangahal, Seoraj, Bashahr and the hill parts of Mandi and Suket,
since the 3,000-li circuit is too large for the Kulu valley alone.

**Excavations.** No digging. Vogel (AR 1903–04, pp. 263–64), publishing a 16th-century Kulu copper plate, takes
the equation as settled: "Kuḷḷū is undoubtedly one of the most ancient principalities of the Panjab Hills", and
11th-century Chamba plates already name the lord of Kulūta. Nothing on the capital.

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 4's distances and bearings takes
Makarsa (Cunningham 1871) here, not Mizutani's Kulu; old capital Nagar (route/README.md §6). The figures are one kind
of evidence; see the assessment.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Kulu on the upper Beas, after Cunningham, with the old capital still called
Nagar and the present one at Sultānpur (p. 373). His name for the old capital, Nagarkot, is the usual name of Kangra
and should not be read as that town.

**Assessment.** The country is certain: the surviving name Kulanta and the Chamba plates' Kulūta secure the name
equation. Jagatsukh is
a candidate for the capital that none of the authorities records. It rests on dynastic tradition only, against
the Nagar tradition Mizutani reports.

### 04.04a 洛護羅國 (Lāhul) — Lahul; the gazetteers repeat it and do not meet Jäschke's objection

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Lahul, name doubtful (disputed, hearsay). Cunningham (p. 164), Beal (p. 177),
Watters (p. 299), Majumdar (p. 684) and Vivien (p. 334): Lahul (Lho-yul).

**Gazetteers.** Kangra 1883 and 1899 quote Cunningham's "Lahul (Lho-yul of the Tibetans)". Atkinson mentions
"Lo-hou-lo, the modern Lahul" in passing while discussing the Amazonian kingdom. The three "argues" in
`places.tsv` come from that passage, which argues about the Amazonian kingdom, not about Lahul.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Lāhul, the Tibetans' Lho-yul ("southern land"), noting Jäschke's objection
and that the country was known by report (p. 374).

**Name.** 洛護羅 fits la-hu-la exactly (護 is 匣 for h, as 怙 hu is twice in the key). Lāhula is attested in Abhinavagupta's
Tantrāloka (15.90, "kulūtā lāhulā tathā"), an Indian list that names it beside Kulūta (04.04), so Xuanzang transcribes
an Indian name and the Tibetan Lho-yul is not needed; the Nīlamata's Lāhulā is a Kashmir river, a homonym. Medium
confidence (data/names/).

**Assessment.** The gazetteers repeat Cunningham and add nothing on the linguistic objection Mizutani reports
(Jäschke: Lāhul is the Indian, not the Tibetan, name). The Tantrāloka's Lāhula beside Kulūta meets the objection:
Xuanzang transcribes the Indian name, and Lho-yul is not needed. Lahul is strengthened, at medium confidence, since
the entry is hearsay and the placement rests on direction.

### 04.04b 秣邏娑國 (Tib. mar-sa) — Ladakh; the gazetteers add nothing

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Ladakh (mar-yul) (disputed; Satō objects). Cunningham (p. 164), Beal (p. 178),
Watters (p. 299) and Majumdar (p. 684, after Francke): Ladakh. Vivien (p. 334): 三波訶 = Champaka/Chamba.

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson (1884) puts Mo-lo-so or Malasa, also called San-po-ho, "some 2,000 li … to the north of
Lo-hou-lo", while looking for the Amazonian kingdom. This is consistent with Ladakh but names no site.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Ladakh. He reports Cunningham's Mar-po ("red land", emending 娑 to 婆) and
Shastri's Mar-sa = Mar-yul ("lowland"), and leaves the gloss 三波訶 unexplained (p. 374). He does not mention Vivien's
Chamba.

**Assessment.** No gazetteer evidence bears on the Ladakh equation. Vivien's Chamba remains the only real
alternative among the authorities.

### 04.05 設多圖盧國 (Skt. Śatadru) — the Sarhind tract; Sanghol, with excavated Kushan stupas and a monastery, is now a candidate for the capital

**Identifications.** Mizutani: named after the Sutlej, capital Sarhind (uncertain). Cunningham (pp. 166–7, after
correcting the distance to Bairāt), Beal (p. 178) and Dey: Sarhind. Vivien (p. 336): in the Sarasvati basin,
near Sirtah west of Thanesar. Watters (p. 300): nothing is known.

**Gazetteers.** No hits. The survey reports have no passage on it either.

**Later excavations.** At Sanghol (Ludhiana district), some 15 km from Sarhind, the Punjab state department
(K. K. Rishi and Kuldip Singh, IAR 1989–90, p. 93) found a Kushan stupa of "dharma-chakra" plan (S-2), a second
stupa (S-3) beside a 27-cell monastery (39 × 36 m), coins of the Kunindas, Kushans and Hūṇas, and a
Kharoṣṭhī-inscribed bowl. The excavators claim that "this discovery corroborated the description given by
Hiuen-Tsang … at She-tu-to-lu (now Sanghol)". Their "10 stupas and monasteries" misreads the text, which has ten
saṅghārāmas, and no inscription names the place; the Hūṇa coins carry occupation into the 6th century, but nothing
of Xuanzang's own time is reported.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees that the country is named after the Sutlej and reports Cunningham's Sarhind
for the capital, but says no satisfactory identification exists (pp. 375–376). He has nothing on Sanghol.

**The river on the west.** The text puts a great river on the country's west (西臨大河), and the route reaches it
from Kulūta by "crossing a great river" (濟大河): the Sutlej, after which the country is named, so the country lies
on the river's far bank. The Sutlej leaves the hills running south, from Bhakra past Anandpur to Ropar, and only
there turns west. From Sarhind and Sanghol it lies north (10° and 1°, Natural Earth courses); from Ropar north-west
(302° at the nearest point); from Nalagarh, east of the southward stretch, west-north-west (289°). So the phrase fits
a country in the Ropar–Nalagarh tract, east of the river's southward course, better than one centred on Sarhind
or Sanghol, unless the phrase describes the country's western reach rather than its capital's side. Ropar has an
excavated mound with "large deposits belonging to the early and late historical periods" (IAR 1953–54), and the
explorations of Ropar tahsil list Harappan, Painted Grey Ware, early historical and medieval sites (IAR 1953–54,
1962–63, 1968–69, 1977–78), but a search of the Punjab gazetteers, the survey reports and IAR 1953–2014 for Ropar,
Nalagarh, Kiratpur, Kurali, Kharar and Pinjore found no Buddhist structure or object reported there. The route figures do not decide: from Kulūta 700 li south fits all
these places (Sarhind 162 km, Ropar 123 km, Nalagarh 109 km, roughly south-south-west), and the 800 li south-west
to Bairāt fits none (350–400 km, due south).

**Assessment.** Sarhind rests on Cunningham's emended distance alone and has no Buddhist remains. Sanghol lies in
the same tract and has a large excavated Buddhist establishment, so it is the better candidate for a capital with
monasteries, though its case is still the excavators' assertion. The country stays where Mizutani puts it; the
capital moves, tentatively, from Sarhind to Sanghol. The river on the west points rather to the Ropar–Nalagarh tract
east of the Sutlej's southward course, the Buddhist remains to Sanghol: the capital stays open between them.

### 04.06 波理夜呾羅國 (Skt. Pāriyātra) — Bairāt is still the only candidate, but its excavator found nothing of Xuanzang's eight monasteries

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Bairāt (Virāṭa), 41 miles N of Jaipur, ancient Matsya (agreed); he cites Sahni's
*Archaeological Remains and Excavations at Bairat* (1937). Cunningham (p. 387), Vivien (p. 337) and Watters
(p. 300, after Reinaud): Bairāt. Beal (p. 179) accepts it tentatively; bearing and distance from Sarhind do not
agree. Majumdar (p. 702): the distance is double the 800 li from Śatadru. Dey describes the mountain range, not a
different site.

**Survey.** ASR II (pp. 242–48): the mound "corresponds almost exactly" to the 14–15 li circuit, and the platforms
with monks' cells on the Bijak hill, a mile SW, where an Asoka inscription was found, are taken for two of the
eight monasteries.

**Gazetteers.** Rajputana 1908 and Alwar 1968 repeat the identification, noting the two Asokan inscriptions and
early copper coins.

**Excavations.** Sahni dug the Bijak-ki-pahari site in 1935–36 (AR 1935–36, p. 87): a circular brick temple, two
Asokan pillars in fragments, and a single monastery, rebuilt twice, whose coin hoard ends with Hermaios; "no
objects of a later date than the 2nd century A.D." He concludes: "My own excavations yielded no evidence to
corroborate this identification. The discovery of only one monastery in place of the eight … seems to militate
strongly against" it. He allows that the town mound, two miles off, may hold Buddhist strata, but doubts that
Xuanzang would have failed to say the monasteries were inside the town.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Bairāt, 105 miles SW of Delhi and 41 miles N of Jaipur, after Reinaud,
Cunningham and Watters; he lists the Aśokan edicts and Mauryan finds there without citing a report (p. 377). He does
not mention Sahni's verdict against the identification.

**Assessment.** The excavation removes the survey's monastic evidence: Cunningham's two monasteries are one,
deserted some five centuries before Xuanzang. Bairāt stays the only proposed site, on name, Matsya tradition and
the circuit of the town mound, but the distance has always been wrong and the only excavator of its Buddhist
remains rejects it. Mizutani's "agreed" is too strong, and he cites the report that undermines it.

### 04.07 秣菟羅國 (Skt. Mathurā) — Mathura; the ancient city is the area within the Dhulkot rampart, which takes in the Katra

**Identifications.** All authorities: Mathura. Cunningham (1871, pp. 427–8) and Majumdar (p. 706): the town has
moved north and west as the Jumna encroached.

**Survey.** In 1862–63 (ASR I, pp. 233–39) Cunningham worked from the Katra and the jail mound; by 1881–83 (ASR
XVII, pp. 111–12; XX, pp. 31–32) the 7th-century city itself is the Katra, with the Jumna in the old channel under
its walls.

**Gazetteers.** Muttra 1911 repeats Vogel (below) and reports Führer's old Buddhist city "probably … north of the
present city beyond the Katra". Muttra 1884 and Mathura 1968 argue for Buddhist remains at Mahaban, on the left
bank opposite.

**Excavations.** Vogel (AR 1906–07, p. 140), reviewing all past finds: the attempts of Cunningham and Growse to
identify the sites "have signally failed. Both assumed that the Katra marks the centre of the ancient city, whereas
the site of ancient Mathura is clearly indicated by an extensive elevation of the soil to the south-west of the
town." The Katra was the Yaśa-vihāra, still standing in 549–50 (dated image).

**Later excavations.** A trench on the Katra reached natural soil at 42 ft, through grey ware and NBP levels; the
Katra "represents a large part of the ancient urban settlement", inside
"two rings of mud-ramparts", an outer elliptical and an inner quadrangular (Venkataramayya and Ballabh Saran, IAR
1954–55, pp. 15–16). A section across the Dhulkot (Joshi and Srivastava, IAR 1973–74, pp. 31–32) showed it was
"built as a mud-fortification around the ancient city of Mathura": crescentic, with the Jumna on the east,
enclosing over 3 sq km, tentatively Śaka–Kushan.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Muttra on the W bank of the Jumna, the old city lying further S and the newer
town built to the N as the river encroached (p. 379).

**Assessment.** The identification is secure. On the layout the excavations go against Vogel's verdict: the Katra
lies inside the walled ancient city, as Cunningham held, though Vogel's point that it carried a monastery (the
Yaśa-vihāra) stands. Whether his elevation SW of the town falls within the Dhulkot the reports do not say.
Führer's city north of the Katra and a Buddhist Mahaban remain variants.

### 04.07.03 鄔波毱多伽藍 (Upagupta's monastery) — not located; Cunningham moved it twice, and Mizutani's verdict stands

**Identifications.** Mizutani: cannot now be located (disputed). Cunningham (1871): the Yaśa Vihāra on the Katra
mound. Growse and Dey: Kankali Tila. Beal (p. 181): no mound fits the bearing. Watters (p. 308): Katra "undoubtedly
wrong", Kankali "not quite convincing".

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 233–34) and III (pp. 28–29) put the monastery at the Katra, reading the text's "east" as
west, with the monkey's tank at the jail mound. Once he took the Katra for the city itself, Cunningham moved the
monastery to "the present old fort on the bank of the Jumna, which is just about 1 mile to the east of the Katra"
(ASR XX, pp. 32–33; ASR XVII, pp. 111–12, with the Ambarikha mound for the cave hill). Nothing Buddhist was
found at the fort.

**Gazetteers.** Muttra 1884 reports Growse's Kankali Tila, with its colossal Buddha statues. Muttra 1911 rejects
all the proposals, in Vogel's words (below), and notes that Kankali Tila is a Jain stupa.

**Excavations.** Vogel (AR 1906–07, p. 140): the identifications, "based on a wrong location of the city, are
inadmissible, and both the Upagupta monastery and the monkey tank are still to be discovered". He adds that Cunningham "had to alter the wording of the Chinese
pilgrim's account", reading "west" for "east" and "built of stone" for "cut in the rock".

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji gives no site; he notes that some take the text's 山伽藍 for Upagupta's Naṭabhaṭa
monastery (p. 381).

**Name.** Mizutani's original name for this entry, Uruṇḍa (優留曼陀山), belongs to the hill, not to the monastery: 鄔波毱多
transcribes Upagupta (鄔 u, 波 pa, 毱多 the stock -gupta). The entry's name is Upagupta, secure; the mismatch is one of
labelling and does not bear on the site. Xuanzang's own gloss 唐言近護 translates upa-gupta, and the *Aśokāvadāna* places
the monk at Mathurā ("upagupto nāma … buddho 'dya dharmaṃ deśayiṣyati"); the monastery of the legend there is the
Naṭabhaṭikā, the second name in Mizutani's entry (data/names/).

**Assessment.** Each proposal follows from a view of where the city lay, and the survey's own shift shows how
loose they are. The Katra is named epigraphically for another monastery and Kankali is Jain. Vogel's ground for
rejecting them, a wrong city site, has since lapsed (the Katra lies within the Dhulkot rampart, 04.07), but no
Upagupta monastery has been found. The site stays unknown, as Mizutani says.

### 04.08 薩他泥濕伐羅國 (Skt. Sthāneśvara) — Thanesar; the measured circuit and the excavated Vardhana-period town settle it

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Thanesar (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 376–8), Vivien, Beal, Majumdar (p. 701) and
Dey: Thanesar. Watters (p. 315): the distances do not fit and the site should be c. 100 miles NE of Mathurā.

**Survey.** Cunningham measured the mounds of the fort, the town and Bahari at 14,000 ft round, c. 4 miles with the
hollow between them (ASR II, pp. 220–21), and took the Madrasa mound 4–5 li NW for the Asoka stupa.

**Gazetteers.** Karnal 1976 restates Sahni's case (below), with the stupa mound ¼ mile NW (Sahni: ¾ mile). Ambala
1883 and Meerut division 1875 repeat Cunningham.

**Excavations.** Sahni trial-dug the fort in 1922–23 (AR 1922–23, pp. 90–91): the three mounds "together occupy a
space of about three miles, which closely agrees with the 20 li"; the fort's 14-in. brick structures "cannot be
anterior to about the 7th century A.D." and were probably Harṣa's; he found a west gate with an 11-ft rampart.

**Inscriptions.** Bühler, editing Harṣa's grant (EI 1, pp. 68–69), takes Sthāṇvīśvara, the seat of Harṣa's
ancestors, for Thanesar and the kingdom for the one "which Hiuen Tsiang describes".

**Later excavations.** B. M. Pande dug the Harsh-ka-Tila, a mound 900 × 500 m and 15–23 m high, in 1987–91 (IAR
1987–88, pp. 28–30; 1988–89, pp. 21–24; 1989–90, pp. 27–29; 1990–91, pp. 18–19). Above Kushan and Gupta levels, "the
Pushyabhuti or Vardhana period (middle of the sixth to end of seventh century) was distinguished by a massive brick
building" (a wall of 99+ courses), a renovated fortification, a stone inscription fragment in 7th-century characters
and a head "vaguely recalling the figure … on the coins of Harsha". In 1990–91 the Vardhana deposits showed an earlier
phase of the same building complex, with a terracotta seal and moulds, and a two-phase Kushan mud rampart was traced
on the S slope. No Buddhist monument of the period was found.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji accepts Cunningham's Thānesar only provisionally, because its direction and distance
from Mathurā do not fit the text; he reports V. Smith in agreement, Watters against, and Majumdar Sastri's Thūṇa of
the Mahāvagga (pp. 388–389). His doubt is Watters', and he does not know Sahni's or Pande's excavations.

**Name.** The characters give Sthāneśvara (泥 ne). The *Harṣacarita*'s Sthāṇvīśvara, the form Bühler uses for the
seat of Harṣa's family (above), does not fit 泥 (no ṇv, no ī). Xuanzang heard the form in -e-, the one nearer to the
modern Thanesar. The identification is unaffected (data/names/).

**Assessment.** The measured circuit, twice confirmed, and a fortified town with a palatial building of Harṣa's
dynasty answer Watters' objection, which rests on distances alone. Nothing names the place on the site, but the
dynasty's seat is attested by its own grant. The Asoka stupa is still undug. Mizutani's identification is
strengthened.

### 04.08.02 福地 (Kurukṣetra "Happy Land") — Kurukshetra; the extent is read three ways, and the Asthipur mound is doubtful

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Kurukshetra battlefield, with Asthipur west of Thanesar (agreed). Cunningham
(pp. 381, 385), Beal (p. 184) and Vivien: likewise. Watters (p. 315): a 200-li circuit round Sthāneśvara, not
the Kurukshetra plain.

**Survey.** In ASR II (pp. 212–18) Cunningham reads 400 li for the text's 200, to fit the 48-kos pilgrimage
circuit, and reports that "the site of Asthipur is still pointed out in the plain to the west of the city towards
Aujasghat". In ASR XIV (pp. 87, 98) he instead takes the 200 li as the length of each side and excavated the
Asthipura mound (Madrasa Tila, 700 × 500 ft), finding a platform of unbaked bricks 364 ft long, walls, terracottas
and a head of Śiva, but no bones.

**Gazetteers.** Karnal 1884 (Ibbetson, two scans) equates the Happy Land with the Nardak, argues from a local
etymology (nir-daya against dukh), and says the 200 li "may surely be taken to mean … the radius", close to
Cunningham's later reading. Ambala 1883 repeats ASR II on the Asthipur spot. Karnal 1976 reports that the mound was
excavated (Theh Polar) and yielded 3rd–4th-century AD finds, which it says make the Mahābhārata tradition doubtful.

**Excavations.** Sahni (AR 1921–22, pp. 46–47) defines the region as the land between the Sarasvatī and the
Dṛṣadvatī, with some 360 tīrthas, and lists Amin, Theh Polar, Pehoa and the Thanesar mounds, of which "none …
have yet been explored". He does not discuss Asthipur or the 200 li, and seems unaware of Cunningham's digging.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Kurukṣetra, S of Thānesar between the Sarasvatī and the Dṛṣadvatī, with a
place called Asthipur still in Thānesar's W suburb (pp. 390–391).

**Assessment.** The region is not in doubt. On the extent, both Cunningham's later reading and Ibbetson's abandon
the plain circumference. At Asthipur the survey's own excavation found a large building, not a battlefield, and the
later finds weaken it as the ancient spot.

### 04.08.04 倶昏荼僧伽藍 (*Kohuṇḍa monastery) — unknown; Cunningham's Gunana with Karnal 1884's Sita Mai is the only candidate

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no site, only rival restorations of the name (unknown). Cunningham (p. 395,
tentative): probably Gunana, 17 miles SSW of Thanesar. Beal (p. 186) and Watters (p. 315): no site.

**Survey.** ASR II (pp. 226–27) gives the same proposal: Gunana "appears to agree very well with the recorded
position". No remains are described.

**Gazetteers.** Karnal 1884 (two scans) follows Cunningham's Gunana and suggests "it is not improbable that the
Gominda monastery" survives as the Sita Mai monastery, 4 miles from Gunana. Meerut division 1875 mentions the
monastery only in passing in its Srughna passage (see the data remarks).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji leaves the name (Gominda, Gokaṇṭha, Govinda) and the site undecided (p. 392).

**Name.** None of the restorations fits: 倶 is k (g would be 羣), 昏 is h-u-n (曉 h, with Xuanzang's tone mark) and 荼 a
retroflex ḍa, so the characters give *Kohuṇḍa or *Kuhuṇḍa; Gokaṇṭha, Govinda and Gominda all lose the h of 昏 and need
other characters for their consonants. Nothing of this form is attested in the Sanskrit library. Cunningham's Gunana
shares no sound with it; low confidence (data/names/).

**Assessment.** Gunana or Sita Mai is a tentative candidate, resting on Cunningham's reading of the route and a
surviving monastic site. It is better than nothing but far from secure. The name gives Gunana no support (it shares no
sound with *Kohuṇḍa), so the candidate rests on the route alone.

### 04.09 窣祿勤那國 (Skt. Srughna) — Sugh near Jagadhri (Ambala district), not Kalsi; a sealing reading "Sugha" names the site

**Identifications.** Mizutani: "Sugh near Kālsī (30°32'N 77°53'E), Dehra Dun district" (agreed). Cunningham
(pp. 396, 398) and Watters: Sugh. Beal (p. 187): at or near Kalsi. Majumdar and Dey: Sugh near Kalsi, but Dey
also gives 20 miles NW of Saharanpur. Vivien: the Saharanpur sarkar.

**Survey.** The Kalsi association began with ASR I (pp. c. 160, 246), which only put the Kalsi rock "within the
territory of Srughna". The next season Cunningham revised this: "I found a village named
Sugh" in a bend of the old Jumna, with three forts whose circuit, 19,000 ft without the northern one, is "in accord
with the pilgrim's measurement" (ASR II, pp. 226–31). ASR XIV (p. 138): Kuninda coins are most plentiful about Sugh.

**Gazetteers.** Ambala 1883, Meerut division 1875 and Saharanpur 1981 place Sugh "about 5 km east of Jagadhari
in the Ambala district", with coins, large bricks and tradition. Dehra Dun 1911 and Atkinson's "not far below
Kalsi" follow ASR I.

**Later excavations.** Panjab University dug at Sugh in 1963–64 (IAR 1963–64, p. 27): NBP and grey ware, Śuṅga–Kushan terracottas, and 1.6 km W a large burnt-brick enclosure, "either a fortress
or an enclosure of some monastery". In 1965–66 (Chhabra and Suraj Bhan, IAR 1965–66, p. 36) Period II (c. 100 BC –
AD 500) gave "inscribed clay sealings, one bearing the legend Vyagharaja and the other Sugha, both in the third
century A.D. characters". Haryana's digs of 1995–99 (Malik and Acharya, IAR 1995–96, p. 25; 1998–99, pp. 23–24)
take Srughna as given but found nothing of Xuanzang's pillar or monastery. Acharya's season of 1993–94 (IAR 1993–94,
pp. 51–52) recalls Xuanzang's pillar and monastery but found only Period I (5th–1st c. BC, with NBP sherds) and
Period II (1st c. BC – 3rd c. AD, Śuṅga–Kushan terracottas, brick walls) in 2.1 m of deposit: nothing of his time.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji reports Cunningham's Sugh, placing it near Kalsi with the coordinates 30°32′N
77°53′E, as Mizutani does, and doubts it: apart from the name, neither the distance nor the size fits, since Sugh is
only 40 miles from Thānesar and the tract is far too small for a country of 6,000 li round, even on the wider bounds
some propose (p. 393). The coordinates are Kalsi's, so Ji repeats the confusion noted above. His distance point is
weaker than it looks: the text's 400 li NE run from the Gokaṇṭha monastery, itself 100 li S of the city, so the net
distance from Thānesar is c. 300 li, against c. 55 km to Sugh near Jagadhri.

**Assessment.** The "Sugha" sealing names the place on the site, in the Kushan–Gupta period rather than in Xuanzang's
time, and with the measured circuit and the coins it settles Sugh. Ji's doubt rests on the distance and the country's
size; measured from the Gokaṇṭha monastery, as the text has it, the distance is short but not badly so, and a
country's circuit cannot outweigh a sealing that names the site. Mizutani's coordinates and "Dehra Dun district" are
Kalsi's, some 40 miles away, and reflect Cunningham's first, abandoned view; they should be corrected to Sugh, Ambala
district. Beal's Kalsi is not supported.

### 04.09.03 殑伽河源 (the Ganges "source") — where the Ganges leaves the hills near Hardwar; no gazetteer evidence

**Identifications.** Mizutani: not the Himalayan source but where the Ganges enters the plain near Hardwar
(uncertain). Watters (p. 315): Gaṅgādvāra or Hardwar. Vivien (p. 341): Gangotri. Beal (p. 188): undecided.

**Gazetteers.** No hits.

**Assessment.** The river 3–4 li wide that Beal cites argues for the plains reading. Nothing here changes
Mizutani's view.

### 04.10 秣底補羅國 (*Matipura) — the Bijnor side of the Ganges; Mandawar as capital is plausible but unexcavated

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the region on the east bank of the Ganges opposite Srughna (disputed). Cunningham
(p. 399), Vivien (p. 343), Beal (p. 190) and Dey: Mandawar, near Bijnor. Majumdar (p. 703): Bijnor district.
Watters (p. 321): Mandawar fits neither direction nor area.

**Survey.** Cunningham reached Madawar in 1862–63 (ASR I, pp. 248–51): an old mound half a mile square and 10 ft
high with large bricks, a fort 300 ft square, a town deserted in 1114 by the Kanungo's account, and a circuit that
fits the 20 li. He placed Guṇaprabha's monastery at the Lalpur mound, Saṅghabhadra's at Hidayat Shah's shrine,
and Vimalamitra's stupa by the Birwala Tal and its mango grove. In ASR II (pp. 230–31) he halves the 800 li from
Srughna. Nothing was excavated.

**Gazetteers.** Rohilkhand division 1879 echoes ASR I's site fits. Bijnor 1908 (five passages, two scans) doubts:
"the identification is open to the usual doubts"; the site "has never been scientifically explored"; and "these
identifications are necessarily conjectural". It notes a Buddhist stupa excavated at Mordhaj fort (Najibabad)
elsewhere in the district.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees with Watters and V. Smith that Matipura should be sought across the Ganges
from Srughna rather than at Madawar (St. Martin, Cunningham) (p. 397).

**Assessment.** Survey and gazetteers agree on the Bijnor tract east of the Ganges, which is Mizutani's region.
Mandawar as capital rests on surface mounds and an emended distance. Mizutani's region is mildly strengthened, and
the capital stays open.

### 04.10.05 摩裕羅城 (Mayūra) — Mayapur at Hardwar, with good local remains; the bank remains a question

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Hardwar (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 402–4): ruined Mayapura between Hardwar and
Kankhal, now on the west bank. Beal, Majumdar and Dey: Mayapura or Hardwar. Watters (p. 328): Mayūra was on the
east side, so it is not Mayapura. Vivien (p. 347): the east bank opposite, or perhaps Amroha.

**Survey.** ASR II (pp. 232–35): the old ruined town between Hardwar and Kankhal "is still called Mayapur", and the
Brahmakund well in a small temple at the Gaṅgādvāra is "most probably" the one Xuanzang noticed.

**Gazetteers.** Meerut division 1875, Saharanpur 1921 and 1981, and Atkinson (30 passages) repeat Cunningham but
add remains. The people point out the old city "from a torrent near the Sarovanath temple to Raja Ben's fort",
7,500 ft. There are large-brick foundations, ancient sculpture and coins, and the Maya Devi, Bhairon and Narayan
Bali temples. The Brahmakund identification echoes ASR II. Saharanpur 1981 also cites Watters for Mo-yu-lo east of
the Ganga.

**Inscriptions.** R. C. Majumdar (EI 18), editing a Jodhpur inscription of V.S. 894 that names Gaṅgādvāra and a
king Mayūra defeated by Bauka, suggests that this Mayūra "may refer to the king of the locality" of Xuanzang's
Mo-yu-lo. A conjecture only; it confirms Gaṅgādvāra = Hardwar.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees with Cunningham: the ruins of Māyāpura at the head of the Ganges canal near
Hardwar (Gaṅgādvāra), the river having shifted, against Watters' objection about the bank; he notes that Xuanzang knew
the town by report (p. 406).

**Assessment.** The remains at Mayapur strengthen Hardwar as the city. Watters' objection about the bank is
answered only by Cunningham's guess that the river has shifted, which neither survey nor gazetteer tests.

### 04.11 婆羅吸摩補羅國 (Skt. Brahmapura) — Garhwal–Kumaon; the Talesvara plates attest a royal Brahmapura in Kumaon, probably at or near Lakhanpur; not Srinagar

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Alaknanda valley, capital Srinagar (Garhwal), reading NE for N (agreed).
Vivien (p. 344): Srinagar. Cunningham (pp. 407–8): Garhwal and Kumaon, capital Vairāṭpattan or Lakhanpur on the
Ramganga; he rejects Srinagar as founded in 1358. Beal (p. 198): Garhwal and Kumaon. Francke (AR 1909–10,
p. 106, in passing, while bounding the Eastern Women's kingdom): "the upper Ravi valley".

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson (Himalayan districts II 1884, 23 passages across two scans and the Cornell copy) rejects
Srinagar, which "was of no importance until … the early part of the seventeenth century". He also rejects
Lakhanpur and the Barhepura ruins near Najibabad, and proposes Barahat (Uttarkashi), "an ancient and well-known
site almost exactly fifty miles due north of Hardwar". This keeps Xuanzang's "north". His evidence is the
12th-century trident inscription of Aneka Malla at Barahat (twinned at Gopeswar) and the route: the distance to
Govisana counts from Madawar. Garhwal 1910 accepts Barahat cautiously. Almora 1981 supports Lakhanpur: the
6th-century Taleshwar copperplates were "issued from the city of Brahmapura". Naini Tal 1904 includes Kumaon.

**Inscriptions.** T. R. Gupte (EI 13) edits the two Talesvara copper plates (Almora district), both issued from
Brahmapura, and concludes that "Brahmapura, the capital mentioned in A 1 and B 1 … was Lakhanpur or somewhere near
it", as Cunningham held. He sets aside Vogel's Brahmor in Chamba (the Brahmapura of the Chamba records, whence
Francke's upper Ravi) as too far off.

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 4's distances and bearings takes
Barahat (Uttarkashi) (Atkinson (Himalayan districts II 1884)) here, not Mizutani's Srinagar (Garhwal) (route/README.md
§6). The figures are one kind of evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 4 takes Barahat (Uttarkashi)
(Atkinson (Himalayan districts II 1884)) here, not Mizutani's Srinagar (Garhwal) (route/README.md §7).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees with Mizutani: Garhwal and Kumaon, with the capital at Srinagar (Garhwal), 70
km NE of Hardwar (pp. 407–408). Against Cunningham's emendation of 北 to 東北 he follows Watters: the 從此 of the text
means Matipura, not Mayūra, since the Life does not mention Mayūra and Xuanzang did not start from it. The next stage,
however, is introduced explicitly as 從末底補羅 (to Govisana), which suggests that the text names Matipura when it returns
to it; the reading of 此 is not secure. Ji does not answer the objection that Srinagar is late, and does not know the
Talesvara plates.

**Assessment.** A king ruling from a city called Brahmapura in Kumaon, near Xuanzang's time, is now documented
on plates found there. That does not fix the spot, but it puts the capital in the Ramganga country Cunningham
chose, not at Srinagar, which is late and which Cunningham rejects, nor at Barahat, which keeps the text's bearing
but has only a 12th-century inscription. "Agreed" understates the dispute; Lakhanpur or its neighbourhood is now
the best-supported capital.

### 04.11.02 蘇伐剌拏瞿呾羅國 (Skt. Suvarṇagotra) — a real trans-Himalayan country in western Tibet (Atkinson's Nari-khorsum, Francke's Guge and Rudok)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: a real Himalayan country, the Eastern Women's Kingdom of the Tangshu (disputed;
Thomas: partly Hunza-Nagar). Watters (p. 330): "undoubtedly mythical". Dey: north of Brahmapura; the Amazonian
kingdom in the trans-Himalayan Sutlej valley. Vivien and Beal: no location.

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson (14 passages) argues at length. Suvarṇagotra is the Suvarṇabhūmi of the Purāṇas, "the
Nari-khorsum district of Tibet lying to the north of Kumaon and Garhwal". There are "no indications or traditions
of any Amazonian kingdom" in the Katyur, Juhar or Niti valleys, so "we must search for it across the passes in
Tibet". From the Sui and Tang annals (the Eastern Nü ruled by a woman, absorbed by Lhasa after 742), "there is …
no need to doubt the statements of Hwen Thsang".

**Excavations.** Francke (AR 1909–10, pp. 105–6), reopening ancient graves at Leh, compares their multiple
burials of bones in pots, up to twenty skulls in one grave, and large bronze buttons with the Chinese account of
the Eastern Women's burial of ministers with the sovereign. From the borders in the Sui-shu and Xuanzang (Khotan,
Sanpoho = Ladakh, Brahmapura, Tibet) he concludes that "the Empire apparently comprised the Tibetan provinces of
Guge and Ruthog, and possibly Eastern Ladakh". He dates the graves to AD 1–500 and admits that the skulls are not
Tibetan.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji places the Eastern Women's Kingdom in NW Tibet, N of the Himalaya, S of Khotan and E
of Ladakh, a small matrilineal country known to Xuanzang by report, and judges the New Tang's placing too far east; he
cites Huichao's 蘇跋那具怛羅, a Kashmiri record of a Strīrājya to the north, Pelliot (Notes on Marco Polo II, pp. 696–718)
and Yamaguchi Zuihō (Tōyō gakuhō 54.4, 1971) (p. 409). This agrees with Francke's Guge and Rudok and adds Japanese and
French scholarship against Watters' "mythical".

**Recent scholarship.** Jettmar (1993, p. 108), after Thomas (1935), equates the Land of Gold with the Tibetan
Gser-rigs, one of the women-ruled kingdoms, separates it from the women's state of SE Tibet, and links the "Gold Race"
of the Khotan prophecy with gold-washing in the valleys of former Great Palur (Baltistan); he argues no location
beyond Thomas.

**Assessment.** Atkinson anticipates Mizutani's position that the kingdom was real, from the same Chinese annals.
Francke independently narrows the location to Guge and Rudok, which lie within Atkinson's Nari-khorsum and fit
the text's borders. This strengthens Mizutani against Watters. The archaeology is only an analogy of custom, and
Francke's Brahmapura in the upper Ravi does not match Mizutani's Garhwal.

### 04.12 瞿毘霜那國 (Goviṣāṇa) — Kashipur (Ujain fort, Bhim-gada mound); excavation shows a long-lived town with a temple of Xuanzang's age, but no Buddhist remains

**Identifications.** Mizutani: ruins 1 mile east of Kashipur (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 409, 412) and Beal: the
Ujain fort near Kashipur. Majumdar (p. 704) reports Führer: the capital is Vairāṭpattan. Vivien: a compass plot
near Chandausi.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 251–55): 66 miles from Madawar; a fort c. 11,000 ft round with the southern suburb,
against the text's 12,000, the Dron Sagar tank and fourteen temple mounds. But "I feel quite unable to offer any
identification whatever of the Buddhist remains": the one solid brick mound, near Jagesar Mahadeva, is too small
for the 200-ft stupa. The survey did not return.

**Gazetteers.** Naini Tal 1904 and Himalayan III 1886 echo ASR I and add 3rd–4th-century inscribed bricks. Atkinson (1884) proposes "the ruins near
Dhikuli some 22 miles to the north of Kashipur", for a stupa and the local name Bairat, though Cunningham's
"may be allowed to stand".
Himalayan III 1886 records
Buddhist-style capitals, medallions and lions at Dhikuli, "locally identified with Bairatpatan, the capital of the
old kingdom of Govisana".

**Later excavations.** The Northern Circle dug the Bhim-gada mound at Kashipur, "identified by General Cunningham
with Kiu-pi-shwang-na", in 1965–66 (Y. D. Sharma, IAR 1965–66, p. 53) and 1970–71 (T. S. Iyengar, IAR 1970–71,
pp. 41–44): the plinth of a brick temple 29.5 m long and 6 m high with chaitya-window motifs, "on stylistic grounds
… between sixth to eighth centuries", later a pañcāyatana complex; pottery like Ahichchhatra III (AD 350–750);
and a fill running from PGW to early medieval. The excavators accept the identification without arguing it, and found no inscription.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the old fort near Ujain, 1 mile E of Kashipur, after Cunningham, with the
country taking in Kashipur, Rampur and Pilibhit (pp. 410–411). He prints Kashipur's latitude and longitude transposed.

**Name.** 霜 has the ṣ-initial (生, 13 of 13 for ṣ), so of Mizutani's two forms Goviṣāṇa fits and Govisana does not; 瞿毘
go-vi and 那 na/ṇa are regular. The form rests on the key alone, not on an attestation of the place name, and the
identification is unaffected (data/names/).

**Assessment.** Kashipur keeps the stronger measured fit, and the excavation now shows a town occupied from PGW
times through Xuanzang's century, with a large temple of about his date (he notes deva temples here). Its Buddhist
monuments are still unfound, and Dhikuli, with Buddhist sculpture and a local tradition, is unexcavated. Mizutani
stands, but on a site that nothing names.

### 04.13 堊醯掣呾羅國 (Ahicchattra) — Ramnagar; survey and gazetteers support it

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Ramnagar, west of Bareilly (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 412–14), Beal, Majumdar and
Dey: Ahichhatra or Ramnagar. Vivien: a compass plot NE of Bisauli.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 255–61) is the field basis of the identification. The place "still preserves its ancient
name as Ahichhatr". The fort is a triangle with sides of c. 5,900, 6,100 and 7,400 ft, over 3.5 miles round against
the 17–18 li, with ravines on the north and east for the "natural obstacles". Cunningham dug the ruins of some
twenty temples (the text has nine), and opened the 40-ft Chhatr mound, four of its eight satellite mounds being
stupas: "I have no doubt … as to the identity of the Chhatr mound with the Stupa of Hwen Thsang", though no trace
of the serpent tank was found.

**Gazetteers.** Bareilly 1911 and 1965 and Rohilkhand 1879 echo ASR I: the measured circuit, the name Adikot after
the Ahir Raja Adi and the cobra legend, the excavated stupa and its steatite box. Rohilkhand 1879 names the
"natural defences" as the Aril and Pairiya streams. Bareilly 1965 explains the oversized country circuit as
Harṣa's whole Ahicchatra bhukti (Banskhera plates).

**Inscriptions.** The Pabhosa cave inscriptions near Kosam (2nd/1st c. BC) are of Āṣāḍhasena "of Adhichhatra";
Führer (EI 2, pp. 240–44) equates Adhichhatra with Ramnagar. They attest the city's name and dynasty, not its site.

**Later excavations.** N. R. Banerjee's team (IAR 1964–65, p. 39) found Ochre-coloured Ware below the PGW and
revised the sequence to OCW, PGW, NBP and Kushan–Gupta, on a second, higher mound too. Nothing bears on Xuanzang's
period or his monasteries.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Ahicchattra in E Rohilkhand, with the capital at Rāmnagar near Aonla,
Bareilly district, after Cunningham and Hodgson's report of the ruins (p. 413).

**Recent scholarship.** Dimri's seasons (IAR 2007–08, pp. 106–108, 115), dug to test a ground-penetrating radar survey
(IIT Kanpur) and the settlement outside the W fortification, found a Kushan copper and pottery workshop (crucibles,
ingots) on mound ACT-V, and on ACT-VA and ACT-IV brick structures of the later Gupta and post-Gupta periods, with a
copper coin of the Pañcāla king Achyuta. On ACT-IVA, just outside the W gate, a brick platform with three chambers lay
under a collapsed brick building of moulded and carved bricks, "probably of a temple", of the later Gupta period.
Bhuvan Vikrama's season (IAR 2012–13, pp. 146–147) found settlement continuous from the OCP to the 12th century CE:
PGW circular huts and adobe walls, and a "Mitra Panchal period" (250 BCE–320 CE) of brick houses with over 150 coins.
Nothing names a monastery or the nāga-tank stūpa, but the town was occupied, and building, in Xuanzang's time.

**Assessment.** Name, measured circuit and excavation agree, and nothing counts against them. Mizutani is
strengthened.

### 04.14 毘羅刪拏國 (*Vilaṣaṇa) — Bilsar (Etah), where Cunningham himself moved from Atranji-Khera

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Bilsar or Bilsad, Etah district (agreed). Majumdar (p. 705): Bilsar; Cunningham
himself later dropped Atranji-Khera. Cunningham (1871, p. 420) and Beal (p. 201): Atranji-Khera. Vivien (p. 343):
Karsana.

**Survey.** In 1862–63 Cunningham chose Atranji-Khera without visiting it, from his servant's report of a large
mound, emending 200 li to 300 (ASR I, pp. 265–71). In ASR XI (pp. 13–21) he revises: "in 1862 I … failed to hit
the mark; but in 1877 I was more successful". At Bilsar he found two pillars inscribed by Kumāragupta (G.E. 96)
and a mound 33 ft high; the circuit, c. 1¾ miles, is close to the text's 12 li, and he marched Bilsar–Sankisa three
times to reallot the distances. A trench across the great mound gave large bricks under medieval house walls,
taken for the Asoka stupa; mounds D–H are the five deva temples, F with the inscribed toraṇa pillars.

**Gazetteers.** Etah 1876 echoes ASR I's Atranji-Khera; Etah 1911 echoes ASR XI on Bilsar, though its Atranji
article still follows ASR I.

**Excavations.** Sahni dug at site F, the toraṇa, in 1922–24. He judges Cunningham's identification
"unquestionable" (AR 1922–23, pp. 93–95): the trench into the great mound stopped a few feet down among walls of
bricks "similar to those found in Asokan monuments", so the stupa may lie lower; the monasteries show no surface
sign. He bought a Maitreya bearing the Buddhist creed from a villager. In 1923–24 (pp. 56–57) the clearance west of
the toraṇa gave Kushan rooms and a terracotta sealing with "the Buddhist formula ye dharmma", which "is enough to
establish the existence of Buddhist monuments at Bilsar".

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 4's distances and bearings takes
Karsana (Vivien 1858) here, not Mizutani's Bilsar (Bilsad), Etah district (route/README.md §6). The figures are one
kind of evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 4 takes Atranji-Khera
(Cunningham 1871; Beal 1884; Cunningham ASR I) here, not Mizutani's Bilsar (Bilsad), Etah district (route/README.md
§7).

**Text (Sixi edition).** The Korean edition (Taishō) reads 東行二百六七十里，渡殑伽河，南至毘羅刪拏國. The Song (Sixi) print, checked on
the scan, reads 南行…渡殑伽河，西南至, and so does the Life. Bilsar lies due south of Ahicchatra, so the Song and Life reading
fits; the route model uses it (route/README.md §9). The passage shown follows CBETA's 東.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji leaves the site open: Cunningham first proposed Atranjikhera and later Bilsar
(Bilasand), Etah district, and Ji finds the evidence insufficient (p. 415). He does not cite the Kumāragupta pillars
or Sahni's sealing with the Buddhist creed, which decide the question here.

**Name.** 刪 is ṣ (生) with an -n coda and 拏 is ṇa (7 of 7), so Mizutani's *Virasana / *Bhiraṣana and Julien's Viraśāna
break the key on the sibilant or the nasal; the characters give *Vi/Bhi-la/ra-ṣaṇa. Cunningham (ASR XI) records the
place's name as Bilsanḍ, with a cerebral ḍ (Minhāj's Bilsanda), which keeps the l and a retroflex nasal cluster, so
*Vilaṣaṇa fits both the characters and the modern name. Not attested in Sanskrit; low confidence for the form, but it
adds the name to Bilsar's evidence (data/names/).

**Assessment.** The survey abandoned Atranji-Khera on its own evidence. Bilsar has name, size, distance, Gupta temple
sites for the five deva temples, and now an excavated Buddhist sealing, though no inscription names the town and the
stupa and monasteries are unlocated. Mizutani's Bilsar is secure. The characters (*Vilaṣaṇa) also fit the local name
Bilsanḍ.

### 04.15 劫比他國 (Kapitha / Sāṅkāśya) — Sankisa; measured ramparts, staircase seals and Harṣa's camp at Kapitthikā settle it

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sankisa near Farrukhabad (disputed; Smith: near Pitiali, NE Etah, because of the
elephant capital). Cunningham (pp. 424–6), Vivien, Beal, Watters (p. 335) and Dey: Sankisa. Majumdar (p. 706):
reports Smith and notes that the Kanauj bearing is wrong.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 273–78): ramparts 18,900 ft round against the text's 20 li, old coins, and the Nāga tank
Kandaiya Tal SE of the ruins, still offered milk in Vaiśākha. ASR XI (pp. 31–37) adds Pakna-Bihar, 6 miles E: a great monastery mound with an
Indo-Scythian pedestal inscription, many clay seals with the Buddhist creed, and seals showing three staircases
(lettered Bra, Bu, Sa) and one reading "Devārāma Saṅghasa".

**Gazetteers.** Etah 1876 and Farrukhabad 1911 echo ASR I (four passages).

**Inscriptions.** Harṣa's Madhuban plate of year 25 (AD 631) was issued from the camp at Kapitthikā. Kielhorn
(EI 7, p. 137), correcting Bühler's "Pinthika", takes it to be "the Kie-pi-tha (Kapittha) of Hiuen-Tsang", that
is Saṅkāśya = Sankisa. This attests Xuanzang's name for the place in his own decade, though not its site.

**Later excavations.** B. R. Mani's stratified digs (IAR 1995–96, pp. 89–91; 1996–97, pp. 138–42) found mounds
over a periphery of c. 6 km within an earthen rampart, a PGW-to-Gupta sequence, and W of the elephant-capital
site a room in three phases "part of a monastery which seems to have existed from Maurya to Gupta period". No
inscription naming Saṅkāśya was found.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Sankisa village, Farrukhabad district, after Cunningham; he reports V.
Smith's site near Patiali in NE Etah as given without argument (p. 417).

**Assessment.** Name, measured circuit and the living Nāga cult were already independent of the disputed pillar.
Seals of the three staircases from a monastery next door show that the descent was venerated here, and the
excavations confirm a walled town with a Maurya–Gupta monastery. Kapitthikā in Harṣa's plate shows that the name
Xuanzang used was current in 631. This answers Smith's objection to the town. The Kanauj distance still needs
emendation.

### 04.15.02 三寶階 (Devāvatāra) — at Sankisa, by the Bisari Devi mound, where the pillar base was excavated

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sankisa (disputed; Smith rejects it because of the lion/elephant capital).
Cunningham (p. 425): the central mound, with Paor-Kheria as "steps village". Beal: Sankisa. Majumdar reports
Smith. Dey: the Bisari Devi temple.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 276–78) argues that the pilgrims mistook the elephant capital for a lion, estimates a pillar
of c. 52 ft, and puts the staircases at the Bisari Devi temple, though digging found no pillar base. ASR XI (p. 33)
reports that Cunningham later found it: the brick base (11 ft 9 in × 10 ft 2 in), 50–60 ft from the capital, due N
of the Bisari Devi mound and in line with its east wall, with a thick monastery wall east of the pillar. The steps
"were almost certainly on the old mound now dedicated to Bisari Devi". The Pakna-Bihar staircase seals (04.15)
belong here too.

**Gazetteers.** Farrukhabad 1911 (13 passages, two scans) echoes ASR I at length, including the failure to find a
pillar base, and does not report the excavation in ASR XI.

**Later excavations.** B. R. Mani (IAR 1995–96, pp. 89–91) describes "another mound of solid brick-work, supposed
to be a stupa, topped by a late medieval temple of Bisharidevi", 300 m S of the village, as "the place where Buddha
… descended from the trayastrimsa heaven". In 1996–97 (pp. 138–42) a room W of the elephant-capital site, in three
phases with reused pre-Kushan bricks, seemed part of a Maurya–Gupta monastery, and trenches against the "so-called
Stupa mound", disturbed by Cunningham's digging, showed "some important construction in the close vicinity".
Nothing of the staircases or of Xuanzang's period was reported.

**Text.** The monastery with the stairs is 城西二十餘里 in the Taishō, which follows the Korean edition. The Song, Yuan and
Ming editions and two Japanese manuscripts read 東 (Taishō apparatus, n. 0893013), and so does the Life (城東二十餘里). East
has the stronger support, which matters for placing the monastery relative to the Sankisa mound.

**Assessment.** A pillar standing beside a monastery mound in the position the text gives fixes the spot better
than Cunningham's 1871 central mound, and the new digs confirm a monastery by the pillar. The animal on the capital
remains unexplained, so Smith's point is not answered, but it no longer outweighs the site evidence. Mizutani is
strengthened.

### 05.01 羯若鞠闍國 (Skt. Kanyakubja) — Kanauj; survey and gazetteers support Mizutani's reading of the river

**Identifications.** All authorities: Kanauj. Mizutani: Xuanzang's "Ganges on the west" is the Kālī. Cunningham
(pp. 430–4) emends the direction and distance from Sankisa.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 279–92): the old city ran c. 3 miles from Haji Harmayan to Makarandnagar, matching the
20 × 4–5 li; the Chota Ganga, now carrying the Kālī Nadī, is the old Ganges channel, so the pilgrims' Ganges "is
confirmed, not only by the traditions of the people, but also by the fact that the old channel still exists". But
Cunningham "failed in my search" for the great stupa SE of the town and could not identify the monuments.

**Gazetteers.** Farukhabad and Agra 1884 and Farrukhabad 1911 (four passages) echo ASR I: "Old Kanauj must have
stretched for over three miles along the old cliff of the Ganges", which descends to the Kali Nadi, and "Buddhist
coins and remains are curiously wanting at Kanauj".

**Inscriptions.** Editors of later grants take Kanyakubja for Kanauj as a matter of course (Hirananda Sastri, EI 19;
Sircar, EI 32–34). Hirananda Sastri notes that the Barah plate (V.S. 893) names the Mahodaya camp and Kanyakubja
separately, so Mahodaya is not always a synonym. Bhandarkar (CII 3, pp. 206–7) cites Xuanzang's Kusumapura as an
old name of Kanauj.

**Later excavations.** K. K. Sinha (IAR 1955–56, pp. 19–22) cut "the mound representing the ancient city of
Kanyakubja": a 40-ft deposit from PGW to late medieval, PGW under NBP.

**Text (Sixi edition).** The Song (Sixi) print and the Life read 西北行減二百里 from Kapitha; the Korean edition (Taishō)
reads 東南. Kanauj lies east-south-east of Sankisa, so the Korean reading is the right one here (route/README.md §9).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Kanauj, at the confluence of the Ganges and the Kālī (p. 424).

**Assessment.** The old Ganges channel below the city supports Mizutani's reading of the river, and the city
mound has a continuous sequence through Xuanzang's time. The city is secure; its monuments are not identified.

### 05.01.09 納縛提婆矩羅城 (Skt. Navadevakula) — Newal near Bangarmau; Cunningham abandoned Nohbatganj on visiting it

**Identifications.** Mizutani gives both views without choosing (disputed). St. Martin, Cunningham (1871) and
Watters: Nohbatganj. Cunningham (p. 439) also suggests the city was washed away, or Deokali. Majumdar (pp. 707–8,
after Hoey) and Dey (after Führer): Nawal, just north of Bangarmau, c. 18–19 miles SE of Kanauj.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 293–95) has the 1871 view: the city was near Nohbatganj and swept away by the Ganges. In ASR
XI (pp. 48–52) Cunningham traced the old Ganges bed (the Kalyani Nadi) on the Oudh survey maps and visited it: "I
was much pleased to find my conjecture verified in the ruined mounds of Newal, just 2 miles to the north of
Bangarmau". Newal is 19 miles from Kanauj (Faxian 21, Xuanzang 17), the name keeps the first and last syllables,
and the mounds have bricks 15 × 9 in, Viṣṇu and Buddha heads and a Brahmanical temple. He assigns the monasteries
and the 100-ft stupa to the old fort of Bangarmau and the 200-ft stupa to the site of the Sayid's tomb.

**Gazetteers.** Unao 1903 ("It is agreed by most writers that the old site called Nawal … is the Navadevakula")
echoes ASR XI. Oudh A–G 1877, checking bearings on the map, says it "will probably be Bangarmau". Shahjahanpur,
Moradabad 1883 repeats the superseded Naubatganj.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees with the note: Newal, N of Bangarmau, 35 km SE of Kanauj, and the same place
as Faxian's village of 呵梨; he rejects Adachi's reading of the name as Jīvaka-deva-kula, a Hārītī village, and reports
St. Martin's Nobhatgang (p. 447).

**Assessment.** Name, distance, the old river bed and Buddhist sculpture point to Newal–Bangarmau, and the only
rival was given up by its own author. This should replace Mizutani's undecided entry.

### 05.02 阿踰陀國 (Skt. Ayodhyā) — on the Ganges below Bangarmau, in Mizutani's Fatehpur stretch; the site is not found

**Identifications.** Mizutani prefers Führer's Aphui, 29 miles SE of Fatehpur, as the best fit for the itinerary
(disputed). Vivien (p. 351), Watters (p. 354, reading the Ganges as a tributary) and Dey: Ayodhyā/Oudh.
Cunningham (1871, pp. 439–41) and Beal: Kakupur near Cawnpore, rejecting Ayodhyā. Majumdar lists
Kakupur, Patanvihar, Asni and Aphui. CII III: on the Sarayū near Fyzabad.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 293–98): Kakupur, not visited, reading 60 li for 600. ASR XI (pp. 55–57), with
Navadevakula fixed at Newal: the pilgrim crossed the Ganges southward, so the city is Kutia Khas or Gunir NW of
Fatehpur, or Patan-Bihar, with its "eminently Buddhist name", if the river has moved. He leaves it undecided. ASR XVII (pp. 99–100):
Asni has no Buddhist remains. Throughout he keeps Ajodhya for Viśākha (05.06).

**Gazetteers.** Oudh A–G 1877 argues for Ajodhya: the latitudes make the SE bearing right, the road is Faxian's,
and Ramkot, Swargadwari, Maniparbat and Guptar Bagh are matched to the monuments. Fyzabad
1905 finds Ajodhya "distinctly wanting in remains" of the Buddhist period. Fatehpur 1916 echoes ASR XVII on Asni
and finds Gunir's mounds "formed solely by the action of the ravines". Aphui itself is not discussed.

**Later finds.** K. D. Bajpai (IAR 1970–71, p. 63) publishes a "copper coin of the city of Ayodhya" found at
Ayodhya, with the legend Ajudhe in Brahmi of the 1st century BC. The name Ayodhyā was thus attached to the Faizabad
city some seven centuries before Xuanzang; nothing shows whether it was his Ayodhyā.

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 5's distances and bearings takes
Kutia-Gunir NW of Fatehpur (Cunningham ASR XI) here, not Mizutani's Aphui, 29 miles SE of Fatehpur (route/README.md
§6). The figures are one kind of evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 5 takes Kutia-Gunir NW of
Fatehpur (Cunningham ASR XI) here, not Mizutani's Aphui, 29 miles SE of Fatehpur (route/README.md §7).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji leaves the site open: he reports St. Martin's and Watters' Oudh, Cunningham's Kakupur
(reading 60 li for 600) and Führer's Aphui, 29 miles SE of Fatehpur, followed by Smith and Hori, and asks for further
research (pp. 449–450).

**Name.** No character renders the -y- of Ayodhyā (阿踰陀 a-yo/yu-dha), so Xuanzang heard Ayodhā or Ayudhā, the first of
Mizutani's forms, not the Sanskrit Ayodhyā. Bajpai's coin from Ayodhya with the legend Ajudhe shows the same form
without y at Faizabad in the 1st century BC. It does not locate Xuanzang's city, since any Ayodhyā would take this
form in the vernacular (data/names/).

**Assessment.** The text's crossing of the Ganges to the south, and Ajodhya's lack of Buddhist remains, count
against Oudh; Cunningham's field reasoning converges on the Fatehpur stretch where Mizutani puts Aphui. But none
of the sites examined there has Buddhist remains, so the city is unlocated, and the Ajudhe coin keeps Ajodhya in
play.

### 05.03 阿耶穆佉國 (Ayamukha / Hayamukha) — the Partabgarh–Rae Bareli area; capital perhaps Tusaran Bihar

**Identifications.** Mizutani: unknown, roughly the Rae Bareli–Pratapgarh area NW of Allahabad (uncertain).
Majumdar (p. 708, after Smith): the Partabgarh and Rae Bareli districts, city Tusaran Bihar (Cunningham, ASR XI).
Cunningham (1871, pp. 443–4) and Dey: Daundia Khera. Vivien (p. 351): north of the Sarayū. Hoey (per Fatehpur
1916): Asni.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 295–98) proposed Daundia Khera only as the corollary of Kakupur and admitted it "must still
remain doubtful": a citadel 385 ft square, never 20 li round, no Buddhist remains. In ASR XI Cunningham found
nothing there older than a few centuries (p. 57) and proposed Tusaran Bihar (pp. 63–69): the old town of Bihar in
Bela (Partabgarh) district, on the north bank of an old Ganges bed, c. 30 miles NW of Allahabad. His trench in the Tusaran
mound gave bricks with rounded faces "like the bars of a Buddhist railing", and two small stone stupas came from
the town. Dalmau to Bihar is 50 miles, the Life's 300 li.

**Gazetteers.** Oudh H–M 1877: "Manikpur must have been in the kingdom of Aya Mukhi; at any rate it was not under
Kanauj." Oudh A–G 1877 rejects Daundia Khera, founded "about the 12th century", as ASR XI also found. Fatehpur 1916
reports Hoey's Asni but finds no architectural remains there.

**Inscriptions.** The Kahla plates of Sodhadeva record that a Kalachuri prince of Kālañjara "raided and conquered
Ayomukha"; Mirashi (EI 27) dates the event c. 700 and glosses it "modern Partabgarh and Rai Bareli Districts".
The country's name is thus attested within a lifetime of Xuanzang, as Ayomukha (Cunningham's form); the location
is the editor's, not the plates'.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: roughly the Rae Bareli–Pratapgarh area NW of Allahabad, the exact site
unknown; Cunningham's Daundia-khera is "mere conjecture" (pp. 457–458). He does not mention Tusaran Bihar.

**Name.** 阿 has the zero initial (影) and never renders h, so Julien's Hayamukha does not fit; 耶 is ya (麻 rhyme) and
never yo (that would need 踰 or 喻), so Cunningham's Ayomukha does not fit either. The characters give *Ayamukha, which
is unattested, while Ayomukha is attested (Mahābhārata; ayomukhī in the Kāśikā) and is the form of the Kahla plates'
country of c. 700. A Middle Indic aya- "iron" for Sanskrit ayas- could account for Xuanzang's form as the vernacular
of Ayomukha; that is a suggestion, not an attestation. Low confidence for the form; the location does not depend on it
(data/names/).

**Assessment.** Daundia Khera is eliminated by its own proposer. Survey and gazetteers put the kingdom on the
Ganges in Partabgarh, inside Mizutani's area, and Bihar has a Buddhist name and railing-type bricks. The Kahla
plates confirm that Ayomukha was a real country within raiding reach of Kālañjara about 700. Mizutani's region is
strengthened; Tusaran Bihar is a reasonable but unexcavated capital.

### 05.04 鉢邏耶伽國 (Skt. Prayāga) — Allahabad; the gazetteers argue that the confluence has shifted

**Identifications.** All authorities: Allahabad. Cunningham (pp. 445–6): the 7th-century city lay round the
Akshay Bat, inside the fort.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 296–98) repeats this view and adds nothing on the city's extent.

**Gazetteers.** Muttra, Allahabad, Fatehpur 1884 identifies the "celebrated temple" with Patalpuri because of the
tree in its court. Allahabad 1911 argues: "if the latter was the Patalpuri temple … the confluence of the rivers
was some distance further east than at present".

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Allahabad, at the confluence of the Ganges and the Yamunā (pp. 459–460).

**Assessment.** The site is secure. The gazetteers add the shifting-confluence argument, which matters for
05.04.03–04.

### 05.04.03 天祠大樹 (the great tree of the Deva temple) — the Akshaya Vata at Patalpuri, with one gazetteer doubting

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the banyan in a riverside temple at Allahabad, its successor still venerated
(agreed). Cunningham, Beal, Majumdar and Dey: the Akshaya Vata in the underground Patalpuri temple in the fort.

**Survey.** ASR I (p. 297) places the Akshay Bat underground in a pillared court east of the Ellenborough Barracks,
due north of the Asoka pillar: "there can be little doubt that the famous tree … is the well known Akshay Bat".

**Gazetteers.** Allahabad 1968 and Muttra 1884 repeat this, noting the tradition of a reservoir where devotees
drowned themselves. Allahabad 1911 doubts: "the question is obscured by the uncertainty of identifying the temple
referred to by the Chinese writer with that still in existence". The tree "which in Hiuen Tsang's day stood in
the middle of Prayag … was in later times actually on the river bank". The temple is now underground because the
fort raised the ground level.

**Assessment.** Mizutani's identification stands. The 1911 gazetteer's reservations concern continuity, not a
rival site.

### 05.05 憍賞彌國 (Kauśāmbī) — Kosam; inscriptions naming Kauśāmbī at and around Kosam, and Sharma's excavations, settle it

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Kosam, confirmed by Sharma's excavations (agreed). Cunningham, Beal and Dey: Kosam.
Watters (p. 367) rejects it; Majumdar reports Rewah, Bharhut and Banda.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 303–12): the SW bearing from Prayāga points to the Jumna; the place is still Kosambi-nagar
to the Jains; the ramparts, 23,100 ft round with the lost SW angle restored, fit the 30 li. The gazetteers echo
him.

**Excavations.** Sahni found at Meohar, 7 miles off, an inscription (V.S. 1245) placing it "in the district of
Kauśāmbī" (AR 1921–22, pp. 120–21), and read the Kara inscription (V.S. 1093) as putting Paras, 30 miles NNW, "in
the kingdom of Kausambi": Smith's Bharhut "must now be finally abandoned" (AR 1923–24, pp. 122–24).

**Inscriptions.** On the Kosam pillar itself a record of V.S. 1621 (AD 1564/65) names the place Kauśāmbī, and two
records of the late 7th/8th century show the pillar standing in or just after Xuanzang's day (Pargiter, EI 11,
pp. 88–89). Sahni (EI 18) adds an inscription of Jayachchandra's time that "furnishes final corroboration". The
Allahabad pillar, with Aśoka's edict to the mahāmātras of Kosambi, stood originally at Kauśāmbī (Hultzsch, CII 1,
pp. 159–60; Bhandarkar, CII 3, p. 35).

**Later excavations.** G. R. Sharma (Allahabad University, IAR 1953–54 to 1966–67) traced the pre-NBP rampart, 3½
miles round, excavated the Ghoṣitārāma (05.05.03), and in the SW corner on the Jumna found a stone palace
(315 × 150 m) of "circa 600 B.C., if not earlier", "not unlikely … occupied by king Udayana" (IAR 1960–61,
pp. 33–34); its last building phase is of the 1st–2nd century AD (IAR 1963–64, p. 40).

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 5's distances and bearings takes Rewah
(Vost (via Majumdar 1924)) here, not Mizutani's Kosam (Kausambi) (route/README.md §6). The figures are one kind of
evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 5 takes Rewah (Vost (via
Majumdar 1924)) here, not Mizutani's Kosam (Kausambi) (route/README.md §7).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Kosam, on the N bank of the Yamunā, confirmed by G. R. Sharma's Excavations
at Kauśāmbī 1957–59 (a rampart going back to the 7th century BC, the Aśokan pillar), with two seals of Toramāṇa from
the Ghoṣitārāma, whose destruction he puts in the early 6th century (pp. 467–468, citing U. Thakur, The Hūṇas in
India, 1967). He reports and rejects V. Smith's site near Satna and Watters' near Śrāvastī.

**Assessment.** Inscriptions on the site and in its district name Kauśāmbī from the 11th to the 16th century, the
ramparts fit the 30 li, and the excavated city is of the right size and age. Kosam is settled. Sharma's palace is a
candidate for Xuanzang's "old palace" with Udayana's image, though nothing later than the 1st–2nd century AD is
reported there.

### 05.05.03 具史羅長者故宅 (Ghoṣila's house and monastery) — the excavated Ghoṣitārāma at Kosam, whose Main Stupa has an Aśokan enlargement; Cunningham's Gopsahsa rests on a village name

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the monastery taken for Ghoṣila's, found by excavation north of the east gate of
the Kosam rampart (agreed). Beal and Watters: the SE angle of the city, no site.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 311–15) puts Ghoṣila's garden at Gopsahsa, a village SE of the fort, and the monastery with
the Asoka stupa at Kosam Khiraj, where railing pillars are built into house walls; ASR XXI (pp. 1–3) adds
Vasubandhu's chamber in the Tikri mound. No stupa was traced.

**Gazetteers.** No passages on this site.

**Later excavations.** G. R. Sharma (Allahabad University) excavated the Ghoṣitārāma, "identified 1951", from
1953 to 1957, beside the city's east gateway (IAR 1953–54, pp. 9–10; 1954–55, pp. 16–19; 1955–56, pp. 19–22;
1956–57, pp. 28–29): a massive square Main Stupa, small stupas, a Hārītī shrine, a south boundary wall of 181 ft,
an inscription of the nun Buddhamitrā of Kaniṣka's reign, Bhadramagha's name on a jar, a standing Buddha and
railing pillars. Occupation ran in sixteen phases from c. 6th century BC to the 6th century AD, ending in
destruction by the Hūṇas under Toramāṇa. The Main Stupa, begun c. 5th century BC, received its largest addition in
the 3rd century BC, and the excavator recalls "that Hiuen Tsang credits Asoka with the construction of a stupa at
Ghoshitarama" (IAR 1955–56, p. 19). The coded reports do not quote the record that gave the name.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the site found N of the E gate of Kosam, after Mizutani (p. 470).

**Assessment.** An extensive monastery at Kosam with an Aśokan-stage great stupa, flourishing from the Kushans to
the Guptas and wrecked c. 500, fits Xuanzang's old monastery with Aśoka's 200-ft stupa, seen among Kauśāmbī's
dilapidated monasteries, though its place by the east gate matches the text's "not far to the southeast" only
loosely. Gopsahsa and the Tikri mound, which rest on a village name and surface material, are
superseded. Mizutani's identification is strengthened.

### 05.05.06 迦奢布羅城 (*Kāśapura) — open; Sultanpur is weak, and Partabgarh 1904's Bihar is Cunningham's Hayamukha site

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sultanpur on the Gomati, after Cunningham (uncertain; Mizutani doubts that
Xuanzang went there). Cunningham (pp. 457–8), Beal and Dey: Sultanpur (Kuśabhavanapura). Vivien: near the latitude
of Lucknow. Majumdar lists Jhusi (Vost) and Mohanlalganj (Smith). Watters: none.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 312–15): Kosam to Sultanpur by the Papamau ghat is 109 miles, the 700 li, and Sultanpur to
Ajudhya 30 miles, the next stage's 170–180 li. But Cunningham found no stupa at Sultanpur, only a report of an old
tope near Mahmudpur 5 miles NW; the fort mound is 750 ft square and the town c. 2 miles round (the text's 10 li).
The survey never returned to it.

**Gazetteers.** Sultanpur 1903 rejects its own town (four passages): "Nothing has ever been discovered at Mahmudpur
or the old site of Sultanpur"; kia renders ka, "never ku", so the name is Kāśipura; and Cunningham's 60 miles is
really 54–55. Partabgarh 1904 proposes Bihar in tahsil Kunda, "the same word as vihara", with ancient mounds,
"carved stones and ornamental bricks of Buddhist date", and an adjoining village "Silawatpur alias Kashipur".
Allahabad 1911 and 1968 report Jhusi (Kesi). Lucknow 1904 reports Mohanlalganj. Oudh 1877 suggests Kikori, Bari or
Misrikh.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees with Cunningham's Sultānpur on the Gomatī (old Kuśabhavanapura) only roughly:
Xuanzang did not go there, and the exact site needs research (p. 474).

**Assessment.** Partabgarh's Bihar is evidently the town Cunningham described in ASR XI as Tusaran Bihar and proposed
for Hayamukha (05.03), so the same remains are claimed for two places. It lies c. 30 miles NW of Allahabad, far short
of 700 li from Kauśāmbī. Sultanpur keeps the distance but has no remains and a weak name. Mizutani is weakened, with
no better candidate. The key removes the name argument as well: 迦 is never ku, so Xuanzang's Kāśapura excludes
Cunningham's Kuśabhavanapura, as it does the gazetteer's Kāśipura (data/names/).

### 05.06 鞞索迦國 (*Viṣaka) — unidentified; Ajodhya (Sāketa) is the only argued candidate, and its monuments are disputed

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no decision (disputed). Cunningham (1871, pp. 459–64) and Beal: Sāketa = Faxian's
Sha-chi = Ayodhyā. Watters (p. 374): perhaps Sāketa, but not Sha-chi. Vivien (p. 354): Bisvah or Biseipur. Smith:
Nimkhar. Führer: Sanchankot (Unao). Hoey: Pasha (Gonda). Burgess: Lucknow.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 317–28) sets out the case: Faxian's Sha-chi has the same tooth-brush legend; Buddha's
years at Viśākha match his years at Sāketa; coins of Dhanadeva and Viśākhadatta are found only at Ajudhya; and
Ajudhya lies 30 miles (170–180 li) north of Sultanpur. On the ground: the Sugriv Parbat (500 × 300 ft, with a well
and cloistered rooms) as the great monastery, the 65-ft Mani Parbat as the Asoka stupa, the Kuber Parbat as the
hair-and-nail stupa, and a mahwa tree the coolies called a Raja's tooth-brush. ASR XI (p. 68) and XVII (p. 100)
keep this view.

**Gazetteers.** Oudh A–G 1877 doubts Cunningham's Ajodhya monuments: two of Ramkot's largest bastions "are called
to this day Sugriva, and Kabir tila", so his mounds have no Buddhist tradition, and the tooth-brush tree was
"wrongly … identified with Ajodhya". It offers Satrikh or Hargam but concludes that "it is really impossible to
identify … Visakha". Fyzabad 1905: "the whole question is still uncertain". Faizabad 1960: Pi-sho-ka = Sha-chi = the
Buddhist Sāketa, "but [it] was not identical with our Ayodhya". Unao 1903 reports Sujankot; Lucknow 1904 Mahona.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji reports Cunningham's Sāketa in Oudh, Watters' objection, St. Martin's Biseipur and V.
Smith's Nimkhar on the Gomatī, 20 miles S of Sitapur, and accepts none (p. 476).

**Name.** Xuanzang's own fanqie 山格反 gives 索 the ṣ-initial (生), not s, and 迦 is unaspirated k (the repeat 索伽國 has g),
so the characters give *Viṣaka or *Veṣaga; Viśākha fails on both the sibilant (ś would be 書) and the aspirate (kh is
佉). No *Viṣaka is attested in the Buddhist, epic, Purāṇic or inscriptional texts searched. Low confidence; the name no
longer supports the link with Viśākhā, and through her with Sāketa (data/names/).

**Assessment.** The survey gives Ajodhya its best case (coins and legend), but the distance rests on Sultanpur
(05.05.06), and the mounds are disputed and unexcavated. Part of the case rested on the name: the Viśākhā link and the
Viśākhadatta coins assume a Viśākha- name, which Xuanzang's characters (*Viṣaka) do not give. If Ayodhyā (05.02) lay
on the Ganges, Ajodhya is free for this country, but little beyond Faxian's tooth-brush legend now ties it here.
Mizutani's agnosticism stands; Ajodhya remains the only argued candidate, weaker than before.
