# Notes on disputed places: juan 4–5

Places in juan 4–5 that meet at least one of the three criteria in `NOTES_INSTRUCTIONS.md`, in master-table order
(34 notes), with the evidence of the Archaeological Survey reports. Gazetteer volumes are cited as `district year`.
Survey reports are cited as ASR with volume and page, and are Cunningham's: I (1862–63), II (1863–65), III
(1871–72), V (1872–73), IX, XI (1875–78), XIV (1878–79), XVII (1881–82), XX (1882–83), XXI (1883–85). "Atkinson"
means the Himalayan Districts text, which exists as *Himalayan districts II 1884* and in the Cornell scan catalogued
as *unidentified 1874* (cu31924023947751). The two have the same wording and are counted as one text. Checked but
not noted, because the authorities agree, no gazetteer argues and the survey only confirms Mizutani: 04.02.03
(Tamasavana = Sultanpur; ASR XIV, pp. 55–59, measured the Badshahi serai on the old mound and suspected it stands on
the monastery's foundations), 04.06 (Pāriyātra = Bairāt; ASR II, pp. 242–48: the mound "corresponds almost exactly"
to the 14–15 li, and the Bijak hill ruins are taken for two of the monasteries; Dey's entry describes the mountain
range, not a different site), 05.04.04 (the Great Giving Field at the confluence; ASR I, p. 298, only notes that
the sandy plain has been eroded).

### 04.01 磔迦國 (Skt. Takka) — the country is the central Punjab; the capital is still open (Asarur or near Sialkot)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Punjab plain between the Beas and the text's "Indus" (taken to be the Chenāb);
capital unnamed, perhaps "new Śākala" (uncertain). Cunningham (1871, pp. 170, 207–8): the plains from the Indus to
the Beas; the capital Taki is the ruins of Asarur, 19 miles NE of Sangala. Majumdar (1924, p. 687): with Śākala at
Sialkot, the capital belongs c. 2 miles NE of Sialkot, not at Asarur. Vivien (p. 328): the Ravi–Beas doab, capital
near Amritsar. Beal (p. 165): Chenab/Ravi region. Watters (p. 291): no site. Dey: Madra/Takkadeśa between the
Ravi and Chenab.

**Survey.** Cunningham found Asarur in November 1863 (ASR II, pp. 191–203): ruins 15,600 ft round, which "agrees
sufficiently well" with the 20-li circuit, moulded and wedge-shaped bricks, and the Sodra mound near Thatta Sayyadan
for the Asoka stupa. The distance from Sangala (16 miles direct) does not fit the text's 15 li, so he reads 115 li.
Later volumes add only texts: the Takiya of the Chach-nāma (ASR V, p. 113, with moulded bricks from Asarur) and the
Thakkiya of the Rājataraṅgiṇī, "close to Sangala" (ASR IX, pp. 84, 103). The survey's view never moved.

**Gazetteers.** Gujranwala 1883 echoes ASR II at length: the circuit "≈ 20 li", moulded bricks, a 21-ft well, the
Sodra mound and the Ransi mound with its Nao-gaja tomb. Jhang 1884, Lyallpur 1905 and Punjab 1919 repeat the same
view. None of them argues independently.

**Assessment.** The survey shows that a large early town lay at Asarur and that Takka was a real district name, but
its placing of the capital still depends on Cunningham's Sangala (Sanglawala Tibba) and on an emended distance. If
Śākala is Sialkot, as Mizutani holds, Asarur loses its anchor and Majumdar's site near Sialkot follows. Mizutani's
tentative picture stands.

### 04.01.02 奢羯羅故城 (Skt. Śākala) — Sialkot (Mizutani) against Sanglawala Tibba (Cunningham); the gazetteers only repeat Cunningham

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sialkot (disputed). Majumdar (p. 686) and Dey: Sialkot, rejecting Sangala as a
different place. Cunningham (p. 206) and Beal (p. 166): Sanglawala Tibba, west of the Ravi. Vivien (p. 329): near
Amritsar. Watters (p. 288): uncertain.

**Survey.** Cunningham surveyed the Sangala hill in 1863 (ASR II, pp. 37–38, 191–99): a rocky triangle, its north
side 215 ft high, brick ruins c. 1.5 miles round, bricks 15 × 9 × 3 in. He put the Asoka stupa on the Mundapapura
ridge to the NW, read the robbers' forest as the Pilu woods he walked through, and the Āpagā as the Ayak Nadi.
Against the classical writers who put Sangala east of the Ravi, he holds that "the detailed measurements of Hwen
Thsang are too precise … and the coincidence of name is too exact to be set aside" (p. 199). He did not examine
Sialkot, and the view never moved (ASR IX, p. 103).

**Gazetteers.** Jhang 1884, Lyallpur 1905 and Gujranwala 1883 (19 passages, three volumes) all repeat Cunningham.
Their local detail is the small rocky hill with brick-covered ridges and "hills of iron-bearing grey rock", which
they read as matching Xuanzang's note on iron. Gujranwala adds Cunningham's claim that Ransi = Nara-Sinha confirms
the identification.

**Assessment.** The survey measured the hill but found nothing that names it; the case is still name and
itinerary, and the ruins (1.5 miles round) are small for a great city. Neither survey nor gazetteers weaken
Mizutani's Sialkot.

### 04.02 至那僕底國 (Skt. Cīnabhukti) — Chiniyari (Chine), as Mizutani holds; Cunningham himself abandoned Patti

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Chiniyari, on the Amritsar–Sialkot road (disputed). Majumdar (p. 687): Chine or
Chiniyari, tentatively. Cunningham (1871, pp. 230, 232) and Beal (p. 173): Patti (Haibatpur-Patti), 27 miles NE of
Kasur. Watters (p. 293): Patti "need not be seriously considered". Vivien (p. 332): the Katoch country (Kangra).
Smith (per Mizutani): Ferozpore.

**Survey.** In 1878–79 Cunningham gave up Patti (ASR XIV, pp. iv, 53–54). He visited "the lofty mound of China, 11
miles from Amritsar, which, I have no doubt, is the Chinapati of Hwen Thsang": the villages Unchakila, Bichlakila
and Shahbazpur on the road to Sialkot, a central mound 400 ft square and 46–50 ft high with a lower mound twice its
size on the north, large bricks (12.5 × 8.5 × 2.5 in), and Mirza Mughal Beg's "Chiniyari, also called Alexander's
mound". ASR XXI (p. 134) adds that a brick contractor digging the mound found a very old Viṣṇu image.

**Gazetteers.** Lahore 1883 (two scans) argues for Patti with the reasoning of the *Ancient Geography*: Lahore is
the "great Brahmanical city" on Takka's eastern border, and "midway between the two cities [Lahore and Jullundur]
is a village called Patti, to this day". It does not know the survey's revision.

**Assessment.** The only argument for Patti was the name, and its author withdrew it after seeing a large ancient
mound at Chine that keeps the name and lies on the right road. Mizutani's Chiniyari is strengthened, though nothing
found there is specifically Buddhist.

### 04.03 闍爛達羅國 (Skt. Jālaṃdhara) — Jalandhar; the gazetteers argue only about how far the kingdom extended

**Identifications.** All authorities: Jalandhar. Cunningham (pp. 156–7) and Majumdar (p. 683): Trigarta, which
included the hills as far as Kangra.

**Gazetteers.** Kangra 1883 argues that the kingdom covered "the low hills from the Ravi to Simla" and that the
eleven hill principalities did not yet exist separately. Hoshiarpur 1884 and 1905 and Jalandhar 1884 and 1908
convert the text's dimensions to miles. Jalandhar 1884 adds that the old city was a little over 2 miles round and
that only two tanks (Gufa, Brahmkund) remain.

**Assessment.** The site is not in question. The gazetteers support Mizutani and Cunningham and add the
Kangra-hills extent.

### 04.04 屈露多國 (*Kulūta) — Kulu; Atkinson offers Jagatsukh (Nast) as the old capital

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Kulu district of the upper Beas; old capital Nagar (agreed). Cunningham
(pp. 162–3): Kulu, old capital Makarsa or Nagar. Beal, Watters, Majumdar and Dey: Kulu.

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson (Himalayan districts II 1884) names "Nast or Jagatsukh in the upper valley of the Bias,
the ancient seat of the Pala Rajas of Kulu" and notes that "Kulu is still called Kulanta by the people". Kangra
1883 and 1899 argue that the kingdom took in Bangahal, Seoraj, Bashahr and the hill parts of Mandi and Suket,
since the 3,000-li circuit is too large for the Kulu valley alone.

**Assessment.** The country is certain, and the surviving name Kulanta strengthens the name equation. Jagatsukh is
a candidate for the capital that none of the authorities records. It rests on dynastic tradition only, against
the Nagar tradition Mizutani reports.

### 04.04a 洛護羅國 (Lāhul) — Lahul; the gazetteers repeat it and do not meet Jäschke's objection

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Lahul, name doubtful (disputed, hearsay). Cunningham (p. 164), Beal (p. 177),
Watters (p. 299), Majumdar (p. 684) and Vivien (p. 334): Lahul (Lho-yul).

**Gazetteers.** Kangra 1883 and 1899 quote Cunningham's "Lahul (Lho-yul of the Tibetans)". Atkinson mentions
"Lo-hou-lo, the modern Lahul" in passing while discussing the Amazonian kingdom. The three "argues" in
`places.tsv` come from that passage, which argues about the Amazonian kingdom, not about Lahul.

**Assessment.** The gazetteers repeat Cunningham and add nothing on the linguistic objection Mizutani reports
(Jäschke: Lāhul is the Indian, not the Tibetan, name).

### 04.04b 秣邏娑國 (Tib. mar-sa) — Ladakh; the gazetteers add nothing

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Ladakh (mar-yul) (disputed; Satō objects). Cunningham (p. 164), Beal (p. 178),
Watters (p. 299) and Majumdar (p. 684, after Francke): Ladakh. Vivien (p. 334): 三波訶 = Champaka/Chamba.

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson (1884) puts Mo-lo-so or Malasa, also called San-po-ho, "some 2,000 li … to the north of
Lo-hou-lo", while looking for the Amazonian kingdom. This is consistent with Ladakh but names no site.

**Assessment.** No gazetteer evidence bears on the Ladakh equation. Vivien's Chamba remains the only real
alternative among the authorities.

### 04.05 設多圖盧國 (Skt. Śatadru) — Sarhind, tentatively; no gazetteer evidence

**Identifications.** Mizutani: named after the Sutlej, capital Sarhind (uncertain). Cunningham (pp. 166–7, after
correcting the distance to Bairāt), Beal (p. 178) and Dey: Sarhind. Vivien (p. 336): in the Sarasvati basin,
near Sirtah west of Thanesar. Watters (p. 300): nothing is known.

**Gazetteers.** No hits. The survey reports have no passage on it either.

**Assessment.** Sarhind rests on Cunningham's emended distance alone. The question is as open as Mizutani leaves
it.

### 04.07 秣菟羅國 (Skt. Mathurā) — Mathura; the survey and the gazetteers disagree on where the old city lay

**Identifications.** All authorities: Mathura. Cunningham (1871, pp. 427–8) and Majumdar (p. 706): the town has
moved north and west as the Jumna encroached.

**Survey.** Cunningham's layout changed. In 1862–63 (ASR I, pp. 233–39) he worked from the Katra and the jail mound,
where inscriptions name a Kunda-Suka ("dry tank") monastery beside a tank, which he took for the tank where the
monkey died: "there can be no doubt of the accuracy of my identification". By 1881–83 (ASR XVII, pp. 111–12; ASR
XX, pp. 31–32) the 7th-century city itself is the Katra (Kesopura), with the Jumna then flowing in the old channel
under its walls, from "an examination of the ground, compared with Hwen Thsang's statements".

**Gazetteers.** Muttra 1911 (Führer's view) argues that "the site of ancient Muttra is clearly indicated by an
extensive elevation of the soil to the south-west". It finds Kans-ka-Tila largely natural, places the old
Buddhist city "probably … north of the present city beyond the Katra", and reads the Chaubara mounds as Buddhist
stupas. Muttra, Allahabad, Fatehpur 1884 and Mathura 1968 argue for Mahaban, on the left bank opposite, as a site
of Faxian's monasteries "on both sides of the river", from Buddhist sculpture in the fort and the Assi-Khamba
building. Mathura 1968 notes that local inscriptions name more than a dozen monasteries.

**Assessment.** The identification is secure. The survey and the gazetteers bear only on the layout, and there
they disagree: Cunningham's late Katra city, Führer's elevation to the SW, and a Buddhist Mahaban across the river.

### 04.07.03 鄔波毱多伽藍 (Upagupta's monastery) — not located; Cunningham moved it twice, and Mizutani's verdict stands

**Identifications.** Mizutani: cannot now be located (disputed). Cunningham (1871): the Yaśa Vihāra on the Katra
mound. Growse and Dey: Kankali Tila. Beal (p. 181): no mound fits the bearing. Watters (p. 308): Katra "undoubtedly
wrong", Kankali "not quite convincing".

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 233–34) and III (pp. 28–29) put the monastery at the Katra, reading the text's "east" as
west, with the monkey's tank at the jail mound. Once he took the Katra for the city itself, Cunningham moved the
monastery to "the present old fort on the bank of the Jumna, which is just about 1 mile to the east of the Katra"
(ASR XX, pp. 32–33; ASR XVII, pp. 111–12, with the Ambarikha mound for the cave hill and a tank by the Collector's
kacheri mound for the monkey's tank). The fort proposal rests on reading high ground as the pilgrim's "mountain";
nothing Buddhist was found there.

**Gazetteers.** Muttra 1884 reports Growse's Kankali Tila, with its colossal Buddha statues and Huvishka
inscription, but no foundation line. Muttra 1911 rejects the proposals: "their identifications, based on a wrong
location of the city, are inadmissible, and both the Upagupta monastery and the monkey tank near it … have yet to
be discovered". It also notes that the Katra inscription names the Yaśa Vihāra and that Kankali Tila is a Jain
stupa.

**Assessment.** Each proposal follows from a view of where the city lay, and the survey's own shift shows how
loose they are. The Katra is named epigraphically for another monastery and Kankali is Jain. The site stays
unknown, as Mizutani says.

### 04.08 薩他泥濕伐羅國 (Skt. Sthāneśvara) — Thanesar; the measured circuit supports it

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Thanesar (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 376–8), Vivien, Beal, Majumdar (p. 701) and
Dey: Thanesar. Watters (p. 315): the distances do not fit and the site should be c. 100 miles NE of Mathurā.

**Survey.** Cunningham measured the mounds of the fort, the town and Bahari at 14,000 ft round, c. 4 miles with the
hollow between them, "as nearly as possible an exact square of one mile on each side" (ASR II, pp. 220–21). He
found two ruined mounds with reddish-yellow bricks 4–5 li NW of the city, the Madrasa mound, for the Asoka stupa.

**Gazetteers.** Karnal 1976 restates the survey's case: the fort, Bahari and the town stand on "three mounds
[that] together occupy a space of about 3 miles … which closely agrees with the 20 li of Yuan Chwang", with 14-inch
fort bricks ascribed to Harṣa, and the stupa mound ¼ mile NW. Ambala 1883 and Meerut division 1875 repeat
Cunningham.

**Assessment.** The measured circuit and the stupa mound support Thanesar against Watters' objection, which rests
on distances alone. Mizutani's identification is strengthened.

### 04.08.02 福地 (Kurukṣetra "Happy Land") — Kurukshetra; the extent is read three ways, and the Asthipur mound is doubtful

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Kurukshetra battlefield, with Asthipur west of Thanesar (agreed). Cunningham
(pp. 381, 385), Beal (p. 184) and Vivien: likewise. Watters (p. 315): a 200-li circuit round Sthāneśvara, not
the Kurukshetra plain.

**Survey.** In ASR II (pp. 212–18) Cunningham reads 400 li for the text's 200, to fit the 48-kos pilgrimage
circuit, and reports that "the site of Asthipur is still pointed out in the plain to the west of the city towards
Aujasghat". In ASR XIV (pp. 87, 98) he instead takes the 200 li as the length of each side and excavated the
Asthipura mound (Madrasa Tila, 700 × 500 ft), finding a platform of unbaked bricks 364 ft long, walls, terracottas
and a head of Śiva, but no bones.

**Gazetteers.** Karnal 1884 (Ibbetson, two scans) equates the Happy Land with the Nardak, argues from a local
etymology (nir-daya against dukh), and says the 200 li "may surely be taken to mean … the radius", close to
Cunningham's later reading. Ambala 1883 repeats ASR II on the Asthipur spot. Karnal 1976 reports that the mound was
excavated (Theh Polar) and yielded 3rd–4th-century AD finds, which it says make the Mahābhārata tradition doubtful.

**Assessment.** The region is not in doubt. On the extent, both Cunningham's later reading and Ibbetson's abandon
the plain circumference. At Asthipur the survey's own excavation found a large building, not a battlefield, and the
later finds weaken it as the ancient spot.

### 04.08.04 倶昏荼僧伽藍 (*Gokaṇṭha / Govinda monastery) — unknown; Cunningham's Gunana with Karnal 1884's Sita Mai is the only candidate

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no site, only rival restorations of the name (unknown). Cunningham (p. 395,
tentative): probably Gunana, 17 miles SSW of Thanesar. Beal (p. 186) and Watters (p. 315): no site.

**Survey.** ASR II (pp. 226–27) gives the same proposal: Gunana "appears to agree very well with the recorded
position". No remains are described.

**Gazetteers.** Karnal 1884 (two scans) follows Cunningham's Gunana and suggests "it is not improbable that the
Gominda monastery" survives as the Sita Mai monastery, 4 miles from Gunana. Meerut division 1875 mentions the
monastery only in passing in its Srughna passage (see the data remarks).

**Assessment.** Gunana or Sita Mai is a tentative candidate, resting on Cunningham's reading of the route and a
surviving monastic site. It is better than nothing but far from secure.

### 04.09 窣祿勤那國 (Skt. Srughna) — Sugh near Jagadhri (Ambala district), not Kalsi

**Identifications.** Mizutani: "Sugh near Kālsī (30°32'N 77°53'E), Dehra Dun district" (agreed). Cunningham
(pp. 396, 398) and Watters: Sugh. Beal (p. 187): at or near Kalsi. Majumdar and Dey: Sugh near Kalsi, but Dey
also gives 20 miles NW of Saharanpur. Vivien: the Saharanpur sarkar.

**Survey.** The Kalsi association began with the survey. In 1862–63 Cunningham looked for the capital on the
Jumna near Paota and Topra and was "satisfied with having shown that the inscribed rock of Khalsi is situated
within the territory of Srughna" (ASR I, pp. c. 160, 246). The next season he revised this: "I found a village named
Sugh" in a bend of the old Jumna, with three forts whose circuit, 19,000 ft without the northern one, is "in accord
with the pilgrim's measurement" (ASR II, pp. 226–31). ASR XIV (p. 138) adds that coins of the Kunindas are found
"in greater numbers about Sugh … than in any other part of the country".

**Gazetteers.** Ambala 1883, Meerut division 1875 and Saharanpur 1981 place Sugh exactly, "about 5 km east of
Jagadhari in the Ambala district". Ambala 1883 echoes ASR II's 19,000 ft and records coins on the spot and at
Chaneti. Meerut 1875 adds tradition, large bricks and the later name Mandal. Dehra Dun 1911 and Atkinson's "not far
below Kalsi" are loose descriptions in the line of ASR I.

**Assessment.** Sugh is strengthened by a measured circuit and coin finds. Mizutani's coordinates and "Dehra Dun
district" are Kalsi's, some 40 miles away, and reflect Cunningham's first, abandoned view; they should be
corrected to Sugh, Ambala district. Beal's Kalsi is not supported.

### 04.09.03 殑伽河源 (the Ganges "source") — where the Ganges leaves the hills near Hardwar; no gazetteer evidence

**Identifications.** Mizutani: not the Himalayan source but where the Ganges enters the plain near Hardwar
(uncertain). Watters (p. 315): Gaṅgādvāra or Hardwar. Vivien (p. 341): Gangotri. Beal (p. 188): undecided.

**Gazetteers.** No hits.

**Assessment.** The river 3–4 li wide that Beal cites argues for the plains reading. Nothing here changes
Mizutani's view.

### 04.10 秣底補羅國 (*Matipura) — the Bijnor side of the Ganges; Mandawar as capital is plausible but unexcavated

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the region on the east bank of the Ganges opposite Srughna (disputed). Cunningham
(p. 399), Vivien (p. 343), Beal (p. 190) and Dey: Mandawar, near Bijnor. Majumdar (p. 703): Bijnor district.
Watters (p. 321): Mandawar fits neither direction nor area.

**Survey.** Cunningham reached Madawar in 1862–63 (ASR I, pp. 248–51): an old mound half a mile square and 10 ft
high with large bricks, a fort 300 ft square, a town deserted in 1114 by the Kanungo's account, and a circuit that
fits the 20 li. He placed Guṇaprabha's monastery at the Lalpur mound, Saṅghabhadra's at Hidayat Shah's shrine,
and Vimalamitra's stupa by the Birwala Tal and its mango grove. In ASR II (pp. 230–31) he halves the 800 li from
Srughna. Nothing was excavated.

**Gazetteers.** Rohilkhand division 1879 echoes ASR I's site fits. Bijnor 1908 (five passages, two scans) doubts:
"the identification is open to the usual doubts"; the site "has never been scientifically explored"; and "these
identifications are necessarily conjectural". It notes a Buddhist stupa excavated at Mordhaj fort (Najibabad)
elsewhere in the district.

**Assessment.** Survey and gazetteers agree on the Bijnor tract east of the Ganges, which is Mizutani's region.
Mandawar as capital rests on surface mounds and an emended distance. Mizutani's region is mildly strengthened, and
the capital stays open.

### 04.10.05 摩裕羅城 (Mayūra) — Mayapur at Hardwar, with good local remains; the bank remains a question

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Hardwar (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 402–4): ruined Mayapura between Hardwar and
Kankhal, now on the west bank. Beal, Majumdar and Dey: Mayapura or Hardwar. Watters (p. 328): Mayūra was on the
east side, so it is not Mayapura. Vivien (p. 347): the east bank opposite, or perhaps Amroha.

**Survey.** ASR II (pp. 232–35): the old ruined town between Hardwar and Kankhal "is still called Mayapur", and the
Brahmakund well in a small temple at the Gaṅgādvāra is "most probably" the one Xuanzang noticed.

**Gazetteers.** Meerut division 1875, Saharanpur 1921 and 1981, and Atkinson (30 passages) repeat Cunningham but
add remains. The people point out the old city "from a torrent near the Sarovanath temple to Raja Ben's fort",
7,500 ft. There are large-brick foundations, ancient sculpture and coins, and the Maya Devi, Bhairon and Narayan
Bali temples. The Brahmakund identification echoes ASR II. Saharanpur 1981 also cites Watters for Mo-yu-lo east of
the Ganga.

**Assessment.** The remains at Mayapur strengthen Hardwar as the city. Watters' objection about the bank is
answered only by Cunningham's guess that the river has shifted, which neither survey nor gazetteer tests.

### 04.11 婆羅吸摩補羅國 (Skt. Brahmapura) — Garhwal–Kumaon; Srinagar is the weakest of three capitals (Lakhanpur, Barahat, Srinagar)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Alaknanda valley, capital Srinagar (Garhwal), reading NE for N (agreed).
Vivien (p. 344): Srinagar. Cunningham (pp. 407–8): Garhwal and Kumaon, capital Vairāṭpattan or Lakhanpur on the
Ramganga; he rejects Srinagar as founded in 1358. Beal (p. 198): Garhwal and Kumaon.

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson (Himalayan districts II 1884, 23 passages across two scans and the Cornell copy) rejects
Srinagar, which "was of no importance until … the early part of the seventeenth century". He also rejects
Lakhanpur and the Barhepura ruins near Najibabad, and proposes Barahat (Uttarkashi), "an ancient and well-known
site almost exactly fifty miles due north of Hardwar". This keeps Xuanzang's "north". His evidence is the
12th-century trident inscription of Aneka Malla at Barahat (twinned at Gopeswar) and the route: the distance to
Govisana counts from Madawar. Garhwal 1910 accepts Barahat cautiously. Almora 1981 supports Lakhanpur: the
6th-century Taleshwar copperplates were "issued from the city of Brahmapura". Naini Tal 1904 includes Kumaon.

**Assessment.** The gazetteers count against Mizutani's Srinagar, which is late and which Cunningham himself
rejects. They leave two candidates. Lakhanpur has the epigraphic Brahmapura of the Taleshwar plates. Barahat
keeps the text's bearing but has only a 12th-century inscription. "Agreed" understates the dispute.

### 04.11.02 蘇伐剌拏瞿呾羅國 (Skt. Suvarṇagotra) — a real trans-Himalayan country; Atkinson puts it in Nari-khorsum

**Identifications.** Mizutani: a real Himalayan country, the Eastern Women's Kingdom of the Tangshu (disputed;
Thomas: partly Hunza-Nagar). Watters (p. 330): "undoubtedly mythical". Dey: north of Brahmapura; the Amazonian
kingdom in the trans-Himalayan Sutlej valley. Vivien and Beal: no location.

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson (14 passages) argues at length. Suvarṇagotra is the Suvarṇabhūmi of the Purāṇas, "the
Nari-khorsum district of Tibet lying to the north of Kumaon and Garhwal". There are "no indications or traditions
of any Amazonian kingdom" in the Katyur, Juhar or Niti valleys, so "we must search for it across the passes in
Tibet". From the Sui and Tang annals (the Eastern Nü ruled by a woman, absorbed by Lhasa after 742), "there is …
no need to doubt the statements of Hwen Thsang".

**Assessment.** Atkinson anticipates Mizutani's position that the kingdom was real, from the same Chinese annals.
He adds a concrete location (western Tibet, north of Kumaon and Garhwal) that fits the text's borders. This
strengthens Mizutani against Watters, but the location is general.

### 04.12 瞿毘霜那國 (Govisana) — Kashipur (Ujain fort) still fits best; Dhikuli is a live alternative

**Identifications.** Mizutani: ruins 1 mile east of Kashipur (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 409, 412) and Beal: the
Ujain fort near Kashipur. Majumdar (p. 704) reports Führer: the capital is Vairāṭpattan. Vivien: a compass plot
near Chandausi.

**Survey.** Cunningham's case is in ASR I (pp. 251–55): 44 kos (66 miles) from Madawar; a fort 3,000 × 1,600 ft,
9,000 ft round and c. 11,000 ft with the southern suburb, against the text's 12,000; walls 30 ft high, the Dron
Sagar tank, bricks 15 × 10 × 2.5 in, and fourteen temple mounds. But "I feel quite unable to offer any
identification whatever of the Buddhist remains": the one solid brick mound, near Jagesar Mahadeva, is too small
for the 200-ft stupa. The survey did not return.

**Gazetteers.** Naini Tal 1904 and Himalayan III 1886 echo ASR I on Kashipur (circuit, tanks, Drona-sagar) and add
3rd–4th-century inscribed bricks, but "there is no positive confirmation". Atkinson (1884) proposes "the ruins near
Dhikuli some 22 miles to the north of Kashipur", for the elevated site, the remains of a stupa and local testimony
linking the name Bairat with Dhikuli, though "General Cunningham's identification may be allowed to stand". His
text puts Dhikuli on the Ramganga in one place and on the Kosi in another. Himalayan III 1886 records
Buddhist-style capitals, medallions and lions at Dhikuli, "locally identified with Bairatpatan, the capital of the
old kingdom of Govisana".

**Assessment.** Kashipur keeps the stronger measured fit, but by its proposer's own account its Buddhist monuments
are unfound. Dhikuli has Buddhist sculpture and a local tradition. "Agreed" overstates the case, so Mizutani is
weakened without being displaced.

### 04.13 堊醯掣呾羅國 (Ahicchattra) — Ramnagar; survey and gazetteers support it

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Ramnagar, west of Bareilly (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 412–14), Beal, Majumdar and
Dey: Ahichhatra or Ramnagar. Vivien: a compass plot NE of Bisauli.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 255–61) is the field basis of the identification. The place "still preserves its ancient
name as Ahichhatr". The fort is a triangle with sides of c. 5,900, 6,100 and 7,400 ft, over 3.5 miles round against
the 17–18 li, with ravines on the north and east for the "natural obstacles". Cunningham dug the ruins of some
twenty temples (the text has nine), and opened the 40-ft Chhatr mound, four of its eight satellite mounds being
stupas: "I have no doubt … as to the identity of the Chhatr mound with the Stupa of Hwen Thsang", though no trace
of the serpent tank was found.

**Gazetteers.** Bareilly 1911 and 1965 and Rohilkhand 1879 echo ASR I: the measured circuit, the name Adikot after
the Ahir Raja Adi and the cobra legend, the excavated stupa and its steatite box. Rohilkhand 1879 names the
"natural defences" as the Aril and Pairiya streams. Bareilly 1965 explains the oversized country circuit as
Harṣa's whole Ahicchatra bhukti (Banskhera plates).

**Assessment.** Name, measured circuit and excavation agree, and nothing counts against them. Mizutani is
strengthened.

### 04.14 毘羅刪拏國 (*Vilaśana) — Bilsar (Etah), where Cunningham himself moved from Atranji-Khera

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Bilsar or Bilsad, Etah district (agreed). Majumdar (p. 705): Bilsar; Cunningham
himself later dropped Atranji-Khera. Cunningham (1871, p. 420) and Beal (p. 201): Atranji-Khera. Vivien (p. 343):
Karsana.

**Survey.** In 1862–63 Cunningham rejected Soron and chose Atranji-Khera without visiting it, from his servant's
report of a mound c. 2 miles round with a 44-ft summit, emending 200 li to 300 (ASR I, pp. 265–71). In ASR XI
(pp. 13–21) he revises: "in 1862 I … failed to hit the mark; but in 1877 I was more successful". At Bilsar he found
two pillars inscribed by Kumāragupta (G.E. 96) and a mound 33 ft high; the circuit, c. 1¾ miles, is close to the
text's 12 li; he marched Bilsar–Sankisa three times (17–18 miles) to reallot the distances. A trench across the
great mound gave large bricks (14.5 × 8.5 in) under medieval house walls, taken for the Asoka stupa; mounds D–H
are the five deva temples, D excavated with square temple walls, F with the inscribed toraṇa pillars.

**Gazetteers.** Etah 1876 echoes ASR I's case for Atranji-Khera, including the kos argument. Etah 1911's Bilsar
article echoes ASR XI (the 33-ft mound, five temple sites, the Kumāragupta inscriptions of 415/416 AD), while its
Atranji article still follows ASR I.

**Assessment.** The survey abandoned Atranji-Khera on its own evidence. Bilsar has name, size, distance, Gupta
temple sites matching the five deva temples, and some excavation, though no inscription names the town.
Mizutani's Bilsar is secure.

### 04.15 劫比他國 (Kapitha / Sāṅkāśya) — Sankisa; the survey's measured ramparts and staircase seals settle it

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sankisa near Farrukhabad (disputed; Smith: near Pitiali, NE Etah, because of the
elephant capital). Cunningham (pp. 424–6), Vivien, Beal, Watters (p. 335) and Dey: Sankisa. Majumdar (p. 706):
reports Smith and notes that the Kanauj bearing is wrong.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 273–78): ramparts 18,900 ft round against the text's 20 li ("there can be no doubt …
that the place is actually the same"), punch-marked and cast coins, and the Nāga tank Kandaiya Tal SE of the ruins,
still offered milk in Vaiśākha. ASR XI (pp. 31–37) adds Pakna-Bihar, 6 miles E: a great monastery mound with an
Indo-Scythian pedestal inscription, many clay seals with the Buddhist creed, and seals showing three staircases
(lettered Bra, Bu, Sa) and one reading "Devārāma Saṅghasa".

**Gazetteers.** Etah 1876 and Farrukhabad 1911 echo ASR I (four passages): "absolute identity in name", the
position relative to Mathura, Ahichhatra and Kanauj, the circuit, and the Naga tank: "the one solitary spot that can
be identified with certainty". Farrukhabad 1911 adds that the gate village Paor-kheriya was wholly Brahman.

**Assessment.** Name, measured circuit and the living Nāga cult were already independent of the disputed pillar.
Seals of the three staircases from a monastery next door show that the descent was venerated here. This answers
Smith's objection to the town. The Kanauj distance still needs emendation.

### 04.15.02 三寶階 (Devāvatāra) — at Sankisa, by the Bisari Devi mound, where the pillar base was excavated

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sankisa (disputed; Smith rejects it because of the lion/elephant capital).
Cunningham (p. 425): the central mound, with Paor-Kheria as "steps village". Beal: Sankisa. Majumdar reports
Smith. Dey: the Bisari Devi temple.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 276–78) argues that the pilgrims mistook the elephant capital for a lion, estimates a pillar
of c. 52 ft, and puts the staircases at the Bisari Devi temple, though digging found no pillar base. ASR XI (p. 33)
reports that Cunningham later found it: the brick base (11 ft 9 in × 10 ft 2 in), 50–60 ft from the capital, due N
of the Bisari Devi mound and in line with its east wall, with a thick monastery wall east of the pillar. The steps
"were almost certainly on the old mound now dedicated to Bisari Devi". The Pakna-Bihar staircase seals (04.15)
belong here too.

**Gazetteers.** Farrukhabad 1911 (13 passages, two scans) echoes ASR I at length, including the failure to find a
pillar base, and does not report the excavation in ASR XI.

**Assessment.** A pillar standing beside a monastery mound in the position the text gives fixes the spot better
than Cunningham's 1871 central mound. The animal on the capital remains unexplained, so Smith's point is not
answered, but it no longer outweighs the site evidence. Mizutani is strengthened.

### 05.01 羯若鞠闍國 (Skt. Kanyakubja) — Kanauj; survey and gazetteers support Mizutani's reading of the river

**Identifications.** All authorities: Kanauj. Mizutani: Xuanzang's "Ganges on the west" is the Kālī. Cunningham
(pp. 430–4) emends the direction and distance from Sankisa.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 279–92): the old city ran c. 3 miles from Haji Harmayan to Makarandnagar, matching the
20 × 4–5 li; the Chota Ganga, now carrying the Kālī Nadī, is the old Ganges channel, so the pilgrims' Ganges "is
confirmed, not only by the traditions of the people, but also by the fact that the old channel still exists". But
Cunningham "failed in my search" for the great stupa SE of the town and could not identify the monuments.

**Gazetteers.** Farukhabad and Agra 1884 and Farrukhabad 1911 (four passages) echo ASR I: "Old Kanauj must have
stretched for over three miles along the old cliff of the Ganges", which descends to the Kali Nadi, and "Buddhist
coins and remains are curiously wanting at Kanauj".

**Assessment.** The old Ganges channel below the city supports Mizutani's reading of the river. The city is
secure, and its monuments are not identified.

### 05.01.09 納縛提婆矩羅城 (Skt. Navadevakula) — Newal near Bangarmau; Cunningham abandoned Nohbatganj on visiting it

**Identifications.** Mizutani gives both views without choosing (disputed). St. Martin, Cunningham (1871) and
Watters: Nohbatganj. Cunningham (p. 439) also suggests the city was washed away, or Deokali. Majumdar (pp. 707–8,
after Hoey) and Dey (after Führer): Nawal, just north of Bangarmau, c. 18–19 miles SE of Kanauj.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 293–95) has the 1871 view: the city was near Nohbatganj and swept away by the Ganges. In ASR
XI (pp. 48–52) Cunningham traced the old Ganges bed (the Kalyani Nadi) on the Oudh survey maps and visited it: "I
was much pleased to find my conjecture verified in the ruined mounds of Newal, just 2 miles to the north of
Bangarmau". Newal is 19 miles from Kanauj (Faxian 21, Xuanzang 17), the name keeps the first and last syllables,
and the mounds have bricks 15 × 9 in, Viṣṇu and Buddha heads and a Brahmanical temple. He assigns the monasteries
and the 100-ft stupa to the old fort of Bangarmau and the 200-ft stupa to the site of the Sayid's tomb.

**Gazetteers.** Unao 1903 ("It is agreed by most writers that the old site called Nawal … is the Navadevakula")
echoes ASR XI. Oudh A–G 1877, checking bearings on the map, says it "will probably be Bangarmau". Shahjahanpur,
Moradabad 1883 repeats the superseded Naubatganj.

**Assessment.** Name, distance, the old river bed and Buddhist sculpture point to Newal–Bangarmau, and the only
rival was given up by its own author. This should replace Mizutani's undecided entry.

### 05.02 阿踰陀國 (Skt. Ayodhyā) — on the Ganges below Bangarmau, in Mizutani's Fatehpur stretch; the site is not found

**Identifications.** Mizutani prefers Führer's Aphui, 29 miles SE of Fatehpur, as the best fit for the itinerary
(disputed). Vivien (p. 351), Watters (p. 354, reading the Ganges as a tributary) and Dey: Ayodhyā/Oudh.
Cunningham (1871, pp. 439–41) and Beal: Kakupur near Cawnpore, rejecting Ayodhyā. Majumdar lists
Kakupur, Patanvihar, Asni and Aphui. CII III: on the Sarayū near Fyzabad.

**Survey.** Cunningham's site moved three times. ASR I (pp. 293–98): Kakupur, not visited, reading 60 li for 600.
ASR XI (pp. 55–57), with Navadevakula now fixed at Newal: the pilgrim went SE and crossed the Ganges southward, so
the city lies on the south side of the Ganges, either Kutia Khas or Gunir NW of Fatehpur (Gunir as Vasubandhu's
monastery) if the river held its present course, or Patan-Bihar, south of the old bed, whose "eminently Buddhist
name" and square brick building in the fort fit, if it did not. He leaves it undecided. ASR XVII (pp. 99–100):
Asni has no Buddhist remains. Throughout he keeps Ajodhya for Viśākha (05.06).

**Gazetteers.** Oudh A–G 1877 argues for Ajodhya against Cunningham, whose "arguments are not convincing": the
latitudes make the SE bearing right, the Kanauj–Bangarmau–Ajodhya road is Faxian's, and Ramkot, Swargadwari,
Maniparbat and Guptar Bagh are matched to the monuments, though the 1,100-mile circuit leaves "no room". Fyzabad
1905 finds Ajodhya "distinctly wanting in remains" of the Buddhist period. Fatehpur 1916 echoes ASR XVII on Asni
and finds Gunir's mounds "formed solely by the action of the ravines". Aphui itself is not discussed.

**Assessment.** The text's crossing of the Ganges to the south, and Ajodhya's lack of Buddhist remains, count
against Oudh; Cunningham's field reasoning converges on the Fatehpur stretch where Mizutani puts Aphui. But none
of the sites examined there has Buddhist remains, so the city is unlocated and Ajodhya cannot be excluded.

### 05.03 阿耶穆佉國 (Ayamukha / Hayamukha) — the Partabgarh–Rae Bareli area; capital perhaps Tusaran Bihar

**Identifications.** Mizutani: unknown, roughly the Rae Bareli–Pratapgarh area NW of Allahabad (uncertain).
Majumdar (p. 708, after Smith): the Partabgarh and Rae Bareli districts, city Tusaran Bihar (Cunningham, ASR XI).
Cunningham (1871, pp. 443–4) and Dey: Daundia Khera. Vivien (p. 351): north of the Sarayū. Hoey (per Fatehpur
1916): Asni.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 295–98) proposed Daundia Khera only as the corollary of Kakupur and admitted it "must still
remain doubtful": a citadel 385 ft square, never 20 li round, no Buddhist remains. In ASR XI Cunningham found
nothing there older than a few centuries (p. 57) and proposed Tusaran Bihar (pp. 63–69): the old town of Bihar in
Bela (Partabgarh) district, on the north bank of an old Ganges bed, c. 30 miles NW of Allahabad. A half-mile brick
mound SE of the town ends in the Tusaran mound; his trench gave bricks with rounded faces "like the bars of a
Buddhist railing" before graves stopped it, and two small stone stupas, "Buddha" and "Buddhi", came from the town.
Dalmau to Bihar is 50 miles, the Life's 300 li; for Prayāga he reads 170 li for 700. His fallback is Singror on the
Ganges.

**Gazetteers.** Oudh H–M 1877: "Manikpur must have been in the kingdom of Aya Mukhi; at any rate it was not under
Kanauj." Oudh A–G 1877 rejects Daundia Khera, founded "about the 12th century", as ASR XI also found. Fatehpur 1916
reports Hoey's Asni but finds no architectural remains there.

**Assessment.** Daundia Khera is eliminated by its own proposer. Survey and gazetteers put the kingdom on the
Ganges in Partabgarh, inside Mizutani's area, and Bihar has a Buddhist name and railing-type bricks. Mizutani's
region is strengthened; Tusaran Bihar is a reasonable but unexcavated capital.

### 05.04 鉢邏耶伽國 (Skt. Prayāga) — Allahabad; the gazetteers argue that the confluence has shifted

**Identifications.** All authorities: Allahabad. Cunningham (pp. 445–6): the 7th-century city lay round the
Akshay Bat, inside the fort.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 296–98) repeats this view and adds nothing on the city's extent.

**Gazetteers.** Muttra, Allahabad, Fatehpur 1884 identifies the "celebrated temple" with Patalpuri because of the
tree in its court. Allahabad 1911 argues: "if the latter was the Patalpuri temple … the confluence of the rivers
was some distance further east than at present".

**Assessment.** The site is secure. The gazetteers add the shifting-confluence argument, which matters for
05.04.03–04.

### 05.04.03 天祠大樹 (the great tree of the Deva temple) — the Akshaya Vata at Patalpuri, with one gazetteer doubting

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the banyan in a riverside temple at Allahabad, its successor still venerated
(agreed). Cunningham, Beal, Majumdar and Dey: the Akshaya Vata in the underground Patalpuri temple in the fort.

**Survey.** ASR I (p. 297) places the Akshay Bat underground in a pillared court east of the Ellenborough Barracks,
due north of the Asoka pillar: "there can be little doubt that the famous tree … is the well known Akshay Bat".

**Gazetteers.** Allahabad 1968 and Muttra 1884 repeat this, noting the tradition of a reservoir where devotees
drowned themselves. Allahabad 1911 doubts: "the question is obscured by the uncertainty of identifying the temple
referred to by the Chinese writer with that still in existence". The tree "which in Hiuen Tsang's day stood in
the middle of Prayag … was in later times actually on the river bank". The temple is now underground because the
fort raised the ground level.

**Assessment.** Mizutani's identification stands. The 1911 gazetteer's reservations concern continuity, not a
rival site.

### 05.05 憍賞彌國 (Kauśāmbī) — Kosam; the survey's inscriptions and ramparts anticipate the excavation

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Kosam, confirmed by G. R. Sharma's excavations (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 451, 454,
reading 150 li for 500), Beal and Dey: Kosam. Watters (p. 367): rejects Kosam. Vivien: undecided. Majumdar
reports Rewah, Bharhut and Banda alternatives.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 303–12), following Bayley's suggestion: the SW bearing from Prayāga "points unmistakably
to the line of the Jumna"; the place is still Kosambi-nagar to the Jains; an Akbar-period inscription on the stone
pillar names it Kausambipura, and a 6th–7th-century one 3–4 ft below ground shows the pillar stood then; the
ramparts are 23,100 ft round, c. 5 miles 7 furlongs with the lost SW angle restored, against the 30 li. Udayana's
palace is put at the central mound under the Jain temple. ASR I thought the dragon's cave carried away by the
Jumna, since the Pabhosa hill "has no cave"; ASR XXI (pp. 1–3) found an artificial cave high in the Pabhosa hill
with a Nāga legend and Gupta rock inscriptions, though Pabhosa lies NW, not SW.

**Gazetteers.** Muttra, Allahabad, Fatehpur 1884 echoes ASR I verbatim (pillar inscription, ramparts, "name,
size, and position"). Allahabad 1911 echoes ASR XXI on the Pabhosa cave, and adds a 1036 AD inscription from Kara
fort naming the "mandala … of Kausamba" and the Digambara name Kausambinagri.

**Assessment.** Name, inscriptions and measured ramparts strengthen Kosam well before the excavations Mizutani
cites.

### 05.05.03 具史羅長者故宅 (Ghoṣila's house and monastery) — Mizutani's excavated monastery at Kosam; Cunningham's Gopsahsa rests on a village name

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the monastery taken for Ghoṣila's, found by excavation north of the east gate of
the Kosam rampart (agreed). Beal and Watters: the SE angle of the city, no site.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 311–15) puts Ghoṣila's garden at Gopsahsa, a village by Chota Garhawa SE of the fort, and
the monastery with the 200-ft Asoka stupa at Kosam Khiraj, ¼ mile SE, where railing pillars of two sizes are built
into house walls. ASR XXI (pp. 1–3) repeats that the name "appears to be preserved" in Gopsahsa and puts
Vasubandhu's two-storeyed brick chamber in the Tikri mound nearby, where two rooms were measured; no stupa was
traced.

**Gazetteers.** No passages on this site.

**Assessment.** Cunningham's site keeps the text's SE bearing but rests on a village-name match and surface
material. Mizutani's rests on excavation at Kosam itself. Mizutani's identification stands; Gopsahsa and the Tikri
mound are a record of the survey's alternative.

### 05.05.06 迦奢布羅城 (*Kāśapura) — open; Sultanpur is weak, and Partabgarh 1904's Bihar is Cunningham's Hayamukha site

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sultanpur on the Gomati, after Cunningham (uncertain; Mizutani doubts that
Xuanzang went there). Cunningham (pp. 457–8), Beal and Dey: Sultanpur (Kuśabhavanapura). Vivien: near the latitude
of Lucknow. Majumdar lists Jhusi (Vost) and Mohanlalganj (Smith). Watters: none.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 312–15): Kosam to Sultanpur by the Papamau ghat is 109 miles, the 700 li, and Sultanpur to
Ajudhya 30 miles, the next stage's 170–180 li. But Cunningham found no stupa at Sultanpur, only a report of an old
tope near Mahmudpur 5 miles NW; the fort mound is 750 ft square and the town c. 2 miles round (the text's 10 li).
The survey never returned to it.

**Gazetteers.** Sultanpur 1903 rejects its own town (four passages): "Nothing has ever been discovered at Mahmudpur
or the old site of Sultanpur"; kia renders ka, "never ku", so the name is Kāśipura; and Cunningham's 60 miles is
really 54–55. Partabgarh 1904 proposes Bihar in tahsil Kunda, "the same word as vihara", with ancient mounds,
"carved stones and ornamental bricks of Buddhist date", and an adjoining village "Silawatpur alias Kashipur".
Allahabad 1911 and 1968 report Jhusi (Kesi). Lucknow 1904 reports Mohanlalganj. Oudh 1877 suggests Kikori, Bari or
Misrikh.

**Assessment.** Partabgarh's Bihar is evidently the town Cunningham described in ASR XI as Tusaran Bihar and
proposed for Hayamukha (05.03), so the same remains are claimed for two places. It lies c. 30 miles NW of
Allahabad, far short of 700 li from Kauśāmbī. Sultanpur keeps the distance but has no remains and a weak name.
Mizutani is weakened, with no better candidate.

### 05.06 鞞索迦國 (*Viśākha) — unidentified; Ajodhya (Sāketa) is the only argued candidate, and its monuments are disputed

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no decision (disputed). Cunningham (1871, pp. 459–64) and Beal: Sāketa = Faxian's
Sha-chi = Ayodhyā. Watters (p. 374): perhaps Sāketa, but not Sha-chi. Vivien (p. 354): Bisvah or Biseipur. Smith:
Nimkhar. Führer: Sanchankot (Unao). Hoey: Pasha (Gonda). Burgess: Lucknow.

**Survey.** ASR I (pp. 317–28) sets out the case: Faxian's Sha-chi has the same tooth-brush legend; Buddha's
years at Viśākha match his years at Sāketa; coins of Dhanadeva and Viśākhadatta are found only at Ajudhya; and
Ajudhya lies 30 miles (170–180 li) north of Sultanpur. On the ground: the Sugriv Parbat (500 × 300 ft, with a well
and cloistered rooms) as the great monastery, the 65-ft Mani Parbat as the Asoka stupa, the Kuber Parbat as the
hair-and-nail stupa, and a mahwa tree the coolies called a Raja's tooth-brush. ASR XI (p. 68) and XVII (p. 100)
keep this view.

**Gazetteers.** Oudh A–G 1877 doubts Cunningham's Ajodhya monuments: two of Ramkot's largest bastions "are called
to this day Sugriva, and Kabir tila", so his mounds have no Buddhist tradition, and the tooth-brush tree was
"wrongly … identified with Ajodhya". It offers Satrikh or Hargam but concludes that "it is really impossible to
identify … Visakha". Fyzabad 1905: "the whole question is still uncertain". Faizabad 1960: Pi-sho-ka = Sha-chi = the
Buddhist Sāketa, "but [it] was not identical with our Ayodhya". Unao 1903 reports Sujankot; Lucknow 1904 Mahona.

**Assessment.** The survey gives Ajodhya its best case (coins and legend), but the distance rests on Sultanpur
(05.05.06), and the mounds are disputed and unexcavated. If Ayodhyā (05.02) lay on the Ganges, Ajodhya is free for
Viśākha. Mizutani's agnosticism stands, with Ajodhya the leading candidate.

- **Sthāneśvara, the Asoka stupa 4–5 li NW**: the Madrasa mound with reddish-yellow bricks (Cunningham, ASR II,
  pp. 220–21).
- **Mathurā, the dry tank where the monkey died**: the tank by the jail mound, whose monastery inscriptions call it
  Kunda-Suka (Cunningham, ASR I, p. 239; ASR III, pp. 28–29); later the tank by the Collector's kacheri mound at
  Jalalpur, with the Ambarikha mound as Upagupta's cave hill (Cunningham, ASR XVII, pp. 111–12).
- **Sāṅkāśya, the great monastery 20 li E**: the square mound S of Pakna-Bihar, 6 miles E of Sankisa, with an
  Indo-Scythian pedestal inscription, creed seals, three-staircase seals and a seal reading "Devārāma Saṅghasa"
  (Cunningham, ASR XI, pp. 31–37).
- **Kauśāmbī, the dragon's cave**: the artificial cave in the Pabhosa hill, with a Nāga legend and Gupta rock
  inscriptions (Cunningham, ASR XXI, pp. 1–3; ASR I, p. 312, had thought it carried away by the Jumna).
  **Vasubandhu's two-storeyed brick chamber**: the Tikri mound near Garauli and Gopsahsa, SE of the fort (ASR XXI,
  pp. 1–3).
