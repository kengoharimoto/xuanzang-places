# Notes on disputed places: juan 4–5

Places in juan 4–5 that meet at least one of the three criteria in `NOTES_INSTRUCTIONS.md`, in master-table order
(33 notes). Gazetteer volumes are cited as `district year`. "Atkinson" means the Himalayan Districts text, which
exists as *Himalayan districts II 1884* and in the Cornell scan catalogued as *unidentified 1874*
(cu31924023947751). The two have the same wording and are counted as one text. Checked but not noted, because the
authorities agree and no gazetteer argues: 04.02.03 (Tamasavana), 04.06 (Pāriyātra = Bairāt; Dey's entry
describes the mountain range, not a different site), 05.04.04, 05.05.03.

### 04.01 磔迦國 (Skt. Takka) — the country is the central Punjab; the capital is still open (Asarur or near Sialkot)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Punjab plain between the Beas and the text's "Indus" (taken to be the Chenāb);
capital unnamed, perhaps "new Śākala" (uncertain). Cunningham (1871, pp. 170, 207–8): the plains from the Indus to
the Beas; the capital Taki is the ruins of Asarur, 19 miles NE of Sangala. Majumdar (1924, p. 687): with Śākala at
Sialkot, the capital belongs c. 2 miles NE of Sialkot, not at Asarur. Vivien (p. 328): the Ravi–Beas doab, capital
near Amritsar. Beal (p. 165): Chenab/Ravi region. Watters (p. 291): no site. Dey: Madra/Takkadeśa between the
Ravi and Chenab.

**Gazetteers.** Gujranwala 1883 restates Cunningham's case for Asarur at length. A ruined town 11.5 miles NE of
Sakala fits the text's "15 li … in open flat country"; the ruins measure c. 15,600 ft round, "≈ 20 li"; there are
moulded bricks, a 21-ft well, and the Ransi mound with its Nao-gaja tomb. Jhang 1884, Lyallpur 1905 and Punjab
1919 repeat the same view. None of them argues independently.

**Assessment.** All of the Asarur evidence depends on Cunningham's Sangala (Sanglawala Tibba). If Śākala is
Sialkot, as Mizutani holds, Asarur loses its anchor and Majumdar's site near Sialkot follows. The gazetteers
leave Mizutani's tentative picture as it was.

### 04.01.02 奢羯羅故城 (Skt. Śākala) — Sialkot (Mizutani) against Sanglawala Tibba (Cunningham); the gazetteers only repeat Cunningham

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sialkot (disputed). Majumdar (p. 686) and Dey: Sialkot, rejecting Sangala as a
different place. Cunningham (p. 206) and Beal (p. 166): Sanglawala Tibba, west of the Ravi. Vivien (p. 329): near
Amritsar. Watters (p. 288): uncertain.

**Gazetteers.** Jhang 1884, Lyallpur 1905 and Gujranwala 1883 (19 passages, three volumes) all repeat
Cunningham. Their local detail is the small rocky hill with brick-covered ridges and "hills of iron-bearing grey
rock", which they read as matching Xuanzang's note on iron. Gujranwala adds Cunningham's claim that Ransi =
Nara-Sinha confirms the identification.

**Assessment.** The gazetteers pre-date the Sangala/Sakala distinction and add only the iron-rock remark, which
fits a whole belt of hills (Chiniot, Kirana). They do not weaken Mizutani's Sialkot.

### 04.02 至那僕底國 (Skt. Cīnabhukti) — Chiniyari (Mizutani) or Patti (Cunningham); still unsettled

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Chiniyari, on the Amritsar–Sialkot road (disputed). Majumdar (p. 687): Chine or
Chiniyari, tentatively. Cunningham (pp. 230, 232) and Beal (p. 173): Patti (Haibatpur-Patti), 27 miles NE of
Kasur. Watters (p. 293): Patti "need not be seriously considered". Vivien (p. 332): the Katoch country (Kangra).
Smith (per Mizutani): Ferozpore.

**Gazetteers.** Lahore 1883 (two scans) argues for Patti through Cunningham's reasoning. Lahore is the "great
Brahmanical city" on Takka's eastern border, and "midway between the two cities [Lahore and Jullundur] is a
village called Patti, to this day". The only evidence offered is the place-name.

**Assessment.** The gazetteers add no new evidence for Patti, only Cunningham's argument from the name. Mizutani's
Chiniyari is no better supported, so the place stays open.

### 04.03 闍爛達羅國 (Skt. Jālaṃdhara) — Jalandhar; the gazetteers argue only about how far the kingdom extended

**Identifications.** All authorities: Jalandhar. Cunningham (pp. 156–7) and Majumdar (p. 683): Trigarta, which
included the hills as far as Kangra.

**Gazetteers.** Kangra 1883 argues that the kingdom covered "the low hills from the Ravi to Simla" and that the
eleven hill principalities did not yet exist separately. Hoshiarpur 1884 and 1905 and Jalandhar 1884 and 1908
convert the text's dimensions to miles. Jalandhar 1884 adds that the old city was a little over 2 miles round and
that only two tanks (Gufa, Brahmkund) remain.

**Assessment.** The site is not in question. The gazetteers support Mizutani and Cunningham and add the
Kangra-hills extent.

### 04.04 屈露多國 (*Kulūta) — Kulu; Atkinson offers Jagatsukh (Nast) as the old capital

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Kulu district of the upper Beas; old capital Nagar (agreed). Cunningham
(pp. 162–3): Kulu, old capital Makarsa or Nagar. Beal, Watters, Majumdar and Dey: Kulu.

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson (Himalayan districts II 1884) names "Nast or Jagatsukh in the upper valley of the Bias,
the ancient seat of the Pala Rajas of Kulu" and notes that "Kulu is still called Kulanta by the people". Kangra
1883 and 1899 argue that the kingdom took in Bangahal, Seoraj, Bashahr and the hill parts of Mandi and Suket,
since the 3,000-li circuit is too large for the Kulu valley alone.

**Assessment.** The country is certain, and the surviving name Kulanta strengthens the name equation. Jagatsukh is
a candidate for the capital that none of the authorities records. It rests on dynastic tradition only, against
the Nagar tradition Mizutani reports.

### 04.04a 洛護羅國 (Lāhul) — Lahul; the gazetteers repeat it and do not meet Jäschke's objection

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Lahul, name doubtful (disputed, hearsay). Cunningham (p. 164), Beal (p. 177),
Watters (p. 299), Majumdar (p. 684) and Vivien (p. 334): Lahul (Lho-yul).

**Gazetteers.** Kangra 1883 and 1899 quote Cunningham's "Lahul (Lho-yul of the Tibetans)". Atkinson mentions
"Lo-hou-lo, the modern Lahul" in passing while discussing the Amazonian kingdom. The three "argues" in
`places.tsv` come from that passage, which argues about the Amazonian kingdom, not about Lahul.

**Assessment.** The gazetteers repeat Cunningham and add nothing on the linguistic objection Mizutani reports
(Jäschke: Lāhul is the Indian, not the Tibetan, name).

### 04.04b 秣邏娑國 (Tib. mar-sa) — Ladakh; the gazetteers add nothing

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Ladakh (mar-yul) (disputed; Satō objects). Cunningham (p. 164), Beal (p. 178),
Watters (p. 299) and Majumdar (p. 684, after Francke): Ladakh. Vivien (p. 334): 三波訶 = Champaka/Chamba.

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson (1884) puts Mo-lo-so or Malasa, also called San-po-ho, "some 2,000 li … to the north of
Lo-hou-lo", while looking for the Amazonian kingdom. This is consistent with Ladakh but names no site.

**Assessment.** No gazetteer evidence bears on the Ladakh equation. Vivien's Chamba remains the only real
alternative among the authorities.

### 04.05 設多圖盧國 (Skt. Śatadru) — Sarhind, tentatively; no gazetteer evidence

**Identifications.** Mizutani: named after the Sutlej, capital Sarhind (uncertain). Cunningham (pp. 166–7, after
correcting the distance to Bairāt), Beal (p. 178) and Dey: Sarhind. Vivien (p. 336): in the Sarasvati basin,
near Sirtah west of Thanesar. Watters (p. 300): nothing is known.

**Gazetteers.** No hits.

**Assessment.** Sarhind rests on Cunningham's emended distance alone. The question is as open as Mizutani leaves
it.

### 04.07 秣菟羅國 (Skt. Mathurā) — Mathura; the gazetteers put the ancient city north or south-west of the modern town

**Identifications.** All authorities: Mathura. Cunningham (pp. 427–8) and Majumdar (p. 706): the town has moved
north and west as the Jumna encroached.

**Gazetteers.** Muttra 1911 (Führer's view) argues that "the site of ancient Muttra is clearly indicated by an
extensive elevation of the soil to the south-west". It finds Kans-ka-Tila largely natural, places the old
Buddhist city "probably … north of the present city beyond the Katra", and reads the Chaubara mounds as Buddhist
stupas. Muttra, Allahabad, Fatehpur 1884 and Mathura 1968 argue for Mahaban, on the left bank opposite, as a site
of Faxian's monasteries "on both sides of the river", from Buddhist sculpture in the fort and the Assi-Khamba
building. Mathura 1968 notes that local inscriptions name more than a dozen monasteries.

**Assessment.** The identification is secure. The gazetteers bear on the layout: where the old city lay and the
probable Buddhist presence at Mahaban. On the layout they disagree among themselves.

### 04.07.03 鄔波毱多伽藍 (Upagupta's monastery) — not located; the gazetteers confirm Mizutani's verdict

**Identifications.** Mizutani: cannot now be located (disputed). Cunningham: the Yaśa Vihāra on the Katra mound.
Growse and Dey: Kankali Tila. Beal (p. 181): no mound fits the bearing. Watters (p. 308): Katra "undoubtedly
wrong", Kankali "not quite convincing".

**Gazetteers.** Muttra 1884 reports Growse's Kankali Tila, with its colossal Buddha statues and Huvishka
inscription, but no foundation line. Muttra 1911 rejects both proposals: "their identifications, based on a wrong
location of the city, are inadmissible, and both the Upagupta monastery and the monkey tank near it … have yet to
be discovered". It also notes that the Katra inscription names the Yaśa Vihāra and that Kankali Tila is a Jain
stupa.

**Assessment.** The gazetteers support Mizutani's conclusion on independent grounds: the Katra is named
epigraphically for another monastery, and Kankali is Jain. The site stays unknown.

### 04.08 薩他泥濕伐羅國 (Skt. Sthāneśvara) — Thanesar; the Karnal gazetteer's measurement supports it

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Thanesar (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 376–8), Vivien, Beal, Majumdar (p. 701) and
Dey: Thanesar. Watters (p. 315): the distances do not fit and the site should be c. 100 miles NE of Mathurā.

**Gazetteers.** Karnal 1976 argues from the ground: the fort, the Bahari suburb and the town stand on "three
mounds [that] together occupy a space of about 3 miles … which closely agrees with the 20 li of Yuan Chwang",
with 14-inch fort bricks ascribed to Harṣa. It also locates the Asoka stupa 4–5 li NW at a mound ¼ mile NW of the
town. Ambala 1883 and Meerut division 1875 repeat Cunningham.

**Assessment.** The measured circuit and the stupa mound support Thanesar against Watters' objection, which rests
on distances alone. Mizutani's identification is strengthened.

### 04.08.02 福地 (Kurukṣetra "Happy Land") — Kurukshetra; Ibbetson reads the extent differently and Karnal 1976 doubts Asthipur

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Kurukshetra battlefield, with Asthipur west of Thanesar (agreed). Cunningham
(pp. 381, 385), Beal (p. 184) and Vivien: likewise. Watters (p. 315): a 200-li circuit round Sthāneśvara, not
the Kurukshetra plain.

**Gazetteers.** Karnal 1884 (Ibbetson, two scans) equates the Happy Land with the Nardak and argues from a local
etymology (nir-daya against dukh). Against Cunningham, it says the 200 li "may surely be taken to mean … the
radius". Ambala 1883 says the bones are gone but "the site of Asthipur [is] still pointed out locally". Karnal
1976 reports that the mound was excavated (Theh Polar) and yielded 3rd–4th-century AD finds, which it says make
the Mahābhārata tradition doubtful.

**Assessment.** The region is not in doubt. The gazetteers sharpen two details Mizutani gives without
discussion: the extent (radius against circumference) and the Asthipur spot, which the excavation evidence
weakens as an ancient site.

### 04.08.04 倶昏荼僧伽藍 (*Gokaṇṭha / Govinda monastery) — unknown; Cunningham's Gunana with Karnal 1884's Sita Mai is the only candidate

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no site, only rival restorations of the name (unknown). Cunningham (p. 395,
tentative): probably Gunana, 17 miles SSW of Thanesar. Beal (p. 186) and Watters (p. 315): no site.

**Gazetteers.** Karnal 1884 (two scans) follows Cunningham's Gunana and suggests "it is not improbable that the
Gominda monastery" survives as the Sita Mai monastery, 4 miles from Gunana. Meerut division 1875 mentions the
monastery only in passing in its Srughna passage (see the data remarks).

**Assessment.** Gunana or Sita Mai is a tentative candidate, resting on Cunningham's reading of the route and a
surviving monastic site. It is better than nothing but far from secure.

### 04.09 窣祿勤那國 (Skt. Srughna) — Sugh near Jagadhri (Ambala district), not Kalsi

**Identifications.** Mizutani: "Sugh near Kālsī (30°32'N 77°53'E), Dehra Dun district" (agreed). Cunningham
(pp. 396, 398) and Watters: Sugh. Beal (p. 187): at or near Kalsi. Majumdar and Dey: Sugh near Kalsi, but Dey
also gives 20 miles NW of Saharanpur. Vivien: the Saharanpur sarkar.

**Gazetteers.** Ambala 1883, Meerut division 1875 and Saharanpur 1981 place Sugh exactly. It lies "in a bend of
the old bed of the Jamna" on the Jagadhri–Saharanpur road, "about 5 km east of Jagadhari in the Ambala district".
Ambala 1883 measures the fortified position at 19,000 ft, "in accord with the pilgrim's measurement", and records
coins on the spot and at Chaneti. Meerut 1875 adds tradition, large bricks and the later name Mandal. Dehra Dun
1911 and Atkinson's "not far below Kalsi" are loose descriptions, and they note Xuanzang's silence on the Kalsi
edict.

**Assessment.** The gazetteers strengthen Sugh with a measured circuit and finds. They also show that the
coordinates and "Dehra Dun district" in Mizutani's entry are Kalsi's, some 40 miles away, which should be
corrected to Sugh, Ambala district. Beal's Kalsi is not supported.

### 04.09.03 殑伽河源 (the Ganges "source") — where the Ganges leaves the hills near Hardwar; no gazetteer evidence

**Identifications.** Mizutani: not the Himalayan source but where the Ganges enters the plain near Hardwar
(uncertain). Watters (p. 315): Gaṅgādvāra or Hardwar. Vivien (p. 341): Gangotri. Beal (p. 188): undecided.

**Gazetteers.** No hits.

**Assessment.** The river 3–4 li wide that Beal cites argues for the plains reading. Nothing here changes
Mizutani's view.

### 04.10 秣底補羅國 (*Matipura) — the Bijnor side of the Ganges; Mandawar as capital is plausible but untested

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the region on the east bank of the Ganges opposite Srughna (disputed). Cunningham
(p. 399), Vivien (p. 343), Beal (p. 190) and Dey: Mandawar, near Bijnor. Majumdar (p. 703): Bijnor district.
Watters (p. 321): Mandawar fits neither direction nor area.

**Gazetteers.** Rohilkhand division 1879 repeats Cunningham's on-the-spot fits: an "old mango grove still on the
spot", the Lalpur mound of ancient bricks as Guṇaprabha's monastery, Hidayat Shah's shrine built of older
material as Saṅghabhadra's, and a town repeopled in 1114. Bijnor 1908 (five passages, two scans) doubts: "the
identification is open to the usual doubts"; the site "has never been scientifically explored"; and "these
identifications are necessarily conjectural". It notes a Buddhist stupa excavated at Mordhaj fort (Najibabad)
elsewhere in the district.

**Assessment.** The gazetteers support placing Matipura in the Bijnor tract east of the Ganges, which is
Mizutani's region. They also show that Mandawar as capital rests on unexcavated mounds. Mizutani's region is
mildly strengthened, and the capital stays open.

### 04.10.05 摩裕羅城 (Mayūra) — Mayapur at Hardwar, with good local remains; the bank remains a question

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Hardwar (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 402–4): ruined Mayapura between Hardwar and
Kankhal, now on the west bank. Beal, Majumdar and Dey: Mayapura or Hardwar. Watters (p. 328): Mayūra was on the
east side, so it is not Mayapura. Vivien (p. 347): the east bank opposite, or perhaps Amroha.

**Gazetteers.** Meerut division 1875, Saharanpur 1921 and 1981, and Atkinson (30 passages) repeat Cunningham but
add remains. The people point out the old city "from a torrent near the Sarovanath temple to Raja Ben's fort",
7,500 ft. There are large-brick foundations, ancient sculpture and coins, and the Maya Devi, Bhairon and Narayan
Bali temples. The Brahmakund well-temple is the "great temple" at the Ganges gate. Saharanpur 1981 also cites
Watters for Mo-yu-lo east of the Ganga.

**Assessment.** The remains at Mayapur strengthen Hardwar as the city. Watters' objection about the bank is
answered only by Cunningham's guess that the river has shifted, which no gazetteer tests.

### 04.11 婆羅吸摩補羅國 (Skt. Brahmapura) — Garhwal–Kumaon; Srinagar is the weakest of three capitals (Lakhanpur, Barahat, Srinagar)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Alaknanda valley, capital Srinagar (Garhwal), reading NE for N (agreed).
Vivien (p. 344): Srinagar. Cunningham (pp. 407–8): Garhwal and Kumaon, capital Vairāṭpattan or Lakhanpur on the
Ramganga; he rejects Srinagar as founded in 1358. Beal (p. 198): Garhwal and Kumaon.

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson (Himalayan districts II 1884, 23 passages across two scans and the Cornell copy) rejects
Srinagar, which "was of no importance until … the early part of the seventeenth century". He also rejects
Lakhanpur and the Barhepura ruins near Najibabad, and proposes Barahat (Uttarkashi), "an ancient and well-known
site almost exactly fifty miles due north of Hardwar". This keeps Xuanzang's "north". His evidence is the
12th-century trident inscription of Aneka Malla at Barahat (twinned at Gopeswar) and the route: the distance to
Govisana counts from Madawar. Garhwal 1910 accepts Barahat cautiously. Almora 1981 supports Lakhanpur: the
6th-century Taleshwar copperplates were "issued from the city of Brahmapura". Naini Tal 1904 includes Kumaon.

**Assessment.** The gazetteers count against Mizutani's Srinagar, which is late and which Cunningham himself
rejects. They leave two candidates. Lakhanpur has the epigraphic Brahmapura of the Taleshwar plates. Barahat
keeps the text's bearing but has only a 12th-century inscription. "Agreed" understates the dispute.

### 04.11.02 蘇伐剌拏瞿呾羅國 (Skt. Suvarṇagotra) — a real trans-Himalayan country; Atkinson puts it in Nari-khorsum

**Identifications.** Mizutani: a real Himalayan country, the Eastern Women's Kingdom of the Tangshu (disputed;
Thomas: partly Hunza-Nagar). Watters (p. 330): "undoubtedly mythical". Dey: north of Brahmapura; the Amazonian
kingdom in the trans-Himalayan Sutlej valley. Vivien and Beal: no location.

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson (14 passages) argues at length. Suvarṇagotra is the Suvarṇabhūmi of the Purāṇas, "the
Nari-khorsum district of Tibet lying to the north of Kumaon and Garhwal". There are "no indications or traditions
of any Amazonian kingdom" in the Katyur, Juhar or Niti valleys, so "we must search for it across the passes in
Tibet". From the Sui and Tang annals (the Eastern Nü ruled by a woman, absorbed by Lhasa after 742), "there is …
no need to doubt the statements of Hwen Thsang".

**Assessment.** Atkinson anticipates Mizutani's position that the kingdom was real, from the same Chinese annals.
He adds a concrete location (western Tibet, north of Kumaon and Garhwal) that fits the text's borders. This
strengthens Mizutani against Watters, but the location is general.

### 04.12 瞿毘霜那國 (Govisana) — Kashipur (Ujain fort) still fits best; Dhikuli is a live alternative

**Identifications.** Mizutani: ruins 1 mile east of Kashipur (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 409, 412) and Beal: the
Ujain fort near Kashipur. Majumdar (p. 704) reports Führer: the capital is Vairāṭpattan. Vivien: a compass plot
near Chandausi.

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson (1884) proposes "the ruins near Dhikuli some 22 miles to the north of Kashipur". His
grounds are the elevated site, enough remains for a stupa, and local testimony connecting the name Bairat with
Dhikuli. Even so, "General Cunningham's identification may be allowed to stand". His text puts Dhikuli on the
Ramganga in one place and on the Kosi in another. Himalayan districts III 1886 records Buddhist-style capitals,
medallions and lions at Dhikuli, "locally identified with Bairatpatan, the capital of the old kingdom of
Govisana". For Kashipur, Naini Tal 1904 and Himalayan III 1886 restate the measured circuit (over 11,000 ft with
the southern suburb, against the text's 12,000), the tanks, the Drona-sagar and 3rd–4th-century inscribed
bricks. But "there is no positive confirmation", and Cunningham's Khagpur mound is too small for the 200-ft
stupa.

**Assessment.** Kashipur keeps the stronger measured fit, but its Buddhist monuments are unfound. Dhikuli has
Buddhist sculpture and a local tradition. "Agreed" overstates the case, so Mizutani is weakened without being
displaced.

### 04.13 堊醯掣呾羅國 (Ahicchattra) — Ramnagar; the gazetteers support it

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Ramnagar, west of Bareilly (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 412–14), Beal, Majumdar and
Dey: Ahichhatra or Ramnagar. Vivien: a compass plot NE of Bisauli.

**Gazetteers.** Bareilly 1911 and 1965 and Rohilkhand 1879 argue from the ground. The walled city, "over 3.5 miles
round", matches the 17–18 li. The ruins are called Adikot after the Ahir Raja Adi and the cobra legend. Cunningham
excavated the 40-ft Chhatar stupa and found a steatite box. Rohilkhand 1879 identifies the "natural defences" as
the Aril and Pairiya streams. Bareilly 1965 explains the oversized country circuit as Harṣa's whole Ahicchatra
bhukti (Banskhera plates).

**Assessment.** The gazetteers support Mizutani with measured circuit, name tradition and excavation, and nothing
counts against them.

### 04.14 毘羅刪拏國 (*Vilaśana) — Bilsar (Etah); Cunningham's Atranji-Khera is superseded

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Bilsar or Bilsad, Etah district (agreed). Majumdar (p. 705): Bilsar; Cunningham
himself later dropped Atranji-Khera. Cunningham (1871, p. 420) and Beal (p. 201): Atranji-Khera. Vivien (p. 343):
Karsana.

**Gazetteers.** Etah 1876 sets out Cunningham's earlier case for Atranji-Khera: a great mound c. 2 miles round, a
44-ft summit taken as the Asoka stupa, and a distance problem (43 miles direct against 200 li) explained away by
regional kos. Etah 1911 gives both views. Its Bilsar article follows Cunningham's 1877 identification: "a great
mound 33 feet in height, probably the site of Asoka's stupa", the sites of five Brahman temples, and Kumāragupta
pillar inscriptions of 415/416 AD. Its Atranji article is still conditional on the older view.

**Assessment.** The Gupta inscriptions and the five-temple layout at Bilsar fit the text better than Atranji's
mound and kos argument. The gazetteers strengthen Mizutani. Etah 1911 is internally inconsistent.

### 04.15 劫比他國 (Kapitha / Sāṅkāśya) — Sankisa; the gazetteers add the living Nāga cult

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sankisa near Farrukhabad (disputed; Smith: near Pitiali, NE Etah, because of the
elephant capital). Cunningham (pp. 424–6), Vivien, Beal, Watters (p. 335) and Dey: Sankisa. Majumdar (p. 706):
reports Smith and notes that the Kanauj bearing is wrong.

**Gazetteers.** Etah 1876 and Farrukhabad 1911 argue Cunningham's case (four passages): "absolute identity in
name", the position relative to Mathura, Ahichhatra and Kanauj, and ruins matching the 20-li circuit. The
decisive local item is the Naga tank Kandaiya Tal SE of the ruins, where "milk [is] offered daily in Baisakh and
when rain is wanted", as in Faxian. "The tank of the Naga is the one solitary spot that can be identified with
certainty." Farrukhabad 1911 adds that the gate village Paor-kheriya was wholly Brahman, matching Xuanzang's many
Brahmans. Etah 1876 proposes emending 200 li to 300 for Sankisa–Kanauj (50 miles by road).

**Assessment.** The living Nāga cult and the name are independent of the disputed pillar. They strengthen
Sankisa against Smith, who objected only to the capital. The Kanauj distance still needs an emendation.

### 04.15.02 三寶階 (Devāvatāra) — at Sankisa; the gazetteers repeat Cunningham's pillar and temple argument

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sankisa (disputed; Smith rejects it because of the lion/elephant capital).
Cunningham (p. 425): the central mound, with Paor-Kheria as "steps village". Beal: Sankisa. Majumdar reports
Smith. Dey: the Bisari Devi temple.

**Gazetteers.** Farrukhabad 1911 (13 passages, two scans) quotes Cunningham at length. The elephant capital is
"undoubtedly much older" than the pilgrims, and the shaft estimated from the neck diameter (c. 52 ft) matches
Faxian's 30 cubits. The Bisari Devi temple "most probably occupies the site of the three staircases", and the
three mounds east of Nivi-ka-kot are the three stupas. Excavations were unsuccessful and no pillar base was found.

**Assessment.** The gazetteers repeat Cunningham. They leave the animal on the capital unexplained, so Smith's
objection stands. The location follows 04.15.

### 05.01 羯若鞠闍國 (Skt. Kanyakubja) — Kanauj; the gazetteers support Mizutani's reading of the river

**Identifications.** All authorities: Kanauj. Mizutani: Xuanzang's "Ganges on the west" is the Kālī. Cunningham
(pp. 430–4) emends the direction and distance from Sankisa.

**Gazetteers.** Farukhabad and Agra 1884 and Farrukhabad 1911 (four passages) argue that "Old Kanauj must have
stretched for over three miles along the old cliff of the Ganges". The town stands on mounds and ravines "which
descend through that cliff to the Kali Nadi", the old river bed. They note that "Buddhist coins and remains are
curiously wanting at Kanauj". Cunningham could "only … guess vaguely" at four of the pilgrims' monuments.

**Assessment.** The old Ganges cliff now above the Kālī Nadī supports Mizutani's reading of the river. The city is
secure, and its monuments are not identified.

### 05.01.09 納縛提婆矩羅城 (Skt. Navadevakula) — Nawal, near Bangarmau, has the most support

**Identifications.** Mizutani gives both views without choosing (disputed). St. Martin, Cunningham and Watters:
Nohbatganj. Cunningham (p. 439) also suggests the city was washed away, or Deokali. Majumdar (pp. 707–8, after
Hoey) and Dey (after Führer): Nawal, just north of Bangarmau, c. 18–19 miles SE of Kanauj.

**Gazetteers.** Unao 1903 says: "It is agreed by most writers that the old site called Nawal, about 18 miles
south-east of Kanauj, is the Navadevakula". Oudh A–G 1877, checking bearings on the map, says it "will probably
be Bangarmau, an old city so situated". Shahjahanpur, Moradabad 1883 repeats Cunningham's Naubatganj, with Faxian's
forest "supposed swept away by the Ganges".

**Assessment.** Two of the three gazetteers point to the Nawal–Bangarmau site, an old city with Kushan coins (per
Mizutani), at the right bearing and distance. Nohbatganj rests on a lost site.

### 05.02 阿踰陀國 (Skt. Ayodhyā) — Ajodhya (Oudh) is at least as well supported as Mizutani's Aphui

**Identifications.** Mizutani prefers Führer's Aphui, 29 miles SE of Fatehpur, as the best fit for the itinerary
(disputed). Vivien (p. 351), Watters (p. 354, reading the Ganges as a tributary) and Dey: Ayodhyā/Oudh.
Cunningham (pp. 439–41): Kakupur near Cawnpore, rejecting Ayodhyā. Beal reports Cunningham. Majumdar lists
Kakupur, Patanvihar, Asni and Aphui. CII III: on the Sarayū near Fyzabad.

**Gazetteers.** Oudh A–G 1877 argues for Ajodhya against Cunningham, whose "arguments are not convincing". Kanauj
(27°8'N) to Ajodhya (26°47'N) makes the SE bearing right, and the Kanauj–Bangarmau–Ajodhya road is Faxian's. It
matches Ramkot, Swargadwari, Maniparbat (Asaṅga's convent) and the Guptar Bagh temples to Xuanzang's monuments,
but admits that the 1,100-mile circuit leaves "no room". Fyzabad 1905 finds Ajodhya "distinctly wanting in
remains which can be assigned with certainty to the Buddhist period", though unexcavated mounds lie to the east.
Faizabad 1960: "generally identified with Ayodhya". In the Fatehpur region, Fatehpur 1916 reports that Cunningham
"abandoned" Asni "after visiting the place". It finds Gunir's mounds "formed solely by the action of the ravines",
against Cunningham's Vasubandhu monastery there. Aphui itself is not discussed.

**Assessment.** The gazetteers find no remains supporting the Fatehpur-region candidates they examine (Asni,
Gunir). For Ajodhya they offer a bearing, road and monument case that Mizutani does not weigh, though Buddhist
remains are lacking. The evidence here makes Ajodhya at least as good as Aphui.

### 05.03 阿耶穆佉國 (Ayamukha / Hayamukha) — the Partabgarh–Rae Bareli area; Manikpur supports Mizutani's region

**Identifications.** Mizutani: unknown, roughly the Rae Bareli–Pratapgarh area NW of Allahabad (uncertain).
Majumdar (p. 708, after Smith): the Partabgarh and Rae Bareli districts, city Tusaran Bihar (Cunningham, ASR XI).
Cunningham (pp. 443–4) and Dey: Daundia Khera. Vivien (p. 351): north of the Sarayū. Hoey (per Fatehpur 1916):
Asni.

**Gazetteers.** Oudh H–M 1877: "Manikpur must have been in the kingdom of Aya Mukhi; at any rate it was not under
Kanauj." Oudh A–G 1877 rejects Daundia Khera, whose foundation "about the 12th century" is clear from tradition.
Fatehpur 1916 reports Hoey's Asni but finds no architectural remains there.

**Assessment.** The gazetteers eliminate Daundia Khera and put the kingdom on the Ganges at Manikpur (Partabgarh),
inside Mizutani's area. This mildly strengthens his region. The capital remains unknown.

### 05.04 鉢邏耶伽國 (Skt. Prayāga) — Allahabad; the gazetteers argue that the confluence has shifted

**Identifications.** All authorities: Allahabad. Cunningham (pp. 445–6): the 7th-century city lay round the
Akshay Bat, inside the fort.

**Gazetteers.** Muttra, Allahabad, Fatehpur 1884 identifies the "celebrated temple" with Patalpuri because of the
tree in its court. Allahabad 1911 argues: "if the latter was the Patalpuri temple … the confluence of the rivers
was some distance further east than at present".

**Assessment.** The site is secure. The gazetteers add the shifting-confluence argument, which matters for
05.04.03–04.

### 05.04.03 天祠大樹 (the great tree of the Deva temple) — the Akshaya Vata at Patalpuri, with one gazetteer doubting

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the banyan in a riverside temple at Allahabad, its successor still venerated
(agreed). Cunningham, Beal, Majumdar and Dey: the Akshaya Vata in the underground Patalpuri temple in the fort.

**Gazetteers.** Allahabad 1968 and Muttra 1884 repeat this, noting the tradition of a reservoir where devotees
drowned themselves. Allahabad 1911 doubts: "the question is obscured by the uncertainty of identifying the temple
referred to by the Chinese writer with that still in existence". The tree "which in Hiuen Tsang's day stood in
the middle of Prayag … was in later times actually on the river bank". The temple is now underground because the
fort raised the ground level.

**Assessment.** Mizutani's identification stands. The 1911 gazetteer's reservations concern continuity, not a
rival site.

### 05.05 憍賞彌國 (Kauśāmbī) — Kosam; the gazetteers anticipate the excavation with epigraphic evidence

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Kosam, confirmed by G. R. Sharma's excavations (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 451, 454,
reading 150 li for 500), Beal and Dey: Kosam. Watters (p. 367): rejects Kosam. Vivien: undecided. Majumdar
reports Rewah, Bharhut and Banda alternatives.

**Gazetteers.** Allahabad 1911 defends Cunningham against "the distances quoted by the Chinese pilgrims". It cites
a 1036 AD inscription from Kara fort naming the "mandala … of Kausamba", and notes the place "is still known as
Kausambinagri" to Digambara Jains. Muttra, Allahabad, Fatehpur 1884 adds an Akbar-period inscription on the pillar
naming it Kausambipura and earthen ramparts 23,100 ft round. It notes that Xuanzang's SW bearing from Prayāga
points to the Jumna, not the Ganges.

**Assessment.** The gazetteers strengthen Kosam with epigraphy and measured ramparts well before the excavations
Mizutani cites.

### 05.05.06 迦奢布羅城 (*Kāśapura) — Sultanpur is weakened; Bihar–Kashipur (Partabgarh) is a new candidate

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sultanpur on the Gomati, after Cunningham (uncertain; Mizutani doubts that
Xuanzang went there). Cunningham (pp. 457–8), Beal and Dey: Sultanpur (Kuśabhavanapura). Vivien: near the latitude
of Lucknow. Majumdar lists Jhusi (Vost) and Mohanlalganj (Smith). Watters: none.

**Gazetteers.** Sultanpur 1903 rejects its own town (four passages). "Nothing has ever been discovered at
Mahmudpur or the old site of Sultanpur." Phonetically, kia renders ka, "never ku", so the name is Kāśipura. The
700 li is the Life's figure for Prayāga to Kauśāmbī city, and Cunningham's 60 miles is really 54–55. Partabgarh
1904 proposes Bihar in tahsil Kunda, where "Bihar is of course the same word as vihara". It has ancient mounds,
"carved stones and ornamental bricks of Buddhist date", and an adjoining village "Silawatpur alias Kashipur". Not
excavated. Allahabad 1911 and 1968 report Jhusi (Kesi). Lucknow 1904 reports Mohanlalganj. Oudh 1877 suggests
Kikori, Bari or Misrikh.

**Assessment.** The case for Sultanpur is weakened on both evidence and phonetics. Partabgarh 1904's Bihar joins
a Kāśipura name to a "vihara" name and Buddhist-date fragments, and none of the authorities in these files records
it. It may be the same as the "Tusaran Bihar" Majumdar reports for 05.03, which cannot be checked here. It is the
best-supported candidate in these sources, but only weakly.

### 05.06 鞞索迦國 (*Viśākha) — unidentified; the gazetteers weaken the equation with Ajodhya

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no decision (disputed). Cunningham (pp. 459–64) and Beal: Sāketa = Faxian's Sha-chi
= Ayodhyā. Watters (p. 374): perhaps Sāketa, but not Sha-chi. Vivien (p. 354): Bisvah or Biseipur. Smith: Nimkhar.
Führer: Sanchankot (Unao). Hoey: Pasha (Gonda). Burgess: Lucknow.

**Gazetteers.** Oudh A–G 1877 doubts Cunningham's Ajodhya monuments. Two of Ramkot's largest bastions "are called
to this day Sugriva, and Kabir tila", so Cunningham's mounds have no tradition. A footnote says the tooth-brush
tree was "wrongly … identified with Ajodhya". It offers Satrikh or Hargam but concludes that "it is really
impossible to identify … Visakha". Fyzabad 1905: "the whole question is still uncertain"; no certain Buddhist
remains at Ajodhya. Faizabad 1960 argues that Pi-sho-ka = Sha-chi = the Buddhist Sāketa, "but [it] was not
identical with our Ayodhya". Unao 1903 reports Sujankot; Lucknow 1904 reports Mahona.

**Assessment.** The gazetteers count against Cunningham's equation and add only names (Satrikh, Hargam, Mahona)
with no evidence. Mizutani's agnosticism stands.
