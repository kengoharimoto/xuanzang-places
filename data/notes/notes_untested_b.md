# Notes on places not yet assessed: juans 2–4 (list b)

### 02.01.02 五印度 (India as a whole) — the Five Indias are India; nothing to dispute

**Identifications.** Mizutani: India as a whole, with the traditional fivefold division (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 11,
13), Watters (p. 139) and Dey agree.
**Foucher (1942–47).** Notes that Xuanzang crosses "la frontière de l'Inde du Nord" on entering Lampāka (I, p. 36).
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: India in the traditional fivefold (Purāṇic) division, citing Cunningham for
its half-moon shape (pp. 164–165).
**Assessment.** A general description, not a site; the authorities agree and nothing here bears on it. Mizutani
stands (medium).

### 02.02 濫波國 (lampāka) — Laghman; the name survives and everyone agrees

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Laghman region, E Afghanistan (agreed). Vivien (p. 298), Cunningham (p. 50), Beal
(p. 90), Watters (p. 181; Babur's Lamghanat), Majumdar (p. 674) and Dey: Lamghan/Laghman, N of the Kabul river
between the Alingar and Kunar.
**Evidence.** The name runs from Lampāka to Lamghan. Himalayan Districts II (1884) and the 1874 volume mention it in
passing. Route test: Kapiśa to Laghman 95 km for "600+ li E" (predicted 143 km), bearing 22° off. The Life
(T50:0229b) has the same route over the Black Ridge.
**Foucher (1942–47).** Laghmān. He rode the old direct road from Kāpiśī SE by Nijrao, Tagao and the Bādpash pass (c.
1,600 m) in October 1925, with stūpas along it and a marble liṅga at Tagao; this road, not one through Kabul, is
Xuanzang's (I, pp. 34, 36).
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Laghman, on the N bank of the Kabul river between the Alingar and the Kunar
(p. 219).
**Name.** 濫波 renders only lampa (濫 lam, with -m before the labial; 波 pa): Xuanzang drops the suffix of lampāka, as he
does in kalanda(-ka) and kapittha(-ka). The Sanskrit form and the identification are unaffected (data/names/).
**Assessment.** Name, position between Kapiśa and Nagarahāra, and route agree; there is no site evidence. Mizutani
stands with medium confidence.

### 02.03.05 醯羅城 (haḍḍa) — Hadda S of Jalalabad; name, stūpa field and excavation agree

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Hadda, c. 8 km S of Jalalabad, excavated by Barthoux (agreed). Vivien (p. 304),
Cunningham (p. 52), Beal (p. 95), Watters (p. 184) and Majumdar (p. 675): Hidda, c. 5 miles S of Jalalabad.
**Evidence.** Cunningham notes the village's "many stupas, tumuli and caves". Konow (CII 2.1, p. 157) reports
Masson's jar with an ink Kharoṣṭhī inscription from one of the Hidda stūpas. Route test: 8 km SE for "30+ li"
(predicted 7 km). The Life (T50:0229b) calls it the city of the Buddha's skull bone, 10+ li SE over a sand ridge.
**Foucher (1942–47).** Hi-lo is Hadda "sans contestation possible", 7–8 km S of Jalalabad; his trial excavation of
1923 in a quadrangle by the Teppeh-Kalān found small stūpas and stucco sculpture (I, pp. 152–3).
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Hadda, c. 5 miles S of Jalalabad, from haḍḍa "bone", after Cunningham, with
the French excavations in support (p. 230).
**Kuwayama (1990, 2002).** Hadda: Faxian's 醯羅城 on Nagarahāra's border, with the 佛頂骨精舍. His point is the movement of
relics. Faxian (c. 400) has the parietal bone at Hadda but the robe and staff in a valley 1 yojana NE of the city and
the tooth stūpa in the city; Daoyao (440s; the 道榮 of the 洛陽伽藍記) has robe and staff in the 耆賀濫寺; Xuanzang finds the
tooth stūpa a ruin and the parietal bone, eyeball, robe and staff all in Hadda's two-storeyed building. The relics
were therefore moved to Hadda between the mid-5th century and 629 (1990, pp. 253–257; 2002, pp. 119–120). His Hadda
chapter (2002, ch. VI, pp. 90–106) compares the stūpa courts of Tapa Shotor and Lalma but ties no excavated building
to the skull-bone building. He also suggests (2002, p. 26) that Dao'an's 鉢吐羅越 may hide a *醯羅越, "the royal city of the
Buddha's robe", close to 醯羅; that would put the robe at Hadda in the 4th century, which sits ill with his own
relocation argument.
**Name.** 醯 is hi or he (曉, 齊 rhyme) and never renders a, so haḍḍa "bone", the etymology Ji and others repeat, breaks
the first syllable; 羅 for ḍḍ is exceptional (the key otherwise has ra/la, with ḍ in 荼, 擇 and 仗). The characters give
*Hiḍa or *Hiḷa (Prakrit ḍ > ḷ), which matches the i of the modern Hidda and Cunningham's "Hida" (p. 52). No ancient
form of the place name has been found: CII 2.1 has only the modern "Hidda inscription". Low confidence for the form;
the identification is unaffected (data/names/).
**Recent scholarship.** Errington (2017, pp. 19–20) edits Masson's unpublished treatise (BL G41), which from
Abel-Rémusat's Faxian already suggested that Hi-lo, "could we fancy it by any chance to denote Hida", was the modern
Hadda, before Cunningham. Masson's Hadda 10 (Tope Kelan) held the largest deposit he found: over 200 coins, mostly
Sasanian silver down to Peroz, five Roman solidi of Theodosius II, Marcianus and Leo (to AD 474) and Alkhan Hun
issues, dating the stūpa to the mid or late 5th century (pp. 33, 42). A major new stūpa at Hadda then is consistent
with Kuwayama's view that the relics were gathered there between the mid-5th century and 629, though Errington does
not make the link.
**Assessment.** The name (modern Hidda; Xuanzang's *Hiḍa/*Hiḷa, not haḍḍa), the distance and a large excavated
Buddhist site agree, and no one proposes another place. Secure.

### 02.04 健馱邏國 (gandhāra) — the Peshawar valley; capital fixed by Kaniṣka's inscribed casket

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Gandhāra between the Kunar and the Indus, roughly Peshawar district (agreed).
Vivien (p. 307), Cunningham (pp. 55–56), Beal (p. 97), Watters (p. 199) and Dey agree, differing only on the
boundaries.
**Evidence.** Peshawar 1883 and 1899 deduce from the 1,000 × 800 li that the country took in the Peshawar valley,
the Khaibar hills to Laghman and Kohat–Bannu. Stein (AR 1911–12, p. 99) finds no Xuanzang site at Sahri-Bahlol, which
concerns sites, not the country. The capital's great stūpa is fixed by Kaniṣka's inscribed casket at
Shāh-ji-ki-Dheri (see 02.04.03). Route test: Hadda to Gandhāra 144 km for "500+ li SE" (predicted 120 km).
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees on the Kabul-river basin between the Kunar and the Indus, but adds the
Rawalpindi district (p. 234). For Xuanzang's time that is too wide: the text treats Takṣaśilā, in the Rawalpindi
district, as a separate country subject to Kashmir (03.03).
**Kuwayama (1990, 2002).** The 罽賓 of the 4th–5th-century Buddhist sources (the *Gaoseng zhuan* and *Chu sanzang jiji*)
is Gandhāra, with its capital at Puruṣapura, not Kashmir. The bowl that Faxian saw at Puruṣapura in 402 and that
Zhimeng (after 404), Tanwujie (Dharmodgata, after 420) and Huilan saw "in 罽賓" is one object by its description (dark
purple-blue, the four rims visible); their routes, by Bolor and the Indus, are Faxian's; 乾陀羅 and 揵陀越 do not occur in
those two books; and Kashmir has no Buddhist remains older than the 7th century except Harwan (1990, pp. 115–200, esp.
121–123, 161–166, 188–189; 2002, pp. 22, 142–146). He limits the claim to Huijiao's usage (1990, n. 13, pp.
1480–1481). It adds to the history of the name, not to the extent of the country.
**Assessment.** Agreement, the inscribed casket at the capital and the route make it secure.

### 02.04.01a 布路沙布邏 (puruṣapura) — Peshawar; name and the Kaniṣka stūpa settle it

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Peshawar, S bank of the Kabul river (agreed). Vivien (p. 306), Cunningham (p. 55),
Beal (p. 97), Watters (p. 200), Majumdar (p. 678: "now certain; excavated") and Dey agree.
**Evidence.** Peshawar 1899 traces the name through Parshawar (Masʿudi, Abu Rihan, Babur) and Purshor. Cunningham
(ASR II, pp. 87–92) builds the Gandhāra itinerary on Peshawar. The Kaniṣka casket from Shāh-ji-ki-Dheri, just
outside the Ganj Gate (02.04.03), anchors the city. Pargiter (EI 12, p. 302) takes Peshawar as the capital in passing.
**Foucher (1942–47).** Agrees: the old city lies under Peshawar City, which "ne nous a pas semblé avoir changé de
place", reached from the Khyber by Takkal (I, p. 44).
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the NW part of Peshawar city, on the S side of the Kabul river (p. 235).
**Kuwayama (1990, 2002).** Peshawar. He equates Faxian's 弗樓沙, the 佛律婆[娑]越 of Zhi Sengzai's *Waiguo shi* ("the royal
city of the devaputra") and the 揵陀越王城 of Dao'an's *Shishi Xiyu ji* with it, and puts the bowl's building inside the
city (1990, pp. 162, 170–175; 2002, pp. 24–26; see 02.04.02). Agrees.
**Recent scholarship.** Baums (2019, p. 169), listing the settlements called *nagara* or *pura* in the Gāndhārī
inscriptions, gives Kaniṣkapura at Peshawar (CKI 145). It is a Kushan-period city name, not Xuanzang's 布路沙布邏, and does
not bear on the identification.
**Assessment.** Continuous name and an inscription at the city's chief monument. Secure.

### 02.04.14b 跋虜沙城東門外窣堵波 — But Shahri E of Shahbaz Garhi (Foucher); a single proposal

**Identifications.** Mizutani: But Shahri, a few km out of the E gate of Shahbaz Garhi, S of the road to Hund
(Foucher) (agreed). No other authority names a site.
**Assessment.** It depends on the town at Shahbaz Garhi (02.04.14, which stands) and on Foucher alone; nothing in
these files describes the remains. Mizutani stands, with low confidence.

### 02.04.19 娑羅覩邏邑 (Sālātura) — Lahor near Hund; name and distance fit, bearing disputed in the sources

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Lahor (Lavor) near Ohind, after Cunningham (agreed). Cunningham (p. 67): Lahor, 4
miles NE of Ohind; Beal (p. 114) and Dey: NW of Ohind; Vivien (p. 312): c. 20 li NE, no site; Watters: none.
**Evidence.** Cunningham (ASR II, p. 95) obtained Greek and Indo-Scythian coins at Lahor in 1848 and reads Lahor as
Śalātura. The text says NW; Cunningham's "NE" is not discussed (the ASR coder flags it). Route test on the coordinates
used here: 6.8 km at 289° from Hund for "20+ li NW" (predicted 4.8 km, 26° off), so the site lies W–NW, as the
text and Beal have it.
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Lahor (Lavor) near Ohind, after Cunningham (p. 263).
**Name.** 娑 is s (心), never ś, so Mizutani's śalātura, the grammarians' form (Kāśikā on Pāṇini 4.3.94), does not
match; 羅覩邏 fits la-tu-ra. Bhāmaha's sālāturīya, "the one from Sālātura" (= Pāṇini; Kāvyālaṅkāra 6.62), has the dental
s that 娑 requires: Xuanzang heard the local s-form, as the Gāndhārī merger of the sibilants would lead one to expect.
Medium confidence; the identification is unaffected (data/names/).
**Assessment.** Name, coins and distance agree; there is no inscription or monument. Mizutani stands (medium).

### 03.01 烏仗那國 (uḍḍiyāna) — the Swat valley; its sites are confirmed on the ground

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Swat basin (agreed). Vivien (p. 313), Cunningham (p. 94), Beal (p. 119), Watters
(p. 223, tentative) and Dey agree; they differ only on how far it extended (Panjkora, Bajaur, Buner, Dardistan).
**Evidence.** The Life (T50:0230b) puts the country along the Suvāstu (Swat). Himalayan Districts II (1884) repeats
Beal. Sites within it are confirmed on the ground: the inscribed footprint stone at Tirat (03.01.04) and Mount Ilam
(03.01.05). Route test: Hund to Swat 99 km for "600+ li N" (predicted 143 km), 2° off.
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the Swat country, taking in Panjkora, Bajaur, Swat and Buner (p. 271).
**Name.** 仗 is a retroflex (澄), so the characters give uḍḍiyāna/oḍḍiyāna, the first of Mizutani's forms, and exclude
udyāna "garden", his etymology; 烏荼 oḍḍa (10.09) has the same vernacular ḍḍ. Oḍḍiyāna is the form of the tantric texts
(e.g. the Sādhanamālā). Secure; the identification is unaffected (data/names/).
**Recent scholarship.** Baums (2019, p. 168) reports three Gāndhārī inscriptions of the 1st century AD naming the Oḍi
royal house, "almost certainly" Sanskrit Uḍḍiyāna, and Tira, named in two inscriptions from Swat connected with the
Oḍi kings (CKI 334, 401), as their capital or a major city. The Oḍi form from Swat itself has the vernacular ḍ that 仗
renders; Tira is unlocated and has no bearing on Xuanzang's capital (03.01.01a).
**Assessment.** Agreement, the river name and inscribed site evidence inside the country. Secure.

### 03.01.01b 蘇婆伐窣堵河 (subhavastu) — the Swat river; name continuity

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Swat river (agreed). Vivien (p. 313), Cunningham (p. 93; Arrian's Suastus), Beal
(p. 120), Watters (p. 223) and Dey agree.
**Evidence.** The name runs from Suvāstu/Soastos to Swat. The Life writes 蘇婆薩堵 (Suvāstu) and calls Apalāla's
spring its source (03.01.03).
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the Swat river, the Greeks' Soastos (p. 272).
**Assessment.** Name and agreement; no one dissents. Mizutani stands (medium).

### 03.01.04 如來足迹石 — the footprint stone at Tirat; an inscription says the footprints are the Buddha's

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Tirat village, W bank of the upper Swat, found by Deane, visited by Stein 1926
(agreed). Beal: none.
**Evidence.** Stein (AR 1925–26, p. 161) found the stone with "a short Kharoṣṭhī inscription" saying the footprints
are the Buddha's. Konow (CII 2.1, p. 8) edits the 11-letter inscription (c. mid-1st c. BC) on Deane's boulder and
checks Xuanzang's 30 li SW of Apalāla's spring at Kalam. The Life (T50:0230b) gives the same position.
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Tirat village on the W bank of the upper Swat; the stone is 1 m high and 0.87
m wide, with the Kharoṣṭhī label read by Konow (p. 277).
**Recent scholarship.** Bibi (2017, p. 65) reports that the footprint stone is now displayed in the Swat Museum. Stein
had seen beside it a large mound of a demolished stūpa, with a local tradition of a "Gumbat", no longer visible, and
Tucci found Buddhist sculpture in situ round it.
**Assessment.** An inscribed monument matching the description, in the right place; the stone has since been moved to
the Swat Museum (Bibi 2017). Secure.

### 03.01.04a 如來濯衣石 — the robe-washing rock downstream of Tirat (Stein)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: a large rock found by Stein on the right bank of the road from the lower Swat to
Tirat (agreed). Beal: none.
**Evidence.** Stein (AR 1925–26, p. 161) says the second site both pilgrims mention was also traced. Konow (CII 2.1,
p. 9) has only an estampage of a rock with natural cross-lines and traces of Kharoṣṭhī, of vague provenance, from one
of Deane's "unreliable" agents.
**Recent scholarship.** Bibi (2017, p. 65) describes Stein's rock on the right bank of the Swat, with Sanskrit and
Arabic graffiti, and reports after R. Khan (2014, pp. 96–97) that it "has been blasted some years back".
**Assessment.** It follows from the secure footprint stone (30 li downstream), and Stein reports finding it, but the
rock is not described in these files, Konow's estampage proves nothing, and the rock itself is reported destroyed
(Bibi 2017), so the identification can no longer be checked on the ground. Mizutani stands (medium).

### 03.01.05 醯羅山 — Mount Ilam (Stein); the mountain fits, the distance does not

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Mount Ilam (9,250 ft), c. 15 miles S of Mingora (Stein) (agreed). Beal and Watters:
none.
**Evidence.** Stein climbed it in 1926 (AR 1925–26, p. 163): still a place of Hindu and Muslim worship, with flat
rocks below the summit; first conjectured by Foucher. Route test: 19 km from Mingora for "400+ li S" (predicted
96 km), z −3.3, 35° off; Manglaor, the rival capital (03.01.01a), is no better.
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji doubts Ilām, which fits Xuanzang's distance badly (p. 279). He mentions the view that
the name means "bone" (haḍḍa) and points to Songyun's mountain with the 收骨寺, eight days SE of the royal city (Luoyang
qielan ji 5), whose distance and setting roughly match, but finds the evidence insufficient. Songyun's mountain is the
place of the body-offering to the tigress, which Xuanzang puts beyond the Indus (03.04.05), not the half-verse jātaka
told here, so the parallel is weak.
**Stein's route.** Stein (MASI 42, pp. 102–103) calls the distance, read as a direct one, "certainly wrong" (he writes
"500 li or approximately 80 miles" there, a slip for the "above 400 li" of the translation he quotes on the same
page), and reads it as the total of a roundabout itinerary: Manglaur – Mahāvana – Mo-yü – Girarai (Barandu valley) –
Karakar pass – Ilam, about 62 road miles by the sum of his stage figures (0.25 km per li, the normal value;
route/README.md §10, Test 4). He reconstructed that itinerary to fit the figure, so it explains the distance without
confirming it.
**Recent scholarship.** Olivieri (2016, p. 30) notes that Mount Ilam, the Ram-takht of the Hindus, keeps the Tibetan
toponym Hilo (after Olivieri 1996, p. 69 n. 33) and is called in Pashto Jogiano-sar, "Yogin's peak", with a folk tale
of a Buddhist master and a pupil who died trying to fly from the top. A name and a folk memory, not a monument.
**Assessment.** Stein's fieldwork matches the description and the cult, and no rival exists. The 400 li is far too
long for the direct way from any capital in the Swat valley, but fits Stein's roundabout itinerary. Mizutani stands,
medium.

### 03.01.09 薩裒殺地僧伽藍（珊尼羅闍川） — the Saniraja valley = Adinzai (Deane); the monastery unlocated

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the 珊尼羅闍川 is the Adinzai valley, joining the Swat at Chakdara (Deane) (agreed).
Dey: the Adinzai valley near Chakdarra (Stein). Beal and Watters: none.
**Evidence.** None in these files beyond Deane's and Stein's statements; the Suma stūpa in the same valley rests on
Deane's report of a surviving name (03.01.09a).
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Deane's Adinzai valley, joining the Swat at Chakdara (p. 285).
**Recent scholarship.** Bibi (2017, p. 67) agrees on Deane's Adinzai valley and reports R. Khan (2014, p. 106), who
names Andan Dheri and Sapar (Saparuna) as the probable sites of the monastery and its stūpa; no evidence is given.
**Assessment.** Two field men agree on the valley, but the monastery and its 80-ft stūpa are not identified on the
ground; Khan's Andan Dheri and Sapar, reported by Bibi without argument, are new candidates. Low confidence; nothing
contradicts Mizutani.

### 03.01.10 上軍王窣堵波 — the Shankardar stūpa; distance and bearing fit exactly; Nawe-kalai (Tucci) the rival

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Shankardar stūpa, E bank of the Swat c. 20 km S of Mingora (Stein, Mizuno) (agreed).
Beal (p. 126): E bank of the Swat; Watters: none.
**Evidence.** Natesa Aiyar (EI 18) reports Deane's view that the monument stands between Ghaligai and Shankardar;
the Kurram casket naming Ucharsana (= Uttarasena?) comes from Kurram, not Swat. Route test: 15.6 km SW of Mingora
for "60–70 li SW" (predicted 15.5 km), bearing exact.
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Stein's Shankardar stūpa on a hillock on the E bank of the Swat c. 20 km S of
Mingora (p. 286).
**Recent scholarship.** Filigenzi (2015, pp. 144, 222, 226) reports that Tucci (1958, pp. 294, 299–302) rejected
Deane's and Stein's Shingardar as highly unlikely and put the Uttarasena stūpa at Nawe-kalai (34°39′N, 72°11′E), where
excavation in the late 1980s found a Buddhist sacred area (Abdul Nasir and Faiz-ur-Rehman 1996, as Barabru), with rock
reliefs and traces of ancient canals nearby. She rejects Stein's reading of the Hindu-ghar relief C116 at Ghalegai as
Uttarasena the donor (it is late, comparable with Palola Ṣāhi bronzes), and does not decide between the stūpas. From
the study's points Nawe-kalai lies 21.7 km at 228° from Mingora (0.33 km per li for 60–70 li SW; Shankardar 15.6 km,
exact), and 28.8 km from Manglaor. Tucci is not read here.
**Assessment.** A standing stūpa on the E bank, as the text has it, at the right distance and bearing, with Deane,
Stein and Mizutani agreeing; nothing names it. Tucci's Nawe-kalai, reported by Filigenzi, is a rival with an excavated
Buddhist sacred area, a little long on the distance but on the right bearing. The authorities conflict and nothing
found names either site: open, Shankardar the better fit on the route, low confidence until Tucci's argument is read.

### 03.01.10a 白象化石 — the elephant rock near Ghalagai, beside the Shankardar stūpa

**Identifications.** Mizutani: a rock near Ghalagai, less than half a mile upstream of the Shankardar stūpa (Stein,
Mizuno) (agreed). Beal and Watters: none.
**Evidence.** Only Deane's location of the stūpa between Ghaligai and Shankardar (EI 18).
**Assessment.** It stands or falls with 03.01.10; nothing in these files describes the rock's shape. Low confidence;
nothing contradicts Mizutani.

### 03.01.16 達麗羅川 (darada) — the Darel valley on the Indus

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Darel/Dardistan, named from Darada (agreed); he also cites Stein's Gurez for the
Dards' old capital Daratpurī, which belongs to the Kashmir side, not to Xuanzang's valley. Cunningham (p. 95), Beal
(p. 134) and Watters (p. 237, tentative): Darel on the right bank of the Indus. Vivien (p. 316): perhaps the Tal
valley, more probably Darel.
**Evidence.** Himalayan Districts II (1884) and the 1874 volume repeat Beal ("Tha-li-lo … the modern Darel"). Konow
(CII 2.1) reports Stein's Darel opposite Chilas. Route test: 136 km NE of Mingora for "1000+ li" (predicted 239 km),
1° off. The Life gives it as hearsay.
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Darel, the valley on the W bank of the Indus; with Stein (on Rājataraṅgiṇī
ii.435) he puts the Dards' old capital Daratpurī at Gurez, and cites Faxian's 陀歷 (p. 297).
**Stein's route.** Stein (*Serindia* i, p. 6 n. 8) measures the route Darel – Mankial – down the Indus – Besham –
Ghorband – Manglaur at about 140 miles on the survey maps, to be increased by at least a third for the ground, and
finds the 1000 li "agrees well". On the map length alone the leg gives 0.23 km per li, the normal value; the route
test's shortfall comes from the straight line (route/README.md §10, Test 4). Faxian took 15 days over it.
**Name.** 達麗 fits da(r)-re, but 羅 is la/ra and gives the -da of Darada only if ḍ was heard as l, as Ji argues (羅
renders ḍḍ once, in Hiḍḍa). Read as they stand, the characters give *Darela, the modern valley name Darel (Faxian's 陀歷
also has l); no Sanskrit *Darela is attested, and the Sanskrit sources have only Darad/Darada (Kalhaṇa, Jonarāja).
Either reading leads to the same valley; medium confidence (data/names/).
**Kuwayama (2002).** Faxian's 陀歷 and Xuanzang's valley are the same; he places it where the Indus turns from W to SW,
"or around Chilas" (p. 145). Agrees.
**Recent scholarship.** Neelis (2011, pp. 273–275, 287) puts Da-li-luo in a valley of an upper Indus tributary between
Shatial and Chilas (Darel or Tangir) and stresses that no remains of the Maitreya shrine or its monasteries have been
found, though the upper Indus has abundant petroglyphs and Kharoṣṭhī and Brāhmī graffiti; Xuanzang's information was
secondhand. Miyaji (1988, pp. 116–117) gathers the textual witnesses to the image (Faxian; Baoyun and Fasheng in the
名僧伝, Fasheng seeing it "NE of Udyāna"; Xuanzang) and, after Kuwayama 1987, doubts that Xuanzang saw it. Bibi (2017, p.
68) reports the gold ornament of c. 16 kg found at Pattan in Indus Kohistan, which Farooq Swati and Nasim Khan linked
to the lost wooden Maitreya; Pattan lies downstream of Darel, not in it, and the link is conjecture. Tsuchiya (2010,
pp. 126–128), writing on Faxian's 陀歷, proposes the Pouguch (Poguch) site, on a terrace above Pouguch village on the
left bank of the Darel, 15.3 km above the Indus confluence, which Stein (1913) had called a fort (Lohilo Kot). Her
surface survey of 1998 found an irregular enclosure of red-earth walls (98, 25, 79 and 43 m), a gate 17 m wide with a
basin opposite, and a single mound of cut stones at the highest point, taken for the main temple; no barracks, so not
a fort. Interviews with seven villagers (January 2009; pp. 131–136) gave a Buddhist temple with a great image, mostly
"of gold", once "wooden", once 24 m high, which she takes for a tradition of Faxian's eight-zhang Maitreya, "a first
step" (p. 136). Nothing was dug, and no image, inscription or datable find is reported. Her English paper (Tsuchiya c.
2009, pp. 3115–3120; surveys 1998–2000 and 2003–06) adds Rajikot, a walled hill fort with a siphoned water supply, as
the legendary seat of the ruler who could have raised the image; argues from the valley's defences, all at its head
(the Darband gate 1 km S of Matic, a signal system from the Batakhun pass), that Pouguch near the mouth was its safest
place; and explains the wooden image by the conifer forests of the neighbouring valleys. Survey and legend again, no
excavation; Xuanzang is not discussed. Jettmar (1989, p. XL), introducing the *Antiquities of Northern Pakistan*, puts
the pilgrims' religious centre in Ta-li-lo (Darel, after Stein 1928), in a stretch of the Indus valley nominally
Darada but virtually independent, and suggested that Bīrūnī's famous "idol" of Shamil might be the wooden Maitreya. In
1993 (pp. 80, 112) he follows Cunningham's Darel, finds Faxian's 15 days down the Indus a fair fit, places the
sanctuary in the western part of the Indus valley near the emporium known from the Shatial inscriptions, and withdraws
the Bīrūnī link. Fussman (1994, p. 14 and n. 46) notes that no trace of the shrine has been found at Darel or near it,
and that nothing at Chilas or Shatial evokes it.
**Assessment.** Name and agreement, with the route bearing exact and the distance normal along the Indus road; no site
evidence, and the shrine itself has not been found (Neelis 2011; Fussman 1994). Tsuchiya's Pouguch enclosure is a
candidate site for the shrine, unexcavated; the oral tradition is not site evidence. Mizutani stands (medium).

### 03.02 鉢露羅國 (balūra) — the kingdom of Palola (Bolor): Baltistan or Gilgit; Chitral was once proposed

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Baltistan (agreed). Vivien (p. 316), Cunningham (p. 96), Beal (p. 135), Watters (p.
240, tentative) and Dey: Balti/Baltistan.
**Evidence.** Himalayan Districts II (1884) reports "two places that can answer to the name Bolor": Chitral
(Klaproth) or Baltistan (Cunningham). The text puts Bolor 500 li E of Darel, which rules out Chitral to the W.
Route test: 198 km for "500+ li E" (predicted 120 km), 10° off. Hearsay; the Life names it only on the return.
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees, following Mizutani: Baltistan, probably known by report only (pp. 299–300).
**Name.** 鉢 is p (幫), not b, so Mizutani's balūra/balōra does not fit; 露 is lo/lu and 羅 la/ra. The characters give
*Palola, the dynastic name of the Ṣāhis of Gilgit: printed Paṭola in the Hatun rock inscription (EI 30 no. 38) and in
a Gilgit manuscript colophon (śrīpaṭolaṣāhi, Mahāsannipātaratnaketudhāraṇī), but always Palola as a place name in the
rock inscriptions (von Hinüber 1989, Hodar no. 64; 2004, pp. 105–106; see Recent scholarship), and von Hinüber takes
鉢露羅 for its best Chinese transcription. Xuanzang's 露 (l) agrees. The form *Palola is settled by the inscriptions, now
checked here; what it means for the site is weighed in the assessment (data/names/).
**Kuwayama (1990, 2002).** 鉢露羅 is Zhimeng's 波倫, Bolor or Palur (2002, p. 145), reached by the 5th-century pilgrims
from Tashkurghan over the Naizatash, Ishkoman or Darkot passes (p. 146), which puts it at Gilgit (1990, p. 149). This
is the 5th-century Bolor on the pilgrims' road; he does not discuss Baltistan or the 500 li E of Darel.
**Recent scholarship.** Neelis (2011, pp. 176–177, 275) takes Xuanzang's Bo-lu-luo for the realm of the Palola Ṣāhis
of Gilgit (late 6th–mid 8th century), attested as Buddhist patrons in colophons, rock inscriptions and inscribed
bronzes, and unnamed by Xuanzang because his information was secondhand. In the 8th century Huichao distinguishes
Great Bolor (Tibetan) from Little Bolor (Chinese side, presumably the Palola Ṣāhis), and in 753 the Chinese took the
capital of Baltistan (Great Bolor) at Katsura near Skardu. He does not argue against Baltistan for the 7th century.
Von Hinüber (1989, pp. 64–65), in *Antiquities of Northern Pakistan* 1, edits the Hodar rock inscription (no. 64),
found by Jettmar in 1983 near Chilas, which names a king of the dynasty with the dynastic name in the form palola-,
not paṭola-, much closer to Bolor. In 2004 (pp. 105–106) he shows from rock inscriptions nos. 24–27 that Palola is a
country: a "resident of Palola" (palolavāstavya) and a Jaṭ "gone to Palola" at Dadam Das near Hodar, Yaśovarma with
Palola before his name at Shing Nala, two monks "of Palola" at Alam Bridge. The place name is always Palola, never
Patola, which first appears in the title of the second king, Vajrādityanandi, as a Sanskritisation; Palola is
confirmed by Xuanzang's transcription (the best in the Chinese sources), the Tang P'o-lü and Arabic Bolor/Balūr. These
are visitors' graffiti in the Indus valley: they name the country but do not place its seat. On the seat, Jettmar
(1989, pp. XIX, L; 1993, pp. 78–83) argues for Baltistan: Great Palur, with the residence perhaps in the Skardu basin,
the Gilgit valley (Little Palur, Bru-za) a conquered province ruled by viceroys such as Makarasiṃha of the Hatun
inscription. His evidence is 8th-century: Huichao (723–729), for whom the king had moved from Great to Little Palur
under Tibetan pressure, and the Tang annals' investiture of the king in Great Palur in 717; he adds *palolo* as the
Dardic neighbours' name for the Baltis, the Shigar inscription in Baltistan, and (1993, pp. 80–81) that Xuanzang's
Palur is hearsay, its shape long E–W fitting a kingdom from Gilgit to Baltistan. Fussman (1993, pp. 16–17) replies
that a Skardu capital could be settled only by Patola Ṣāhi inscriptions at or near Skardu, and the site of ancient
Gilgit is itself unknown. Von Hinüber (2004, pp. 7–8, 56, 69–71) rejects Jettmar's delimitations of Balūr as
premature; he dates the dynasty from c. 600 to the early 8th century, with all its dated royal inscriptions from the
Gilgit valley (Hatun, 19 December 671; Danyor, NE of Gilgit, redated to 19 February 687), identifies the kings of the
Tang annals' Great Bolor (embassies 696–741) as Palola Ṣāhis, and shows that the Shigar inscription names another king
(of Kuñjāna/Kuljāna) and dates from the 6th century, unrelated to the Palola material.
**Assessment.** Agreement and bearing; no site evidence. The name is settled: 鉢露羅 renders Palola, which the
inscriptions use for a country (von Hinüber 1989, 2004), the kingdom of the Palola Ṣāhis, who ruled from c. 600 to the
early 8th century and so in Xuanzang's time. The seat is not settled. All their dated royal inscriptions (Hatun 671,
Danyor 687) and manuscripts come from the Gilgit valley, and the one inscription from Baltistan once adduced (Shigar)
names another king and is older (von Hinüber 2004); Neelis (2011) and Kuwayama take Bolor as Gilgit. For Baltistan
stand Jettmar and Ji, on the 8th-century Great Bolor of Huichao and the Tang annals, whose kings von Hinüber also
takes for Palola Ṣāhis, and the bearing: Skardu lies at the text's bearing but at twice the distance (198 km for 500+
li), Gilgit at the distance (c. 82 km) but 24° off to the NE. Fussman's test, a Palola Ṣāhi inscription at or near
Skardu, is unmet. The kingdom is identified; whether Xuanzang's informants meant its Baltistan or its Gilgit side is
open. Low confidence, since the country is hearsay and the seat undecided.

### 03.03.02 醫羅鉢呾羅龍王池 (elāpattra) — the Baba Wali (Panja Sahib) spring at Hasan Abdal

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Bābā Walī/Panja Sahib spring at Hasan Abdal (agreed). Beal (p. 137): the same,
after Cunningham. Watters: none.
**Evidence.** Cunningham (ASR II, pp. 135–39), visiting in 1848 and 1864, found direction and distance from
Shah-dheri right, read the Sikh and Muslim spring legends as successors of the Nāga legend, and traced a ruined
monastery 200 ft square and a stūpa near the cypress garden. Rawalpindi 1883, 1895 and Attock 1909 repeat him.
Route test: 14 km NW of Taxila for "70+ li" (predicted 17 km), 12° off.
**Foucher (1942–47).** The spring of Hasan Abdāl, two stages from the Indus across the Chach plain, still venerated
(I, p. 42).
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji gives the name (Elāpattra; Pāli Erāpatha) and the Bharhut relief of the nāga but no
site, and does not mention Hasan Abdal (pp. 302–303).
**Assessment.** Distance, bearing, a living spring cult and Buddhist ruins beside it. Secure.

### 03.04.x1 白衣外道本師悟所求理初說法處 — the Jain founder's place: temple C at Ketas (Cunningham) or Murti (Stein)

**Identifications.** Sources: Cunningham (ASR V, p. 92): the large ruined temple C at Ketas, basement 68½ × 56½ ft,
older than the Kashmiri-style temples above it.
**Evidence.** The text puts the place "not far" from the stone stūpa SE of the capital (03.04.03). For that stūpa the
study prefers Stein's Murti in the Gandhala valley, 4½ miles from Ketas: an excavated stūpa, over 30 camel-loads of
Jaina-style sculpture, and a temple locally called Jaina (Jhelam 1907). Cunningham's temple C is a reading of a
basement; 03.04.02 reads the same structure as a stūpa.
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji gives no site. Since Mahāvīra taught only in Middle India, he takes the claim that
the founder first preached near the Siṃhapura capital for a hearsay accretion (p. 316), part of his case that Xuanzang
knew the country by report (03.04). That bears on the legend, not on where Xuanzang's informants pointed.
**Recent scholarship.** Dar and Hameed (2018, pp. 53–54, 65–66) agree with Stein on Murti, whose stūpa was in its last
phase probably Jaina, with a Hindu temple beside it and Murti red-sandstone sculpture taken to Lahore. They also say
that the Katas stūpa base (03.04.02) was already in Jaina and Hindu hands in Xuanzang's time, so both sites show Jaina
use; they do not place the founder's spot itself.
**Assessment.** Murti offers Jaina remains beside a stūpa; Ketas offers only an undated basement. Open, with Murti
the better-supported alternative.

### 03.06 迦濕彌羅國 (kaśmīra) — Kashmir; no dispute

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Kashmir, entered by the W gate at Baramula (agreed). Vivien (p. 322), Cunningham
(pp. 103–5), Beal (p. 148), Watters (p. 261) and Dey agree; Cunningham extends the kingdom to the Salt Range.
**Evidence.** Route test: 172 km from Uraśā for "1000+ li SE" (predicted 239 km), 46° off; the Life gives the same
route through the stone gate.
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Kashmir, the oval Jhelum valley c. 85 × 25 miles, the 7,000-li circuit
perhaps including dependencies; he reports Chavannes' equation of the Han 罽賓 with Kaspeiria (pp. 321–322).
**Route along the Jhelum.** Stein's *Memoir* (1899, pp. 24–25, 85–86) gives the road: about 39 miles from the Mansahra
area to Muzaffarabad (from Albiruni), 84 road miles on to Baramula (Drew; six marches), and two marches to Srinagar.
Drew's route table (*Jummoo and Kashmir Territories*, 1875, App. IV, route 8) gives the whole road from Abbottabad by
Muzaffarabad and Baramula to Srinagar as 156½ miles in 11 marches. That is 252 km for the 1000 li (0.25 km per li, the
normal value), about 91 li a march (route/README.md §10, Test 4).
**Kuwayama (1990).** Xuanzang's note on the name, 「迦濕彌羅國（舊曰罽賓，訛也）」, shows that 罽賓 = Kashmir was the translators'
equation (Enomoto: the *Divyāvadāna*'s Kaśmīrapura is 罽賓 in the 阿育王傳), not a geographical fact for the 4th–5th
century, when the Buddhist 罽賓 was Gandhāra (02.04) (pp. 119–121). In the Sui period 罽賓 was Kashmir (the one country
that did not answer Yangdi's summons, 唯罽賓不至), and in the Tang it was Kāpiśī (pp. 702–711). This adds to Chavannes' Han
罽賓 = Kaspeiria, reported by Ji, without bearing on the identification.
**Recent scholarship.** Yatoo (2012, pp. 60, 66, 248–249), after Stein's *Rājataraṅgiṇī*, has Xuanzang come from
Uraśā, enter Kashmir at Baramula and spend the night at Hushkapura, modern Ushkar: the Life's monastery 護瑟迦羅, where he
stayed after passing the stone gate (T50:0231a). Ushkar, in Baramula town, is an excavated Buddhist site (Garrick
1882, later Sahni) with rubble structures and plain terracotta floor tiles comparable with Harwan and Kanispora, which
Yatoo dates to the 1st–5th centuries AD; no inscription names it. Wukong took the same road in 759.
**Assessment.** Name and agreement, and the distance normal along the Jhelum road. The Life's first stop, 護瑟迦羅 =
Huṣkapura, is Stein's Ushkar at Baramula, an excavated Buddhist site (Yatoo 2012), which agrees with the W gate at
Baramula; the equation rests on the name, and no inscription names the site. Mizutani stands (medium).

### 03.06.01a 迦濕彌羅國大都城 — Srinagar (Pravarapura)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Srinagar, Pravarasena's city (agreed). Vivien (p. 322), Cunningham (p. 107), Beal (p.
158) and Majumdar (p. 680: Pravarapura) agree. Watters (p. 261) names no site.
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Srinagar, Pravarasena's new capital Pravarapura (the New Tang's 拔邏勿布城) on the

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Pandrethan, 2 miles SE of Takht-i-Sulaiman (agreed). Vivien (p. 322), Cunningham (pp.
107–8), Beal (p. 158) and Majumdar (p. 680) agree.

### 03.07 半笯嗟國 (parṇotsa) — Punch; name survives, distance short in a straight line

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Punch, reached W via Baramula and Uri (agreed). Julien (p. 187), Vivien (p. 322),
Cunningham (p. 147), Beal (p. 163), Watters (p. 281), Majumdar (p. 680) and Dey agree.
**Evidence.** Kashmiri Prünts < Parṇotsa (Stein). IAR 1985–86 (p. 35) explored the Mughal route through
Rajouri–Poonch (Kushan ware, Kashmiri coins, temple ruins at Panjnara), without naming Xuanzang's town. Route test:
70 km from the Kashmir point for "700+ li SW" (predicted 167 km), flagged short; the road via Baramula is longer.
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Punch, between the Jhelum and the Pir Panjal, noting that its area falls far
short of the 2,000-li circuit (pp. 348–349).
**Stein's route.** Stein (*Memoir*, 1899, pp. 17, 81, 400) thinks Xuanzang "in all probability" took the Toṣamaidān
pass. He reckons that road at "about sixty English miles" from Loharin to Srinagar and marched it himself in 1892 in
about 2½ days (*Rājataraṅgiṇī* ii, Note E on iv. 177, pp. 293–298); Loharin to Punch down the Loharin river and the
Tohi adds about 16 miles (our reckoning from his figures). The other road, by Baramula, Uri and the Haji pass, is 88
miles in 7 marches in Drew's route table (1875, App. IV, route 6). That gives 0.175–0.20 km per li, at the low end of
the normal range, against 0.10 in a straight line (route/README.md §10, Test 4).
**Assessment.** Name and agreement; the distance is short in a straight line but near normal along the passes.
Mizutani stands (medium).

### 03.08 曷邏闍補羅國 (rājapura) — Rajauri

281), Majumdar (p. 683) and Dey agree.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Rajaori, after Cunningham, whose circuit (c. 300 miles) is about half

### 04.01.x6 磔迦國東境大城 — Lahore (Cunningham); the study's Chiniyari undercuts it

**Identifications.** Sources: Cunningham, via Lahore 1883 and 1916: Lahore.

### 04.02.03 答秣蘇伐那僧伽藍 (tamasāvana) — Sultanpur (Lodhi); fits Jalandhar, not Cīnabhukti

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sultanpur, c. 50 km SSE of Amritsar (Cunningham), reading the Life's 50 li for the
Xiyu ji's 500 (agreed). Beal (p. 174, tentative): Sultanpur. Vivien (p. 333): the Beas–Sutlej confluence. Cunningham
(1871, p. 230) and Watters: no site.
**Evidence.** Cunningham (ASR XIV, pp. 55–59): a black mud quagmire ("dark forest"), a 30-ft mound with the Badshahi
serai, whose walls he suspects stand on monastery foundations, large bricks, a female figure and a Gaṇeśa mould; no
Buddhist find. Route test: from Chiniyari 73 km for "50+ li SE" (predicted 12 km, z +3.7); onward to Jalandhar 39 km
for "140–150 li NE" (predicted 35 km). The best-fitting chain takes Vivien's confluence, which fits no better.
**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Cunningham's Sultānpur (Raghunathpura), reading the Life's 50 li with Hori
against Adachi's 150 li (pp. 369–370). He places it "50 li and more SE of Amritsar", which Sultanpur, c. 55 km away,
is not.
**Name.** The characters give Tamasāvana: 蘇 is usually su but also sā in the key, so Mizutani's *tamasu- is not
needed, and the gloss 闇林 is tamas + vana. The form is attested in the MSV Bhaiṣajyavastu (the Buddha's northern
journey, with Rohitaka and Apalāla) and the Divyāvadāna (256), which names Tamasāvana beside Kāśmīrapura: the
Sarvāstivāda tradition to which Xuanzang assigns this monastery. High confidence for the name; neither text fixes a
site (data/names/).
**Assessment.** Sultanpur fits the leg to Jalandhar, but neither the 50 li nor the 500 li from Chiniyari. Low;
Mizutani stands for want of better. The monastery's name is now attested (Tamasāvana, in the MSV and the Divyāvadāna),
but those texts place it only in the north-west, beside Kashmir, and give no site.

### 04.13.x1 龍池側窣堵波 — the Chhatar stūpa at Ahichhatra, excavated with its four small stūpas

**Identifications.** Sources: Cunningham (ASR I, pp. 260–61): the Chhatar (Pisanhari-ka-chhatar) mound c. 1 mile W of
the fort; Rohilkhand 1879, Bareilly 1911 follow.
**Evidence.** Cunningham dug a solid brick stūpa 40 ft high and found a steatite box with beads, pearl and rock
crystal; four of the eight mounds round it are stūpas, answering the text's four small stūpas; he found no trace of
the Nāga tank. Rohilkhand 1879 links the name Ahi-chhatra and the Adi Ahir cobra legend to the stūpa. Ahicchattra
itself is secure (04.13).
**Assessment.** An excavated stūpa with the four satellites the text describes, at a secure city; only the tank is
missing. Secure.
