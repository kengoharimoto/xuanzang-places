# Notes on disputed places: juan 2 and juan 3

Range: Lampāka, Nagarahāra, Gandhāra (juan 2); Udyāna, Balūra, Taxila, Siṃhapura, Uraśā, Kashmir, Punach,
Rājapura (juan 3). 47 notes, in master-table order.

Gazetteer volumes used here (each counted once, though most exist in two scans): Peshawar 1883 and 1899;
Rawalpindi 1883 and 1895; Attock 1909; Jhelam 1907; Dehra Dun 1911; Punjab (Rose's Glossary) 1919; Himalayan
Districts II 1884; an unidentified volume of 1874; Bombay Gazetteer XIV (Thana, Places) 1882. Hazara, Shahpur and
Kashmir volumes give no hits in this range. Most of the Panjab passages copy Cunningham, from the *Ancient
Geography* or ASR II and V. The one body of independent argument is Stein's work at Ketas and Murti, which the
Jhelam gazetteer (1907) prints at length.

The Archaeological Survey reports bear on Gandhāra, Taxila and Siṃhapura: Cunningham's ASR II (report for
1863–64), V (1872–73) and XIV (1878–79), and Garrick's report in ASR XIX (1881–82) on Charsadda. Many of the
Peshawar and Rawalpindi gazetteer passages echo ASR II word for word. Cunningham's own view moved at four places:
Varuṣa (Palodheri → Shahbaz Garhi), Siṃhapura (Malot → Ketas in the 1871 book → Malot), the Kunāla stūpa (Karmal
→ the Meri hill) and the stūpa N of the body-offering (Ventura's tope too late → Ventura's tope). Udyāna, Uraśā and
Kashmir have no survey passages in this range.

The Director-General's Annual Reports (1903–37) settle much of Gandhāra and Taxila. Spooner's excavation of
Shāh-ji-ki-Dheri (1908–10) found Kaniṣka's inscribed casket, which locates the stūpa and puts the monastery in the
west mound beside it. Marshall's work at Taxila (1912–16) turned up two inscriptions naming Takṣaśilā (a third came
in 1923–24), cleared the Kushan walls of Sirsukh, dated Cunningham's head-gift stūpa, and assigned the head-gift and
Kunāla stūpas to Bhallar and the Hathiāl ridge. Stein rejected Sahri-Bahlol and found Butan for Ekaśṛṅga (1911–12),
and earlier found Mount Banj for the body-offering (1904–05). Hargreaves examined Mangali for Uraśā and Hund for
Udabhāṇḍa (1923–24). Swat (the Tirāt footprint stone, Mount Ilam; Stein 1925–26) and Kāpiśī also appear, but there
they confirm what Mizutani already holds. Charsadda has no Annual Report passage in this range.

The inscriptions (Epigraphia Indica; Konow's Kharoshthī volume, CII 2.1, 1929) add names on the ground. At Taxila
four records found on the site name Takṣaśilā (the Patika plate, the Sihila vase, the Mahal ladles, the Dharmarājikā
scroll). A ladle from Bedadi near Mansehra names "the Uraśa kingdom", putting Uraśā in Hazara. At Tirath in upper
Swat the footprint stone is inscribed "the footprints of the Buddha Śākyamuni". A well-gift "in the Vajra-stūpa",
brought in as from Mount Banj, gives the body-offering site a dated Buddhist foundation. Konow re-edits the Kaniṣka
casket, and Sahni publishes an 8th-century Śāradā slab from Hund. The Lakkha Mandal praśasti (EI 1) names a
Siṃhapura kingdom but was found far to the east. Hidda (Hadda, 02.03.05), Peshawar (02.04.01a), Darel (03.01.16) and
the Shankardar stūpa (03.01.10, through the Kurram casket's "Ucharsana", a guess on an object from elsewhere)
appear too, but only repeat identifications that Mizutani already holds as agreed, so they get no note. *Indian
Archaeology – A Review* (1953–2001) covers India only and has nothing on juan 2–3.

Recent scholarship (after 1985) is cited by author, year and page from `coded/lit_batch*.tsv` (texts and bibliography
in `literature.tsv`). Works known only through them (such as Errington 1993, Falk 2003, Nasim Khan 2006, Tucci 1958,
R. Khan 2014) have not been read here.

### 02.02a 濫波國大都城 — capital unknown; Mizutani's only candidate lies outside Lampāka

**Identifications.** Mizutani: capital unknown; "possibly Begram on the Logar river 10 km SE of Kabul" (after
Mizuno) (uncertain). Vivien (p. 301): Mandrâour (Mendrâwar), in the angle where the Alingar joins the Kabul river
(tentative). No one else names a site.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Names no capital. In October 1925 he saw Buddhist foundations at the Alingar–Alishang
confluence (Shāmatī) and large mounds on both banks below it near Mandrāwar, which he takes for one of Alexander's
Nikaias, not for Xuanzang's city (I, p. 150).

**Assessment.** Mizutani's candidate does not fit the region. Cunningham (p. 50) and Beal (p. 90) bound Lampāka as the
tract north of the Kabul river between the Alingar and the Kunar, and a Begram on the Logar near Kabul lies well to
the west of that. The master table already flags the quotation as odd. Vivien's Mandrawar is the only candidate inside
Lampāka; Foucher's large mounds there show an old town, but he ties them to Alexander, not to Xuanzang. The capital
stays unidentified, and Mizutani's suggestion should be treated as doubtful (possibly a slip in his source).

### 02.03 那揭羅曷國 (nagarahāra) — the Jalalabad district; no real dispute

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Jalalabad and the southern Kabul-river basin around it (uncertain; his wording is
hedged, but he names no rival). Vivien (p. 302), Cunningham (p. 51), Beal (p. 91), Watters (p. 184) and Dey all put
it in the Jalalabad district/Nangnihar. Cunningham and Beal add Ptolemy's Nagara (Dionysopolis). Majumdar (p. 674)
gives no view of his own.

**Gazetteers.** Two volumes (Himalayan Districts II 1884; unidentified 1874) mention Nagarahāra only in passing, as
subject to Kapiśa. They add nothing.

**Inscriptions.** Thomas (EI 9, p. 146) takes the "Nagara" of the Mathura lion capital to be probably Nagarahāra,
referring to Watters. That concerns the name only, not the site.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Agrees; for the city see 02.03.01a (I, p. 152).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the Jalalabad region on the S bank of the Kabul river, from the Jagdalak pass
to the Khyber (p. 221).

**Kuwayama (1990, 2002).** Jalalabad, as Mizutani. For the Chinese pilgrims of the 4th–5th centuries Nagarahāra was
the western end of Buddhist India, reached as a step beyond Gandhāra (1990, pp. 232–262; 2002, pp. 145–146). He takes
several 5th-century names for it: Tanwujie's 月氏國 across the 辛頭那提河 to the west, where he worshipped the 佛肉髻骨 (a western
月氏國, as against Gandhāra); the 「北天竺月氏國那竭呵城」 of Huiyuan's informant on the shadow; Buddhabhadra's birthplace, 「北天竺那呵利城」
in the 出三藏記集 and 迦維羅衛 in his biographies (against Tsukamoto's Kapilavastu); and Zhimeng's 迦維羅衛國, 1,300 li SW of
Gandhāra, where he saw the Buddha's hair, tooth, uṣṇīṣa bone and shadow. That last he reads as a copyist's corruption
of a name beginning 那迦… (Nagara-), the 那 lost and the rest assimilated to Kapilavastu, which had none of these relics
(1990, pp. 245–251, 328–330, n. 7).

**Assessment.** Every authority agrees. Mizutani's "uncertain" reflects his hedged phrasing rather than any
competing site.

### 02.03.01a 那揭羅曷國大都城 — Begram W of Jalalabad (Cunningham) vs Simpson's site at the Surkhab–Kabul junction

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Dasht-i-Begrām near Chahār-bāgh, SW of Jalalabad (agreed; no authority named).
Cunningham (p. 51–52): Begram, c. 2 miles W of Jalalabad. Vivien (p. 306): the ruins at Begram next to the village
of Nagrak, and explicitly not Balabagh. Against them, Simpson's site: Beal (p. 91) puts the town "in the angle
formed by the junction of the Surkhar and Kabul rivers", and Watters (p. 187, tentative) puts it at the Nagar
fortress on Simpson's rock (= Babur's Adinapur). Majumdar (p. 675) reports Simpson's Bala Hissar ruins c. 4 miles E
of Jalalabad without choosing.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Foucher (1942–47).** After six weeks in the field in 1923, Foucher fixed the city from two secure points, Hadda
(Hi-lo) and the shadow cave at the Syāh-sang gap: together they put it "dans la banlieue Sud-Ouest de la ville
actuelle, là même où la tradition locale conserve le souvenir d'un ancien « Bégrâm »", not at Simpson's confluence
site (I, p. 152). He gives the result as provisional.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji reports Cunningham's Dasht-i-Begram, c. 2 miles SW of Jalalabad, and Simpson's
tableland a little W of it at the Kabul–Surkhab confluence, and chooses neither (p. 221).

**Kuwayama (1990, 2002).** The city is "probably" Shāh Naṣr Ghundai at Chahār-bāgh, c. 10 km SW of the town of
Jalalabad, with many monastery sites on the hill-foots and terraces round it (1990, n. 28, pp. 1490–1491, from the
Kyoto University distribution survey, Mizuno ed. 1970, fig. 24). In 2002 he puts it "some 10 kilometers to the west of
modern Jalalabad bazaar" and reports fragments of Mathura sandstone sculpture he collected there in 1964 and 1972 (p.
90); red-ware imports from the same mound occur in Tapa Skandar Period I (p. 177). He gives no argument from the text.
The mound lies in Mizutani's "Dasht-i-Begrām near Chahār-bāgh", but it is not Cunningham's point: Cunningham's Begram
lay "about 2 miles to the west of Jalalabad, and 5 or 6 miles to the W.N.W. of Hidda" (p. 51), at the near end of
Masson's plain of tumuli. On the round figures alone (Hadda c. 8 km S of Jalalabad), Hadda would lie c. 9 km due E of
a mound 10 km SW of the town, and c. 14 km SE of one 10 km W: the first fits the text's 30+ li but not its SE
(城東南三十餘里至醯羅城), the second the bearing but not the distance. From Cunningham's spot Hadda lies c. 9 km SSE, which fits
both.

**Recent scholarship.** Errington (2017, pp. 16, 153–156), editing Masson's records, sets out three placings without
choosing: Masson's Dasht of Begram, a plain of tumuli 1½–2 miles W of Jalalabad; Simpson's (1881) site further NW,
chosen from the name echoes Naghrat and Nagara Ghundi rather than from the mounds; and the Kyoto survey of 1965
(Mizuno 1970–1, fig. 24), further W still, taking in the Ashrak Burj, the mounds and Chahar Bagh. Her site list gives
"Mound of Shah Nasr" as a name of Masson's Tepe Ashrak (Burj Jemadar), a large unexcavated mound "a little north of
Chahar Bagh" across the Sultanpur ravine (Ball and Gardin no. 261: 34°26′N 70°25′E, c. 3 km W of Jalalabad on those
rounded coordinates, nearer than Kuwayama's 10 km), and Mizuno tentatively took it for the c. 9 m stūpa Xuanzang saw
inside the city. Kuwayama's Shāh Naṣr Ghundai is therefore Masson's Tepe Ashrak, and the Kyoto city is drawn round it.
Masson's late treatise (BL G41, edited pp. 19–20) already put the city in the plain W of Jalalabad.

**Assessment.** The authorities split between the Begram plain (Cunningham, Vivien, Mizutani, Kuwayama) and Simpson's
junction site (Beal, Watters). Foucher's argument from Hadda and the shadow cave, both secure, favours Begram, though
nothing has been dug there. Kuwayama names a mound, Shāh Naṣr Ghundai, inside Mizutani's area, on survey and surface
finds rather than excavation or the text; on the round distances it fits the bearing to Hadda less well than
Cunningham's nearer spot, and his own two statements (10 km SW, 10 km W) differ. Errington (2017) shows the mound to
be Masson's Tepe Ashrak, Mizuno's in-city stūpa, still unexcavated; its distance from Jalalabad is uncertain (Ball and
Gardin's c. 3 km W against Kuwayama's 10 km and Masson's "a little north of Chahar Bagh"). The capital stays on the
Begram plain W/SW of Jalalabad; which mound is open. Mizutani is strengthened, medium confidence.

### 02.03.02 燃燈佛受記窣堵波 — Ahin-Posh (Higuchi) or the "Nagara Goondee" tope (Masson, Simpson)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: possibly the Ahin-Pōsh stūpa E of the old city (after Higuchi) (uncertain). Watters
(p. 187, tentative): the "Nagara Goondee" tope c. 3 furlongs E/SE of the Nagar rock (after Masson and Simpson).

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Foucher (1923) is the earlier source of the Ahin-Pōsh proposal, offered as a working
hypothesis. Measured from his Begram, the Life's SE suits it better than the Xiyu ji's E; the dome, over 75 m round on
the cliff edge, resembles the Dharmarājikā at Taxila; and Simpson's coins of 1879 (Domitian, Trajan, early Kushans)
show a rebuilding in the 2nd century (I, p. 152).

**Kuwayama (1990).** Only the texts: Faxian's 受記處 2 li E of the city and the Life's 城東二里 (pp. 258–260). He names no
site.

**Recent scholarship.** Errington (2017, pp. 41, 154–159) reports a third candidate: Mizuno (1970–1, pp. 112–113)
tentatively took Tepe Khwaja Lahori, a huge mound near the Kabul river on the skirt of Masson's Begram (base c. 549 m
round, diaper-masonry parapets, caves, coins and funeral jars; unexcavated), for the Aśoka stūpa of the Dīpaṅkara
story. On Ball and Gardin's rounded coordinates (34°27′N 70°23′E) it lies W/NW of Tepe Ashrak, Mizuno's in-city stūpa
(02.03.01a), which sits ill with a stūpa 2 li E of the city. For Watters' Nagara Ghundi she gives Jenkins' tunnel of
1878 (a stray Apollodotus coin only) and Simpson's inner wall, probably an earlier core stūpa, in a base c. 36.6 m
square: undated, and nothing but the name ties it to Dīpaṅkara. For Ahin-Posh she publishes Simpson's relic cell of
1879: ashes, a gold amulet case and 18 gold coins of Wima Kadphises, Kaniṣka I and Huviṣka with Roman aurei of
Domitian, Trajan and Sabina (to AD 137), a Kushan deposit, as Foucher said. She notes that Xuanzang's Aśokan
attribution "has not been verified on the ground".

**Assessment.** Each candidate depends on where its proponent puts the capital (02.03.01a): Foucher and Higuchi
measure from Begram, Watters from Simpson's rock. With Begram now favoured, Ahin-Pōsh is the better candidate, but it
remains a hypothesis. The gazetteers do not change it. Mizuno's Tepe Khwaja Lahori (via Errington 2017) is a third,
unexcavated candidate measured from the Kyoto city; none of the three is tied to Dīpaṅkara by a find.

### 02.03.03 瞿波羅龍窟（佛影窟） — the Shadow Cave: somewhere in the cliffs S/SW of Jalalabad

**Identifications.** Mizutani: among the Syāh-sang rocks S of Chahār-bāgh, along the cliffs S of Jalalabad; which
cave cannot be told (after Higuchi) (uncertain). Watters (p. 193, tentative): the E bank of a torrent in the hills
SW of Padmapur; the range running SW from the Ahin Posh tope towards Sultanpur is acceptable; the monastery is
perhaps Gunda Chismeh.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Faxian, Songyun and Xuanzang lead "de façon sûre" to the Syāh-sang gap S of Chahār-bāgh. The
cave itself is lost in the collapsing conglomerate, but half a dozen monasteries and twice as many stūpas lie within
500 m (I, p. 152).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: among the black rock hills S of Chahār-bāgh, in the cliffs S of Jalalabad,
after Higuchi (p. 227).

**Kuwayama (1990, 2002).** No site on the ground. He sets Xuanzang's route (20+ li SW of the city to a small rocky
ridge with a monastery; SW of it a gorge with a waterfall; the cave in the E cliff) beside Faxian's half yojana S of
the city, facing SW, and beside the setting given in the 觀佛三昧海經 (T15 no. 643): 「那乾訶羅國古仙山薝蔔華林毒龍池側青蓮華泉北羅刹穴中阿那斯山巖南」
(1990, pp. 270–273). He argues that the long shadow narrative in that sūtra is an insertion composed by its translator
Buddhabhadra, a native of Nagarahāra, from his own knowledge, and that Buddhabhadra with Faxian informed Huiyuan's
painted copy of the shadow at Lushan (412) (1990, pp. 311–337; 2002, pp. 117–118, 146). The cave is for him a local
creation on the pattern of the Apalāla story of Uḍḍiyāna. Daofu, Baoyun, Zhimeng and Daoyao (440s) saw the shadow; by
Xuanzang's time it was rarely visited (2002, pp. 118–119, after the Life). He doubts Mustamandi's reading of chapel H
at Tapa Shotor (Hadda) as the Gopāla story (2002, pp. 94–95).

**Assessment.** Watters, Higuchi and Foucher put the cave in the same range of hills south-west of the city, and
Foucher's count of monasteries and stūpas round the Syāh-sang gap marks the holy place. None can point to the cave
itself. Kuwayama's sūtra topography is a 5th-century description of the setting, not a location, and leaves the area
as it is. Mizutani is strengthened as to the place.

### 02.04.02 卑鉢羅樹 — the pipal tree: just N of Shāh-ji-ki-Dheri, where a Hindu shrine now stands; the tree is gone

**Identifications.** Mizutani gives no site (unknown), but his note places the Kaniṣka stūpa (Shāh-ji-ki-Dheri)
just south of the tree. Cunningham (p. 91–92, tentative): 8–9 li SE of Peshawar, probably the "stupendous tree" of
Begram that Babur saw in 1505, which has since disappeared.

**Survey.** The argument first appears in Cunningham's ASR II (report for 1863–64, pp. 88–89): "a great Pipal tree,
at 8 or 9 li, or 1½ mile, to the south-east of the city", Songyun's Bodhi tree, seen by Babur in 1505 and not
mentioned by Abul Fazl in 1594, so "it had previously disappeared through simple old age and decay". No fieldwork
is reported.

**Excavations.** Spooner (AR 1908–09, in the Shāh-ji-ki-Dheri report) follows Foucher: the tree "is not to be
found", but "to the north of Shah-ji-ki-Dheri, in the place where one would naturally look for this tree, is a
small octagonal temple still the scene of Hindu worship". Nothing was dug there.

**Gazetteers.** Peshawar 1883 and 1899 echo ASR II almost word for word ("the holy pipal tree, at 8 or 9 *li*, or
1½ mile, to the south-east of the city"), with the Babur and Abul Fazl dating. They also note that no remains of
the stūpa beside it survive.

**Kuwayama (1990, 2002): the bowl's 寶臺.** Mizutani's section covers the bowl as well as the tree (仏鉢および卑鉢羅樹). Xuanzang
saw only its base: 「王城內東北有一故基，昔佛鉢之寶臺也」 (the Life: 王城東北有置佛鉢寶臺). Kuwayama makes the 寶臺 a building other than a stūpa in
the northern part of Puruṣapura. Xuanzang always writes 窣堵波 for a stūpa, and Daoxuan's biography of Xuanzang calls it
a 鉢廟. Faxian, who saw the bowl here in 402 (403 in 2002, p. 144), says that a Yuezhi king built a 塔 and a 僧伽藍 for 700
monks where the bowl could not be moved, and that the monks brought it out at midday and in the evening, which a solid
stūpa would not allow. Zhu Fawei and Zhi Sengzai (*Shuijing zhu* 2; *Yiwen leiju* 73) describe a 浮圖 of seven storeys
with the bowl, hung on gold chains, in the middle of the second storey, and life-size gold and silver images on the
four walls (1990, pp. 161–175; 2002, pp. 21–26). Dao'an's *Shishi Xiyu ji* put the bowl NW of the 揵陀越王城 (1990, p.
173), against Xuanzang's NE; the 2002 version is less sure of that reading (pp. 25–26) and leaves it "either NE or NW"
(p. 120). He rejects Rosenfield's view that the bowl was kept in the Kaniṣka stūpa, which all the pilgrims put E or SE
outside the city (1990, p. 174, n. 17; 2002, p. 26). He dates the bowl in Gandhāra to before 310, through Fotudeng and
Dao'an (1990, pp. 189–191), and doubts Xuanzang's report that it was then in Persia (1990, p. 602; 2002, p. 37). He
proposes no site for the base.

**Assessment.** The site of the tree follows from the excavated stūpa (02.04.03): it stood immediately north of
Shāh-ji-ki-Dheri, c. 1½ miles SE of Peshawar. The Hindu shrine on that spot may carry on its sanctity, as Foucher and
Spooner suggest, but that is an inference, and nothing of the tree remains. The bowl's base, the other object of
Mizutani's section, stood inside the old city in its northern part (NE in both the Xiyu ji and the Life), not at the
Kaniṣka stūpa outside it; it is unlocated under Peshawar.

### 02.04.03 迦膩色迦大窣堵波 — Shāh-ji-ki-Dheri, settled by Kaniṣka's inscribed casket (Spooner 1908–09)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Shāh-ji-ki-Dheri, c. 1 km SE of Peshawar, excavated by Spooner in 1908–09 (agreed).
Vivien (p. 308): under a lieue SE of Peshawar, no site named. Cunningham (p. 92): "No remains of this great stupa now
exist". Majumdar (p. 678): Shah-ji-ki-Dheri, where the relic casket was found.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR II, 1863–64, p. 89) puts the stūpa "close to the holy tree on its south side" and
reports only hearsay that no remains exist. He did not look for it.

**Excavations.** Spooner tested Foucher's east mound outside the Ganj Gate (AR 1908–09, pp. 48–53). At the centre of
what he called the largest stūpa in India lay a bronze casket inscribed in Kharoṣṭhī by "Agisala, overseer of works
at Kanishka's vihāra in the saṃghārāma of Mahāsena", a crystal reliquary with three bone fragments and a coin of
Kaniṣka: "undoubtedly the original relics deposited in the stupa by Kanishka". The casket also bears Kaniṣka's
figure and name (AR 1909–10). Hargreaves (AR 1910–11) cleared the cruciform base, 180 ft across. Vogel (AR 1908–09)
adds that the Sappers and Miners dug here in 1875 and found Buddhist remains burnt by fire, and that Cunningham had
apparently moved to this mound without publishing it. Songyun's "7 li E" against Xuanzang's "8–9 li SE" is put down
to different starting points.

**Inscriptions.** Konow re-reads the casket (CII 2.1, pp. 135–138): "In the year 1 of (the maharaja) Kanishka …
Kanishka's Vihara, in Mahasena's Samgharama, in the acceptance of the Sarvastivadin teachers", and holds the site
"proved to be correct through Dr. Spooner's excavations".

**Gazetteers.** Peshawar 1883 and 1899 echo ASR II: no remains. A footnote in the Thana volume of the Bombay
Gazetteer (1882) says "This is perhaps the great Mânikyâla tope", without argument.

**Kuwayama (2002).** In "Shah-ji-ki Dheri before Kanishka" (ch. V, pp. 69–89), re-reading Spooner's and Hargreaves'
reports with unpublished Frontier Circle photographs, he accepts the mound as the stūpa the pilgrims ascribe to
Kaniṣka, by its size and position (pp. 69–70), but splits its history. The radial walls on the natural soil are the
foundation of a first, circular stūpa (Period I), no later than Kujula Kadphises by comparison with the Dharmarājikā
at Taxila (pp. 85, 88–89); it was destroyed to its foundation, and a square stūpa, later made cruciform, was raised
over it. The relic chamber was built against the broken ends of the radial walls, so the "Kanishka casket" belongs to
the rebuilding, not to the first stūpa (p. 83). He reports Fussman's re-reading of the casket (1987), which makes the
monks Mahāsena and Saṃgharakṣita the donors, recognises Kaniṣka neither as founder nor as donor, and finds no secure
date in his reign (p. 70). The front-view stucco Buddhas in the photographs are of a post-Gandhāran style (p. 71), and
he groups the cruciform plan, with round towers at the corners, with stūpas of the 7th–8th century (pp. 7, 58). He
reads Xuanzang's legend of Kaniṣka's stūpa rising over the boy's small one as the memory of Kaniṣka covering an older,
damaged circular stūpa (p. 89).

**Recent scholarship.** Kuwayama (1995, p. 356 and appendix pp. 346–337, journal pagination running backwards), the
Japanese original of his 2002 chapter, lists every Chinese account of the stūpa with its dimensions (Faxian to
Huichao). He reads Xuanzang's own observations (two small stūpas and two seated Buddhas carved in blue-black stone on
the S of the E stair, a 16-chi double-bodied Buddha, a white stone Buddha 100 paces SW, the stūpa burnt and under
repair) as Gandhāran schist sculpture, which the excavation hardly produced, and so doubts that the stūpa was already
cruciform in 629, though the larger base circuit Xuanzang gives might reflect it; he leaves the point open. It bears
on what Xuanzang saw, not on where. Behrendt (2018, p. 149) cites Xuanzang's account of the fire-damaged stūpa under
repair as late Buddhist activity in Gandhāra and takes it, after Kuwayama (1997), as probably the cruciform stūpa at
Shāh-jī-kī-ḍherī; no new evidence.

**Assessment.** The great stūpa is settled by its size, its position E/SE of the city and an inscribed casket in its
relic chamber that names Kaniṣka (Konow: Kaniṣka's vihāra). Fussman's re-reading, as Kuwayama reports it, removes
Kaniṣka as founder and donor, and Kuwayama puts the casket in a rebuilding over an older circular stūpa; that sequence
agrees with the legend Xuanzang tells, and on his dating parts of the excavated cruciform monument may be later than
Xuanzang. Neither point moves the site. The survey's "no remains" was hearsay, and the gazetteers copying it are
overturned; the Thana footnote carries no weight. Mizutani is confirmed.

### 02.04.06 迦膩色迦伽藍 — Kaniṣka's monastery: the W mound at Shāh-ji-ki-Dheri (Spooner), not Gor Khattri

**Identifications.** Mizutani: possibly Gor Khattri at the NE corner of Peshawar (uncertain). Cunningham (p. 93) and
Watters (p. 208) make the same tentative identification with the Gurh-Katri of Babur and Akbar.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR II, 1863–64, pp. 89–90): Vīra Deva of Magadha was sent in the 9th–10th century to "the
great Vihara of Kanishka", and "this great monastery was still existing in the times of Baber and Akbar under the
name of Gor-Katari". No remains are described.

**Excavations.** Spooner (AR 1908–09): "attached to the main pagoda on the west, Kanishka built a vast monastery.
The relative position of the two mounds at Shah-ji-ki-Dheri agrees exactly with this description"; the west mound
suggests "a monastic quadrangle on an exceptionally large scale". The casket from the stūpa (02.04.03) names "the
Vihara of Kanishka in the monastery of Mahasena", which Spooner (AR 1909–10) takes to mean that Kaniṣka built at the
older Mahāsena monastery, which then "gave way before the larger monastery which the Chinese pilgrims found
associated with Kanishka's name and portions of which we have now recovered". Hargreaves (AR 1910–11) traced the
monastery wall facing the stūpa. Vogel (AR 1908–09) notes that Gor Katri was Cunningham's published first
conjecture, which he later dropped.

**Gazetteers.** Peshawar 1883 and 1899 echo ASR II ("survived as Gor Khatri"), with the Babur, Akbar and Vīra Deva
references.

**Kuwayama (2002).** W of the stūpa lay the 6-m mound where Crompton dug most of his trenches, Hargreaves' "Monastery
Mound" (p. 69); a little W of the stūpa the excavators exposed three superimposed buildings (two of brick, the last of
brick and stone) with square pillar bases and walls faced with round or semicircular towers, which they took for
monastic buildings (p. 70). He does not discuss Kaniṣka's monastery as such, but what he reports fits a monastery W of
the stūpa, as the text requires.

**Assessment.** Gor Khatri rested on the name and later fame of the place, argued before the stūpa was found, and
its author dropped it. The excavated stūpa, the inscription naming Kaniṣka's vihāra, and the great monastic mound
lying west of it, as the text requires, put the monastery at Shāh-ji-ki-Dheri. Mizutani's "possibly Gor Khattri"
should be replaced.

### 02.04.09 布色羯邏伐底城 (puṣkalāvatī) — Charsadda/Prang; mound surveyed by Garrick; Vivien's Nisatta superseded

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Chārsadda, on the left bank of the Swat a little above its confluence with the Kabul
river, 17 miles from Peshawar (agreed). Cunningham (p. 57–58): Hashtnagar, the towns of Parang and Charsada. Beal
(p. 109) and Watters (p. 214) both say Hashtnagar, tentatively. Majumdar (p. 676): Charsada, certain after
excavation. Dey: Pukely on the Swat. The one different view is Vivien's (p. 308, tentative): approximately Nisatta,
on the left bank of the Kabul river a little *below* the Swat confluence.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR II, 1863–64, pp. 89–90) prefers the Life's 100 li to the Xiyu ji's 50, since the
crossing of the Kabul river 12 miles NE requires it; "the full distance of 16 miles will bring us to the two large
towns of Parang and Charsada". Garrick (ASR XIX, 1881–82, pp. 101–104) went on the ground: he measured the Bala
Hissar mound at Charsadda (an irregular triangle, c. 485,000 sq ft at the top) and located three gates in order to
follow the pilgrim's bearings, concluding that "further research only goes to confirm it". He also sought the stūpa
E of the city and the monastery N of it (see the final section).

**Inscriptions.** Pargiter (EI 12, p. 302) and Konow (CII 2.1, pp. 117–118), editing the Hashtnagar pedestal of the
year 384, put the eight Hashtnagar villages (Prang, Charsadda, Rajar …) "on the site of the ancient town
Pushkalavati". Konow cites Marshall and Vogel's excavation of 1903 at the Palatu Dheri mound near Rajar: a Buddhist
monument violently destroyed, with late Kushan coins. No record names the town.

**Gazetteers.** Peshawar 1883 and 1899 echo Cunningham ("twin towns Charsada and Prang"), with the bearing and
distance from Peshawar and Ptolemy's Peukelaotis.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Agrees with Cunningham: Prang, Chārsadda and Rājar at the old Swat confluence (I, p. 42).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Charsadda, 17 miles NE of Peshawar, on the E bank of the Swat a little above
its junction with the Kabul river (p. 251).

**Kuwayama (2002).** Charsadda, the mounds of Bala Hisar or Shaikhan Dheri (p. 177). He takes Faxian's 揵陀衛
(*Gandhavatī) for Puṣkalāvatī (p. 23), but elsewhere equates it with Songyun's 佛沙伏 at Shahbaz Garhi (p. 112;
02.04.14). The two statements do not agree; the site of the town is not in question.

**Recent scholarship.** Baums (2019, p. 169) lists Avaśaüra, a city at Charsadda (CKI 178), among the settlements
called *nagara* or *pura* in the Gāndhārī inscriptions, and Pokhaladi = Puṣkalāvatī only in the British Library
Gāndhārī avadānas (CKM 2, 14), without location. Baums thus places a city of another name at Charsadda; as before, no
inscription ties the name Puṣkalāvatī to the site.

**Assessment.** Garrick's survey adds field evidence to Cunningham's reckoning, and the Palatu Dheri excavation
(behind Majumdar's "certain after excavation") shows Buddhist remains among the villages. Vivien's Nisatta, made
before the survey, no longer competes. Mizutani is strengthened.

### 02.04.12 化鬼子母窣堵波 — the Hārītī stūpa: Tarangzai by distance only (Cunningham)

**Identifications.** Mizutani glosses only the name Hārītī and gives no site (unknown). No authority in the coded
books names one. The text puts the stūpa c. 50 li NW of the stūpa near Puṣkalāvatī.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR II, 1863–64, p. 90) counts 50 li (8⅓ miles) NW from the eye-gift stūpa N of Puṣkalāvatī
to the Hārītī stūpa, and 50 li N from there to the Śyāmaka stūpa (02.04.13): "These two places are probably
represented by Tarangzai and Tangi, two small towns on the Swat River", among the eight towns of Hashtnagar. He
reports no remains at either.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** Tarangzai is a reckoning of distance and bearing, with no monument behind it. It can be recorded as
Cunningham's candidate, but the stūpa remains unlocated.

### 02.04.13 商莫迦菩薩窣堵波 — the Śyāmaka stūpa: Tangi by distance only (Cunningham)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no site (unknown). Beal (p. 112, tentative): "some 90 to 100 li NNE of Pushkalavati"
by the route, which is a reckoning from the text, not a site.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR II, 1863–64, p. 90) puts it at Tangi on the Swat, 50 li N of his Tarangzai (02.04.12),
by distance only. Tangi is also his starting point for the 200 li to Varuṣa (02.04.14). No remains are reported.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** Like Beal's reckoning, Cunningham's Tangi restates the route rather than locating a monument. The
stūpa remains unlocated, with Tangi as a candidate by distance.

### 02.04.14 跋虜沙城 — Shahbaz Garhi; Cunningham's Palodheri, abandoned by Cunningham himself; Shahr-i Bahlol (Errington, unread)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Shahbaz Garhi, 65 km ENE of Peshawar (agreed), after Foucher and Dani. Majumdar (p.
676) and Dey agree; Majumdar notes that Cunningham's identification "has been discarded", by Cunningham himself in
ASR V. Cunningham's earlier site in the *Ancient Geography* (p. 60) was Palodheri, repeated by Watters (p. 217) and
Beal (p. 112), who also wondered about Takht-i-Bahi. Vivien (p. 309, tentative): Barotch on Cunningham's map.

**Survey.** ASR II (1863–64, pp. 90–91) argues for Palodheri, 10 miles N of the Shahbaz Garhi inscription: *dheri*
is a mound of ruins, "Palo may preserve some portion of the name of Palusha", the distance from Tangi fits though
"its direction is east instead of south-east", and the Kashmiri-ghar cave lies 3–4 miles ENE. In ASR V (1872–73) he
writes, without argument in the passages coded, "Po-lu-sha, or Shahbaz-garhi" (p. 42), and floats a link between
Po-lu-sha and the Raja Vara of the Takht-i-Bahi ruins (p. 36).

**Excavations.** The Annual Reports treat Shahbaz Garhi as settled. Stein (AR 1911–12, p. 113) measures from
"Po-lu-sha or Shahbaz-garhi". Hargreaves (AR 1922–23, p. 17) calls "the village of Shahbazgarhi, the Po-lu-sha of
the Chinese pilgrims", with the edict rocks ¾ mile SE and the old Khyber–Taxila road south of them. Nothing was dug
in the town.

**Gazetteers.** None identify the town. The Peshawar volumes mention the edict rock only in a margin heading.

**Foucher (1942–47).** One of Mizutani's sources: Shahbaz Garhi, whose Aśokan edict attests the town's age, on the old
road from Puṣkarāvatī by Hoti-Mardān, which kept to the irrigated plain; this explains the arc
Puruṣapura–Puṣkarāvatī–Po-lu-sha–Udabhāṇḍa (I, p. 42).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Shahbaz Garhi, 65 km ENE of Peshawar, after Foucher and Dani (p. 257).

**Name.** The forms in use break the key at the first syllable: 跋 is voiced (並) and renders bha or ba, never p and
never v in the counted names, so Watters' Paluṣa fails and Julien's Varuṣa survives only because v and b share
characters elsewhere; 虜 is ru/lu and 沙 is ṣa (生). The characters give *Bharuṣa or *Baluṣa, which is not attested in
the Sanskrit library, the inscriptions or the survey reports. Low confidence; Cunningham's "Palo" argument for
Palodheri rested on the p-form and falls with it (data/names/).

**Kuwayama (2002).** Songyun's 佛沙伏 (Boshafu) is Varuṣapura, Shahbaz Garhi, three days W of the Indus; Faxian and
Songyun both took six days from Taxila to it (p. 112). Agrees.

**Recent scholarship.** Neelis (2011, p. 237 and n. 19) gives Varuṣapura as "probably localized at Shahr-i Bahlol", an
ancient city a few km S of Takht-i-Bahi whose Buddhist remains, found since the 19th century, are poorly preserved
under the modern village. He cites Errington (1993, "In search of Pa-lu-sha, a city of the central Gandhara plain",
BAI 7, pp. 55–66) and gives no argument of his own; Errington is not yet read here, so her case is not known. Naiki
(2015, p. 273) repeats Shahbaz Garhi without new evidence. Behrendt (2018, p. 149) gives Po-lu-sha as "likely
Sahrī-Bahlol", after Errington (1993), with no argument. Khan Khattak (2019, p. 90) rejects Cunningham's and Watters'
Palo Dheri as resting only on distances and quotes Errington (1993, p. 63): there Sahri Bahlol is a premise ("if the
latter is identified as Sahri Bahlol"), from which she moves Daṇḍaloka to the Takht-i-Bāhī hill (02.04.15); her
argument for Sahri Bahlol itself is still not read here.

**Route test (Shahr-i Bahlol).** With Shahr-i Bahlol taken at c. 34.25 N, 71.97 E (approximate), the legs that touch
Varuṣa favour Shahbaz Garhi. From the text-derived point for 02.04.13 (itself placed from the text) the 200+ li SE
gives 49.6 km at 105° to Shahbaz Garhi (0.25 km per li) but 32.6 km at 111° to Shahr-i Bahlol (0.16). Daṇḍaloka, 20+
li NE of the town, is Mekha-Sanda 4.3 km NE of Shahbaz Garhi but 20 km E of Shahr-i Bahlol; Errington moves the
mountain to the Takht-i-Bāhī hill, which lies NW of Shahr-i Bahlol, not NE (02.04.15). Stein, who dug six mounds at
Sahri-Bahlol, found its shrines ruined long before the 7th century (AR 1911–12, p. 99; see 02.04.16).

**Assessment.** Palodheri was Cunningham's view of 1863–64, carried into the *Ancient Geography*; he had moved to
Shahbaz Garhi by 1872–73. The Annual Reports repeat Shahbaz Garhi without new evidence, and nothing found in the town
names it: the Aśokan edict shows the place's age, not its name. Errington's Shahr-i Bahlol (1993, reported by Neelis
2011 and Behrendt 2018; quoted by Khan Khattak 2019 only as the premise for her Daṇḍaloka) is a specialist's rival
whose argument has not been read here; the legs measured to and from Varuṣa fit Shahbaz Garhi better, and Stein found
Sahri-Bahlol derelict before Xuanzang's time. Mizutani stands, but at medium confidence, not high: the identification
rests on agreement and the route, not on site evidence naming the town, and Errington's argument should be read before
it is raised again.

### 02.04.14a 跋虜沙城北窣堵波 — Foucher's Chanaka Dheri undermined by excavation; no replacement

**Identifications.** Mizutani (disputed): Foucher put the stūpa at Chanaka Dheri (Kanaka Chaitya), 20 km N of
Shahbaz Garhi. The Kyoto University excavation from 1959 showed that site to be a Kushan palace, not a monastery.
Mizutani reports this without concluding. No other authority names a site.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Recent scholarship.** Naiki (2015, pp. 273–272, 262, 249–248, journal pagination running backwards) re-reads the
unpublished Kyoto University excavations of the central and NW mounds at Chanaka Dheri (Mizuno, six seasons 1959–67):
no stūpa and no Buddhist sculpture beyond one small fragment of a garland-bearer relief. The lower level has two
massive buildings (the N court 72 × 47 m, with a six-pillared hall and a large tank), which he takes for a palace of
the 1st century AD destroyed by an earthquake c. AD 200; above them lie village houses dated by the Ranigat pottery
sequence up to the mid-6th century (with a Kidarite gold hoard) and again after the 9th. Cunningham had dug there for
three days in 1871; Foucher (1901, 1915) proposed the site without digging.

**Assessment.** The only proposed site has been ruled out by excavation, now published in detail (Naiki 2015): a
palace, then a village in Xuanzang's time, with no stūpa. No alternative appears in these sources. The stūpa is
unlocated.

### 02.04.15 彈多落迦山 (*dantalaka) — Mekha-Sanda vs the Kashmir Smast cave vs the cave near the Shahbazgarhi edicts

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Mekha-Sanda, at the N end of the hills running NE from Shahbaz Garhi (Foucher;
excavated by Kyoto University in 1962–67; two rock-cut caves) (agreed). Cunningham (p. 60): the great cave of
Kashmiri-Ghar, 3–4 miles ENE of Palodheri. Majumdar (p. 676): "About 1½ mile N.E. of the Asokan Inscriptions is a
double-chambered cave which answers exactly to the Chinese description of Sudana's cave". Watters (p. 217) reads
2,000 li with the Fang-chih. Beal gives no site.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR II, 1863–64, pp. 91–92) chose the Kashmiri-ghar cave because it lies "within three or
four miles of Palodheri, almost in the very position indicated". He knew it from Court and Loewenthal (a largely
natural cave of several chambers, with steps and buildings). When he moved Varuṣa to Shahbaz Garhi (02.04.14) he did
not re-measure the mountain.

**Excavations.** Hargreaves (AR 1922–23, p. 17): "a few now hardly traceable foundations on the Mekha-Sanda hill to
the north-east of the village alone remain to mark the retreat of the exiled prince". He did not dig.

**Gazetteers.** Peshawar 1883 and 1899 repeat Cunningham (citing ASR V): the "Kashmir Smats" cave temple on the
Sakri ridge of Pajja, 8 miles NW of Bazar, with "the ruins of a small city" below; it "has not been thoroughly
explored yet".

**K. Harimoto's notes.** Songyun's 善特山 (= 檀特山 of the *Weishu* 102, Xuanzang's 彈多落迦山) has a 護塔神 濕婆仙 who drives the
monastery's donkeys, and a shrine (廟) to the 濕婆仙, painted and gilded by the king, where he swept ash in place of a
novice. 濕婆 is the usual transcription of Śiva: a Śaiva shrine inside the Buddhist complex c. 520. Beal (1869, p. 196;
1884, I p. xcix) renders 濕婆仙 as "the Rishi Uh-po (Oupa? conf. Oupagupta)" and 除灰 as "sifting ashes", missing Śiva.

**Nagasawa.** Nagasawa (東洋文庫 法顕伝・宋雲行紀) already translates Songyun's 濕婆仙 as Śiva (p. 192) and suggests that 除灰 is an
error for 塗灰, the ash-smearing worshippers of Śiva (p. 201). He also notes that the jātaka's king of 葉波國 is called 湿波
(p. 200), which leaves a small doubt whether the stūpa-guarding 濕婆仙 is the god.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the Mekha-Sanda hill NE of Shahbaz Garhi (p. 259). He does not mention the
Kyoto excavations.

**Name.** Mizutani's *daṇḍaloka does not fit the characters: 彈多 is dental danta- (a retroflex would need 擇, 荼 or 拏),
and 落, with the rhyme -ak, gives la, not lo. The characters give *dantalaka, which is unattested; no mountain of this
name appears in the Viśvantara versions searched (Jātakamālā, Saṅghabhedavastu, Viśvantarāvadāna), and Xuanzang's note
that the old 檀特 is corrupt implies a longer form than daṇḍaka. Low confidence; the name does not bear on the site
(data/names/).

**Recent scholarship.** Khan Khattak (2019, p. 90) quotes Errington (1993, p. 63): if Pa-lu-sha is Sahri Bahlol
(02.04.14), the mountain "must be equated with the Takht-i-Bāhī hill"; the "slight discrepancies in distance and
direction" she explains by a walk of 2½ miles to the foot of the ridge and then E to the site on its N slope, and a
corroded copper coin of Agathocles (c. 190–180 BC) from the subterranean vaulted chambers on the E side makes an
early, perhaps "Aśokan", foundation feasible, the cells perhaps the Viśvantara cave. Khan Khattak finds this "logical"
though "far from certain", but would put the cave instead at a rock cave c. 2 km W of the Takht-i-Bāhī sacred area,
across two peaks, with remains below it and Buddhist structures on the ridge. The study's check: with Shahr-i Bahlol
at c. 34.25 N, 71.97 E (approximate, as in 02.04.14) and the Takht-i-Bāhī monastery at c. 34.29 N, 71.95 E, the
monastery lies c. 4–5 km NNW of the town (bearing c. 315–330°), not NE; the distance fits "20+ li", the direction does
not. The coin dates the foundation, not the name.

**Assessment.** Cunningham's Kashmir Smast was fixed from Palodheri, which he gave up, and lies far from Shahbaz
Garhi. Majumdar's cave and Mizutani's Mekha-Sanda both sit in the hills NE of Shahbaz Garhi, fitting the text's "20 li
NE", and the Survey in 1922–23 took the same hill and saw foundations on it. Errington's Takht-i-Bāhī hill (1993,
quoted by Khan Khattak 2019) is a dependent alternative: it follows only from her Sahri Bahlol for the town and lies
NW of it, against the text's NE. Mizutani is strengthened.

### 02.04.16 獨角仙人窣堵波 — the Ekaśṛṅga stūpa: Butan above Palai (Stein); Cunningham's Sahri-Bahlol rejected by excavation

**Identifications.** Mizutani gives no site (unknown). Majumdar (p. 677) reports two views without choosing:
Cunningham (ASR V) at "Takht-i-Bahai", and Stein at "Butan, a large important Buddhist site, in the Palai valley"
near the S foot of the Shahkot pass.

**Survey.** Cunningham's site (ASR V, 1872–73, p. 42) is Sahri-Bahlol, with Takht-i-Bahi as the mountain: "The high
mountain I would identify with Takht-i-Bahi, from which Sahri-Bahlol lies south 2½ miles", 16 miles NW of Shahbaz
Garhi. The stūpa is the Dhamami stūpa opened by Bellew. On p. 45 he makes the same stūpa Faxian's eye-gift stūpa
(see the final section).

**Excavations.** Spooner (AR 1907–08), digging at Takht-i-Bahi, agreed with Foucher that "the Chinese pilgrims do
not mention it". Stein excavated six mounds at Sahri-Bahlol (AR 1911–12, p. 99), found most shrines ruined long
before the 7th century, and concluded: "None of Hsuan-tsang's traceable routes in Gandhara took the pilgrims past
Sahri-Bahlol". On p. 113, following a hint from Deane, he traced the convent "about a hundred li to the north-west
of Po-lu-sha" and "to the south of a great mountain": "the remains of this ruined monastery and stupa, at the site
of 'Butan' near the southern foot of the Shahkot Pass and above the large village of Palai". His reading of a
Sahri-Bahlol relief as Ekaśṛṅga is doubted by Hargreaves; the site is not.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** Cunningham's Sahri-Bahlol rested on distance and was loose, since he used one stūpa for two legends.
Excavation now shows its shrines derelict before Xuanzang's time. Stein's Butan has ruins, the right bearing and
distance from Shahbaz Garhi, and the Shahkot range to its north. It is the best candidate, with medium confidence,
since nothing names the site.

### 02.04.17 毘摩天女像（崇山） — the Bhīmā image: Karamar hill (Cunningham) vs Kashmir Smast (Nasim Khan) vs Takht-i-Bahi vs "near Jamalgarhi"

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Karamar hill, ENE of Shahbaz Garhi (agreed; no authority named). Beal (p. 114,
tentative): projecting 150 li NW from Ohind "would bring us near Jamalgarhi". Dey: Takht-i-Bahi, "containing the
Yoni-tirtha and the celebrated temple of Bhimā Devī". Watters (p. 221) explains the image as a dark-blue rock but
gives no site.

**Survey.** Karamar is Cunningham's (ASR II, 1863–64, p. 92): "a very lofty mountain called Karamar, 3,480 feet in
height, which is exactly 8 miles to the south-east of Palo-dheri". He read the bearing as SE from his Palodheri,
not NE, and identified no shrine on it.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**K. Harimoto's notes.** Suggests that the Bhīmā hill's 大自在天祠 served by 塗灰外道 is the same Śaiva milieu as Songyun's 濕婆仙
shrine on the neighbouring Daṇḍaloka (02.04.15). Kuwayama (1972, p. 46) also reads it as a Śaiva ash-smearer cult
site.

**Recent scholarship.** Verardi (2012, pp. 152–154) finds it "spontaneous" to identify the goddess with the
Acimā/Gaurī of the Kashmir Smast cave, and the Maheśvara temple of the ash-smearers with the Pāśupata Vardhamāneśvara
temple and maṭha that M. Nasim Khan excavated there (preliminary report 2001; Khan 2003, 2009); he does not test
distance or bearing. Filigenzi (2015, pp. 26, 142–143) reports both sites without deciding. For Karamar, Foucher
(1902, pp. 363–365) found a ziyārat on the hill, local Muslim veneration, a tale of a fakir's leap from the summit and
a woman fakir Shehr Banu (= Siṃhavāhinī, an epithet of Durgā). For Kashmir Smast, Nasim Khan (2006, pp. 11–13, 43)
puts Xuanzang's place there, and "The name of Bhīmā is largely present among the epigraphic records from the site"
(Falk 2003; Nasim Khan 2006, pp. 74, 102–103; Srinivasan 2011). Falk and Nasim Khan are not read here. Kashmir Smast
is the cave that Cunningham took for the Daṇḍaloka mountain (02.04.15).

**Route test.** On the study's points (Shahbaz Garhi 34.24 N, 72.16 E; Karamar 34.21 N, 72.26 E, approximate) and
Kashmir Smast at c. 34°26′N, 72°14′E (a 1948 cave record as given by GrottoMap; approximate), the text's 50+ li NE
from Varuṣa gives Karamar 9.8 km at 110° (ESE, 65° off; 0.20 km per li, normal) and Kashmir Smast 22.5 km at 17° (NNE,
28° off; 0.45 km per li, about twice the normal). The next leg, 150 li SE to Udabhāṇḍa (Hund), gives Karamar 27.2 km
at 145° (0.18 km per li) and Kashmir Smast 50.4 km at 159° (0.34). Karamar keeps the distances and fails the first
bearing (route/segment_tests.tsv flags it); Kashmir Smast keeps the bearings roughly and runs long on both legs. From
Errington's Shahr-i Bahlol (02.04.14) Kashmir Smast lies 31.6 km at 50°, the bearing exact but the distance still too
long.

**Assessment.** The earlier reading that 50 li NE of Shahbaz Garhi suits Karamar does not hold on the study's
(approximate) point: Karamar lies ESE of the town, and Cunningham reached it by a SE bearing from his Palodheri.
Karamar has Foucher's living cult on the hill but no shrine found. Kashmir Smast has an excavated Śaiva temple and
goddess cave and, as reported, inscriptions naming Bhīmā, which would be site evidence of the kind the confidence rule
asks for; but it lies about twice the text's distance from Shahbaz Garhi, and its inscriptions are known here only
through Filigenzi. Beal's and Dey's sites come from a different reckoning (Beal from Ohind) or from a name
association; by Dey's own figures Takht-i-Bahi (28 miles NE of Peshawar) lies short of Shahbaz Garhi (40 miles NE),
not 50 li beyond it. The place is open between Karamar and Kashmir Smast, with low confidence, until Falk 2003 and
Nasim Khan 2006 are read.

### 02.04.18 烏鐸迦漢荼城 (udakabhāṇḍa) — Hund (Ohind) on the Indus; Watters' doubt answered by Hargreaves' inspection

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Ohind (Hund, Und), NE of the Indus–Kabul confluence, 16 miles above Attock (agreed).
Vivien (p. 311), Cunningham (p. 61; = Embolima), Beal (p. 114) and Majumdar (p. 677; the Udabhāṇḍapura of the
*Rājataraṅgiṇī*, Alberuni's Waihand) agree. Watters (p. 223) doubts Ohind and gives no site of his own.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR II, 1863–64, pp. 92–95) traces the name through the Arabic and Persian writers, notes
that the Indus has carried away half the town, and reports gold-washers' finds of coins.

**Excavations.** Hargreaves inspected "Hund, the ancient Udabhanda, the Ohind of the numismatists, the eastern
terminus of the old road through Gandhara from the Khyber to the Indus" on 13 January 1924 (AR 1923–24, pp. 68–69).
Ancient diaper walls run under the Muslim fort; there is Gandhāra walling SW of the village, high ground marking
monuments of the 3rd–5th century, and probably a stūpa base; he was given a 10th-century Śaiva inscription in
Śāradā. "The 20 li which Hiuen-tsang attributes to the circuit of this town seems in no way exaggerated as the Indus
has undoubtedly swept away" its southern part.

**Inscriptions.** Daya Ram Sahni (EI 22, pp. 97–98) edits an 8th-century Śāradā slab found at Hund by Captain E. H.
Cobb, recording a temple built by the Mahārājñī Kāmeśvarīdevī. He calls Hund "the modern name of Udabhanda", the
Shahi capital, and reports "extensive mounds" round the walled village, with Indo-Greek, Kushan, Shahi and Kashmir
coins "in large numbers"; "No excavations have been carried out". Sircar (EI 35): "Udabhanda … (modern Und near
Attock)". No record is said to name the town.

**Gazetteers.** Himalayan Districts II (1884) and the 1874 volume repeat Cunningham ("Embolima or Ohind … the
Utakhanda of the Chinese Buddhist pilgrims") and add nothing.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Und, the traditional Indus crossing above Attock (Udakabhāṇḍa, al-Bīrūnī's Wayhand, Ohind),
where Xuanzang crossed both ways and the king of Kapiśa wintered (I, p. 40; II, p. 230).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Ohind, NE of the Kabul–Indus confluence, 16 miles above Attock (p. 261).

**Kuwayama (2002).** Udabhāṇḍapura is Hund, on the N bank of the Indus above Attock, with Stein and against Caroe's
tepe near Lahor (p. 245); the winter seat of the 6th–7th-century Kāpiśī kings (p. 210). Agrees.

**Recent scholarship.** Kimmet (2021, pp. 2–3) cites a late 10th-century stone slab "said to come from Hund" whose
Śāradā inscription names the town of Udabhāṇḍa north of the Indus under Jayapāladeva (r. c. 964–1002) (Rehman 1978,
1979; not read here). Excavations under Ihsan Ali in the late 1990s and early 2000s found Hindu Śāhi structures,
published without detail; Buddhist clay votive tablets and miniature stūpas come from the area, Śāhi coins have no
context, and part of the site is lost to the river. Kimmet finds the contemporaneous archaeological evidence thin.

**Assessment.** The name runs unbroken from Udabhāṇḍa to Hund, and the remains and coins show a large town in use from
the Indo-Greeks to the Shahis whose extent fits the 20 li circuit. A Śāhi inscription said to come from Hund names
Udabhāṇḍa (Kimmet 2021, after Rehman), near-site evidence since its provenance is reported, not recorded; Watters'
doubt has no evidence behind it. Mizutani is strengthened.

### 03.01.01a 瞢揭釐城 — Mingora (Italian mission) vs Manglaor (Cunningham, Stein)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Mingora, c. 5 miles WSW of Manglaor, with the Butkara monastery site in its S suburb
(after the Italian mission) (disputed). Everyone else names Manglaor/Mangora as the capital: Vivien (p. 314,
Manglavor), Cunningham (p. 94–95, tentative), Beal (p. 121, tentative), Watters (p. 223), Majumdar (p. 678;
"supported by Stein") and Dey.

**Gazetteers.** None. The Himalayan Districts II volume (1884) mentions Udyāna only through Darel, its "former
capital".

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 3 takes Manglaor (Manglawar)
(Cunningham 1871; Stein; Watters 1904; Majumdar 1924; Dey 1927) here, not Mizutani's Mingora (route/README.md §7).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji takes Mingora, c. 5 miles SW of Manglaor, after the Italian mission, whose
excavations exposed the remains of a city and of monasteries; he reports Cunningham, Hori and Stein for Manglaor (p.
273). He cites no report or date, so this adds nothing to Mizutani's own source.

**Kuwayama (2002).** Mengjieli is "tentatively" Mingora, and the road north runs Mingora–Khwaja Khela–Shang on the
Indus (p. 145). He gives no argument for Mingora over Manglaor.

**Recent scholarship.** Bibi (2017, p. 64) reports Deane (1896, p. 656: Mungali "is undoubtedly Minglaur (Manglawar)
and not Mingaur (Mingora)") and Tucci (1958, p. 288: Mingora, not Manglawar) without deciding. Filigenzi (2015, pp.
180, 207–208) labels Mingora "formerly Mengjieli" after Olivieri (1996, p. 71), with no argument, and keeps Tucci's
equation of the Manglaor area with the Mangala[p]or of the Tibetan sources. Olivieri (2016, pp. 25–26) finds the
densest late (7th–8th-century) Buddhist rock sculpture on the left bank between Manglawar and Mingora, a stretch that
holds both candidates. Olivieri and Filigenzi (2018, pp. 85, 87) summarise the Butkara I sequence at Mingora (Faccenna
1956–62): the period of Great Stūpa 4 runs from the late 2nd/early 3rd to the 7th century AD, with stucco and kanjur
decoration and a restoration after a collapse probably caused by an earthquake, so the great stūpa was still
maintained in Xuanzang's time; this suits Mingora but does not decide between it and Manglaor.

**Assessment.** Mizutani rests on the Italian excavations around Mingora and Butkara. The older authorities rest on
the name Manglaor and on Stein. Both places lie in the same stretch of the Swat valley, 5 miles apart, and nothing in
these files settles which was the 7th-century capital; the recent literature (Bibi, Filigenzi, Olivieri) restates both
positions and adds nothing that decides. Mizutani's choice stands, with medium confidence.

### 03.01.02 忍辱仙人窣堵波 — the Kṣāntivādin stūpa: unlocated

**Identifications.** Mizutani gives no site (unknown). The text has the stūpa 4–5 li E of Mangali. Beal gives no
site.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Recent scholarship.** Bibi (2017, p. 64) reports Tucci (1958, p. 288), who identified this stūpa with Butkara I, the
great stūpa the Italian mission excavated at Mingora, and also took Butkara I for Songyun's Ta-lo monastery with its
gilded images. Bibi adds no evidence; the gilded sculpture bears on Songyun's monastery, not on the Kṣāntivādin
legend. Olivieri and Filigenzi (2018, pp. 85, 87) equate Butkara I with Songyun's Tolo monastery and O rgyan pa's
Dhumat'ala (after Tucci 1978, Faccenna 1980–81), not with the Kṣāntivādin stūpa, and show the Great Stūpa maintained
into the 7th century; nothing ties it to this legend.

**Assessment.** The stūpa is unlocated. It should lie just east of whichever site is taken as the capital (03.01.01a).
Tucci's Butkara I, reported by Bibi, is a candidate only if Mingora is the capital, and it lies on the SE side of the
town rather than 4–5 li E; a new candidate, low.

### 03.01.03 阿波邏羅龍泉 — Apalāla's spring: the head of the Swat at Kalam

**Identifications.** Mizutani gives no site (unknown). He notes that the real source of the Swat is far upstream at
Kalam, and that Petech thought the legend moved upstream over time. Vivien (p. 315), Cunningham (p. 95) and Beal
(p. 126) put the spring at the source of the Swat, 250–260 li NE of the capital, without naming a place. Watters
(p. 229, tentative) and Majumdar (p. 678–679) both follow Deane: Kalam, at the junction of the Utrot and Laspur
streams.

**Inscriptions.** Konow (CII 2.1, pp. 8–9) describes the find. Deane, who saw that the locality "must be looked for
in the neighbourhood of the present head of the Swat river near Kalam", found at Tirath, on the border of Swat
Kohistan, a boulder with two large footprints and an eleven-letter Kharoṣṭhī inscription, *Bodhasa Śakamunisa
padani* ("the footprints of the Buddha Śākyamuni"), of about the mid-1st century BC. Konow: "We are able to identify
these footprints with absolute certainty". The text puts the footprint stone (03.01.04) "more than thirty li"
south-west of the spring.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji gives Deane's Kalam in Swat Kohistan, at the confluence of the Utrot and Ushu
streams, taken from Watters (I, p. 229) and reached from Xuanzang's distance and bearing (p. 276). This is the view
the note already records.

**Assessment.** The inscribed stone fixes the reference point. Apalāla's spring should lie some 30 li upstream
(north-east) of Tirath, in the direction of Kalam, where Deane and those who follow him put it. These files do not
give the Tirath–Kalam distance, so whether the spring is at Kalam itself or short of it is open. Mizutani doubts
that the 250–260 li from the capital reach Kalam. The Tirath fix supports the upper Swat towards Kalam as the area.

### 03.01.06 摩訶伐那伽藍 (mahāvana) — Pinjkotai near Sunigram (Stein) or the Shināsi stūpa at Kukrai (Mizuno); Mahaban (Cunningham) weaker

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Shināsi stūpa at Kukrai, on the way to Ilām, after Mizuno (uncertain).
Cunningham (p. 71): the Mahaban mountain in Buner, with the monastery on its top. Dey (after Stein): Pinjkotai near
Sunigram in Buner, c. 26 miles S of Manglaur. Beal and Watters give no site.

**Survey.** Cunningham's ASR II (1863–64, p. 98) states the Mahaban identification in passing, in his argument
about Aornos; he did not visit the hill.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 3's distances and bearings takes
Mahaban mountain (Buner) (Cunningham 1871; Cunningham ASR II) here, not Mizutani's Shinasi stupa at Kukrai, on the way
to Ilam (route/README.md §6). The figures are one kind of evidence; see the assessment.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji gives no site of his own. He reports Deane, who put the monastery at the W or NW foot
of Mahaban, where remains survive on the lower slopes and the upper part of the hill, and Stein, who judged Mahaban
too far from Xuanzang's figures and proposed Pinjkotai in Sunigram (pp. 280–281). He does not mention Mizuno's
Shināsi. Deane's remains give Mahaban more than its name, but Stein's objection from the distance stands against it.

**Stein (1898).** Stein's Buner report (pp. 31–32 for the stūpa, 60–61 for the route), now read directly, puts Mahāvana at the ruins of
Panjkotai near Sunigram, where he surveyed a large ruined stūpa, and notes that Sunigram lies "almost due south" of
Manglaur, "exactly in the position indicated for the Mahāvana monastery". The nearest route, over the Khalil pass and
by Gokand, Padshah and Elai, is about 26 map miles; adding a quarter for the road he gets 192 li against the text's
200. Measured so, the leg gives 0.26 km per li, the normal value, while Mizuno's Kukrai, 11 km away, gives 0.06
(route/README.md §10, Test 4). Stein's own chain also fits the next stages: 6 miles = 36 li for the 30–40 li to
Mo-yü, 10 miles = 60 li to the Śibi stūpa.
**Assessment.** Stein's Panjkotai has a monument, the bearing and the distance; Kukrai has a stūpa on the way to Ilam
but is far too close for 200 li; Mahaban rests on the name and is too far (Stein: at least 50 road miles, 300 li).
Stein's site is now the better supported, though the ruin carries no inscription. Proposed: Panjkotai near Sunigram,
with Kukrai as the alternative; low to medium, taken as low.

### 03.01.07 摩愉伽藍 — Gōg-dara (Mizuno) or Sura in the Chumla valley (Cunningham)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Gōg-dara stūpa, chosen by direction (Mizuno) (uncertain). Cunningham (p. 71,
tentative): the large village of Sura in the Chumla valley, 10 miles NW of the highest peak of Mahaban. Beal and
Watters give no site.

**Survey.** ASR II (1863–64, p. 98) gives the same Sura identification, by distance from Mahaban, with no remains
described.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji discusses only the name: Watters and Hori take mayū for mayūkha 'light', Julien reads
masūra 'lentil' to fit the gloss 唐言豆 (pp. 281–282). He gives no site.

**Name.** The characters fit none of the three restorations well. 摩 is ma and 愉 yu (以 y-, 虞 u), so Watters' and Hori's
mayū[kha] "light" fits as far as it goes but needs a dropped syllable and does not fit the gloss 唐言豆 "bean"; Julien's
masūra "lentil" and Mizutani's Iranian maš "bean" fit the gloss but not 愉 (s would need a 心 initial, and maš has no
vowel for 愉). No Indic word for a pulse of the shape *mayu- is known (māṣa, mudga, masūra and kalāya all break 愉). Two
characters give easy chance matches; low confidence, and the name does not bear on the site (data/names/).

**Recent scholarship.** Bibi (2017, p. 66), from Rienjang's summary (2012, p. 4) of Stein's work, reports Stein's
identification of the Mo-su monastery with the Gumbatai stūpa of Torwasak village in Buner. It follows from Stein's
Panjkotai for Mahāvana (03.01.06) and his roundabout itinerary to Mount Ilam (03.01.05); Stein's own report is not
read here.

**Assessment.** Each candidate depends on where its proponent puts Mahāvana (03.01.06): Cunningham measures from
Mahaban, Mizuno from Shināsi, Stein from Panjkotai (Torwasak, reported by Bibi). The question cannot be settled from
these sources.

### 03.01.08 尸毘迦王本生窣堵波 — the Śibi stūpa: Cunningham's NW foot of Mahaban; Mizutani says unknown

**Identifications.** Mizutani: "未詳" (unknown). Cunningham (p. 94): the NW foot of the Mahaban mountain in the Buner
valley, which he takes to be Faxian's Su-ho-to. Beal gives no site.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji gives no site; he restores Śivika/Sibika and cites Faxian's Suhetuo (宿呵多) country for
the dove jātaka (p. 283).

**Recent scholarship.** Bibi (2017, p. 66) reports two field identifications: Stein's Girarai ruins (via Rienjang
2012, p. 4), the Girarai of his itinerary to Mount Ilam (03.01.05), and Deane's ruins on the Chanai hills at Surkhavi
and in the Naranji valley, where Deane found no stūpa, only Gandhāran sculpture fragments (Deane 1896, p. 657). Both
follow from their authors' Mahāvana and Mo-su (03.01.06–07). Fussman (1994, p. 15 and n. 59), from Xuanzang's
distances (Mahāvana 200 li S of the capital, the Śibi stūpa 70 li W of it) and Tucci's Mingora, puts the Śibi country
in the mountains S of Mingora and reports that Foucher accordingly accepted Girarai, between Swat and Buner; the
stepped stūpas drawn with this jātaka at Shatial and Thalpan differ from the low dome Xuanzang describes and are not
portraits of it.

**Assessment.** Cunningham's placement follows from his Mahaban chain (03.01.06–07), not from a found monument.
Stein's Girarai and Deane's Surkhavi/Naranji, reported by Bibi, are new candidates resting on their chains in the same
way; neither is described here as a stūpa answering the text. The stūpa remains unlocated.

### 03.01.12 遏部多石窣堵波 (adbhuta) — Deane says extant; Watters doubts

**Identifications.** Mizutani names no place (disputed): Deane says the stūpa "still exists", and Watters (p. 237)

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji gives the name (adbhuta, 'marvellous') and no site (p. 288).

**Recent scholarship.** Bibi (2017, p. 67) reports Tucci (1958, pp. 305–306), who took the huge dome-shaped natural

Bibi, rests on a natural rock read as the "stone stūpa"; its bearing fits, and its distance falls between the two
capitals' figures. A new candidate, low.

### 03.01.13 觀自在菩薩精舍 — the Avalokiteśvara vihāra: unlocated

### 03.01.14 藍勃盧山 — the head of the Aushiri valley (Deane) vs the Laspur mountains (Vivien)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the head of the Aushiri valley near Darora, draining into the Panjkora, where there
is a large lake (after Deane, in Watters) (uncertain). Vivien (p. 315): the mountains of Laspur (Laspissor),
between the Chitral river and the Tal valley, 450 li NE of Manglavor. Beal and Watters give no site of their own.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the head of the Aushiri valley near Darora, draining to the Panjkora, with a
lake a mile long, after Deane, who doubted how Xuanzang measured the distance (p. 290).

**Name.** The Taishō text has 藍勃廬 (CBETA 盧): 藍 lam (with -m before the labial), 勃 b(u)r, 廬/盧 ru or ro. That gives
Lambura, the name of a nāga king listed with Apalāla (03.01.03) in the *Mahāmeghasūtra* ("apalālena ca lambureṇa ca"),
and as Lamburaka beside Elāpattra (03.03.02) in the *Mahāmāyūrī* and the Bower manuscript. The text says the mountain
has a nāga lake on its ridge (山嶺有龍池), so a mountain named after its nāga is what one expects. This replaces the
unexplained Lamboura (Julien), Lanpolu (Beal, Watters) and Rābora (Hori). Medium confidence, since 廬 for a final -ra
is irregular unless 勃 is bur and 廬 -ru; the form helps to place the lake among the Swat nāga cults but does not locate
it (data/names/).

**Assessment.** Deane's lake matches the dragon-lake story. Vivien's Laspur is a map reckoning. The gazetteers do
not touch the question, so Mizutani stands, with low confidence.

### 03.03 呾叉始羅國 (takṣaśilā) — Shah-dheri/Sarai-kala, confirmed by four inscriptions found on the site naming Takṣaśilā; Vivien's Hasan Abdal is superseded

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Taxila, 21 miles NW of Rawalpindi (Sarai-kala), after Marshall (agreed). Cunningham
(p. 128, 138): "ruins of the ancient city near Shah-dheri". Watters (p. 241), Majumdar (p. 681) and Dey agree.
Vivien (p. 319, tentative): near Hassan Abdal, perhaps an old site 7–8 miles E or SE of it. Beal (p. 136) and Dey
report both views.

**Survey.** Cunningham found the site in 1863 (ASR II, 1863–64, p. 85), "to the east of Shah-dheri", 10 miles SE of
Hasan Abdal, and supports it with the pilgrims' three marches from the Indus (pp. 111–122). ASR V (1872–73, pp.
66–68) adds the copper plate of the satrap "Liako Kujuluka", found at Thupkia in Sirsukh, which names "the city of
Takhasila".

**Excavations.** At the Chir Tope (the Dharmarājikā) Marshall found a silver scroll of the year 136 of Azes recording
relics enshrined "at the Dharmarājikā Stūpa in the Tanuva district of Takṣaśilā" (AR 1913–14, pp. 12–13). From the
"Mahal" in Sirkap came a ladle inscribed "… Tachhasilae Kushaviana parigrahe" (AR 1923–24).

**Inscriptions.** Bühler (EI 4, pp. 54–55) and Konow (CII 2.1, pp. 23–29) edit the plate, Patika's of the year 78;
Konow's reading is "absolutely certain": "In the town of Takshaśila, to the north, the eastern region, Kshema by
name". Konow (p. 87) adds a vase found by Shahpur villagers in mound 13 near the Chir Tope ("this stupa was
established … in Takshaśila") and a second Mahal ladle (excavations 1920–27). Hultzsch (CII 1, p. xxxviii) takes
Aśoka's Takkhasilā for Shahdheri, after Cunningham.

**Gazetteers.** Rawalpindi 1883 and 1895 echo ASR II and call the identification "rendered certain" by the
pilgrims' measurements and the copper plate. Punjab 1919 adds nothing.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Three successive Taxilas, after Marshall (I, pp. 42–3); see 03.03.01a.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the excavated ruins between the Indus and the Jhelum, c. 20 miles NW of
Rawalpindi; he reports Cunningham's Shahdheri, Hügel's Rawalpindi and V. Smith's Hasan Abdal (pp. 301–302).

**Recent scholarship.** Baums (2019, pp. 168–169) gives the Gāndhārī forms of the name, Takṣaśila (CKI 60, 66, 233),
Takhaśila (CKI 46, 65, 99) and the adjective Takṣaïlaa (CKI 68), among them CKI 60, a reliquary found at Taxila; it
adds no find to the four inscriptions above.

**Assessment.** Four inscriptions found on the site, of the 1st century BC to 1st century AD, name Takṣaśilā: the
Patika plate, the Sihila vase, the Mahal ladles and the Dharmarājikā scroll. They put Cunningham's and Mizutani's
site beyond doubt. Vivien's Hasan Abdal, based on the Elāpattra spring (03.03.02), no longer competes.

### 03.03.01a 呾叉始羅國大都城 — Sirsukh (Marshall; Kushan walls excavated 1915–16) vs the Bir mound (Cunningham)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sirsukh, the third (Kushan) city NNE of Sirkap, after Marshall, not the Bhir Mound or
Sirkap (agreed). Cunningham (p. 129–130): "there can be little doubt that the city of his time was situated on the
mound of Bir". Beal (p. 136) does not choose among the mounds.

**Survey.** Cunningham's case (ASR II, 1863–64, pp. 117–118): on the Bir mound "the old coins are found in greater
numbers", with lapis lazuli; its size suits the 10 li circuit; and his head-gift stūpa (03.03.04) lies 8,000 ft NNE
of it, i.e. the text's 12–13 li N. He measured Sirsukh (circuit 15,300 ft) but did not treat it as a candidate. In
ASR V (pp. 67–68) he read the Sirsukh copper plate as proof that Sirkap was Taxila proper and Sirsukh a separate
place.

**Excavations.** Marshall's trial digs of 1912–13 touched the Bir mound only lightly. In 1915–16 (AR, pp. 11–14) he
cleared the SE corner of Sirsukh: the fortifications "were constructed of the large diaper masonry characteristic of
the Kushan epoch", with round loopholed bastions, and a block of the 3rd–4th century stood in the middle of the
city. He later put the head-gift stūpa at Bhallar, not at Cunningham's Jhandiala (03.03.04).

**Inscriptions.** On the Patika plate (03.03), Bühler (EI 4, p. 54) equates Takhasila with Sirsukh, citing Cunningham,
at odds with ASR V; Konow (CII 2.1, p. 26) puts the Saka city at Sirkap, with Kṣema to its NE. Both concern the 1st century
BC or AD, not Xuanzang's city.

**Gazetteers.** Rawalpindi 1883 and 1895 echo ASR II word for word: coins, lapis, and "probably the site of the
inhabited part of the city in the time of Hwen Thsang".

**Survey (ASR V).** Cunningham (ASR V, 1872–73, pp. 67–68) uses the Taxila copper plate of the satrap Liaka Kusuluka
to argue that Sirkap was Taxila: the plate was found at Thupkia in the middle of Sirsukh, and it names him satrap of
Chhahara and Chukhsa "to the north-east of the city of Takhasila", which is where Sirsukh lies relative to Sirkap.
This concerns the city of the plate's date (1st c. BC/AD), not directly the capital Xuanzang saw.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Sirsukh is the Kushan city, about as old as Puruṣapura (I, pp. 42–3). He does not say which
city Xuanzang saw.

**Kuwayama (2002).** Without soundings inside Sirsukh its building sequence is "guesswork"; parts of its fortification
may be diaper work carried on from Sirkap (p. 4). He says nothing of occupation in the 7th century.

**Recent scholarship.** Naiki (2021, pp. 221–230) proposes central Rawalpindi, as a hypothesis that needs excavation.
If the Dharmarājikā is the Treasure stūpa (03.03.03), he argues, the city lay 40+ li (c. 17 km) beyond it to the SE;
central Rawalpindi lies 25 km SE, where Cunningham (ASR II, pp. 151–152) saw coins found yearly and ground strewn with
potsherds. He uses the gap this note records (Sirsukh cut only at its S wall; 7th-century occupation unproven, after
Kuwayama 1987), notes that a Kharoṣṭhī plate naming Takhaśila is said to come from Hasan Abdal, and cites survey finds
in Islamabad (Rawal Dam mound, Ban Faqiran stūpa). He takes the li as 0.42–0.44 km.

**Route test (Rawalpindi).** The text measures the dragon pool (03.03.02) 70+ li NW of the city and the Treasure stūpa
30+ li SE of the pool; Naiki's 40 li from the stūpa to the city assumes the three lie on one line, which he himself
allows may not hold (pp. 219–220). From the pool at Hasan Abdal, Sirsukh lies 15.4 km at 116° (0.22 km per li,
normal), central Rawalpindi 41.3 km at 126° (0.59 km per li, more than twice the study's calibration and still long on
Naiki's own 0.42–0.44).

**Assessment.** The Bir mound rests on surface finds and on a circular argument with a head-gift stūpa that Marshall
has since put elsewhere. The Annual Reports show Sirsukh to be the walled Kushan city, the latest of the three, still
occupied in the 3rd–4th century. That supports Mizutani, though nothing here proves occupation in the 7th century.
Naiki's central Rawalpindi exploits that gap, but it has no site evidence beyond Cunningham's surface finds and fails
the 70 li from the dragon pool; it is recorded as an alternative. Sirsukh stands, with medium confidence.

### 03.03.03 四大寶藏窣堵波 — Baoti Pind: the stūpa Cunningham excavated, with the bearing corrected; the Dharmarājikā (Naiki)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the ruined stūpa on the ridge above Baoti Pind (agreed). Beal and Watters give no
site.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR II, 1863–64, pp. 139–140): "The distance points to the neighbourhood of Baoti Pind,
where I found the ruins of a large town and of several Buddhist monuments. But the bearing is east", since SE would
lead into the open plain. In the gorge is the Langar-kot (Srikot) fort. On the highest, NE point of the Baoti ridge
he found a stūpa 65 ft across on a platform, with a small monastery (70 × 58 ft), and "judged that it might
possibly be the actual stupa which was said to have been built by Asoka". His excavation reached a stone chamber at
8 ft and, under a slab at 14 ft, a large red earthenware vessel.

**Gazetteers.** Rawalpindi 1883 and 1895, and Attock 1909, echo ASR II, adding "never-failing springs" and the
local tradition giving the hill fort to Raja Sirkap. They report that "it is now impossible to ascertain which of
the ruined stupas is the right one".

**Foucher (1942–47).** Calls the stūpa "non encore identifiée"; he meant to look for it in December 1918 but was
prevented (I, p. 156). He does not take up Cunningham's Baoti Pind.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the ruined stūpa on the Baoti Pind ridge (p. 303).

**Recent scholarship.** Naiki (2021, pp. 222–226, 229) proposes the Dharmarājikā (Chir Tope), the largest stūpa in the
area (drum 34.5 m, against 19.5 m at Baoti Pind), which Xuanzang should have recorded. In its Court J (excavated
1930–31) six murdered people, one beheaded, lay unburied under the burnt collapse, with three Hephthalite coins among
25, one of the Alkhan king Javukha (c. 492/3): the monastery was destroyed after the late 5th century and never
rebuilt, as the text says of this one (久絕僧徒). It lies between the Margala and the Hathial/Sarda hills (入兩山間). From the
dragon pool at Hasan Abdal the Dharmarājikā lies 15.5 km at 122° and the Baoti Pind stūpa 14.3 km at 135°, both SE as
the text has it (Cunningham's "the bearing is east" does not show on the study's approximate Baoti Pind point, which
should be checked); Naiki's further step, the capital 40 li beyond, does not follow from the text (03.03.01a).

**Assessment.** The survey supplies a specific monument with a stūpa, monastery and relic deposit on Mizutani's ridge.
Naiki's Dharmarājikā answers the text's deserted monastery with excavated evidence of its abandonment and is the
larger monument; both lie SE of the dragon pool at about the same distance, and nothing found names either. The two do
not depend on the capital, since the text measures the stūpa from the pool. The place is open between Baoti Pind and
the Dharmarājikā, with low confidence.

### 03.03.04 捨頭窣堵波 — Bhallar stūpa (Marshall, AR 1915–16) vs Babar-khana (Cunningham, re-dated by Marshall)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Bhallar stūpa at the W end of Sarda hill (Marshall), with the extant stūpa early
medieval (agreed). Cunningham (p. 134–135): the great ruined stūpa in the Babar-khana tract, 1,200 ft N of Jhandiala
Pind, c. 2 miles N of the Bir mound.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR II, 1863–64, pp. 114–121) cites the name Babar Khana, "tiger's house", the mound
Seri-ki-pind, and the road through the Hatial and Sirkap gates running "due north" to the Jhandiala stūpa. In ASR V
(p. 72) he excavated a two-storeyed monastery beside it whose corner rooms he matched with Xuanzang's pavilions.

**Excavations.** Marshall reopened the Jhandiala stūpa (AR 1912–13, p. 16): a round 3rd-century stūpa over a
Scytho-Parthian one with coins of Soter Megas. Cunningham "penetrated only as far as the round stupa of the 3rd
century and proceeded to identify it with a stupa erected by Asoka, more than five hundred years before that date";
a gold relic casket lay in his spoil. In 1915–16 (AR, pp. 3–4) Marshall repaired "the Bhalar Stupa, which I have now
been able to identify with the stupa of the 'Sacrificed Head'", giving no argument; its terrace and monastery were
still uncleared. In AR 1914–15 (p. 13) he makes Bhalar contemporary with the Kunāla stūpa, c. 3rd century; Mizutani
calls the extant stūpa early medieval.

**Gazetteers.** Rawalpindi 1883 and 1895 restate Cunningham's view in summary.

**Survey (ASR V).** Cunningham (ASR V, p. 67) proposes Hatial (Athial, "place of bones"), the lower slope of the
fortified Sur-garh hill at Taxila, for the head-gift, reading "head" for "body" in Songyun. The basis is the
place-name.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the Bhallar stūpa at the W end of Sarda hill, 8 miles N of Taxila (p. 304).

**Kuwayama (2002).** He puts Bhallar among the new foundations of Taxila's last building period (VI), with a stūpa of
gigantic size in huge dressed limestone blocks, a technique he matches in 7th–8th-century Kashmir, and makes Xuanzang
an eyewitness of the revival in the early decades of that period (pp. 6–7). On his scheme the stūpa Xuanzang saw at
Bhallar was new or newly rebuilt.

**Recent scholarship.** Naiki (2021, pp. 228–231) accepts that Bhallar fits distance and bearing from Sirsukh, but
with his Rawalpindi capital (03.03.01a) moves the stūpa to the tope Court's map (1836) marks 5 miles NW of Rawalpindi,
N of the road, now within Islamabad: a long mound 20 ft high where an old dig had found the walls of a square temple,
gone by Cunningham's visit (ASR II, pp. 151–152). He adds that Margala, "cut neck", echoes the head-gift legend. No
remains survive, and he had not yet visited the spot.

**Assessment.** Cunningham's 12–13 li N is measured from the Bir mound; from Sirsukh, which lies at the NE corner of
Babar-khana on his own plan, the Jhandiala stūpa is not to the north. Marshall's dating objection decides nothing,
since by his own account Bhalar is no older and the "Aśoka" is legend either way. Kuwayama's late date for Bhallar
(his Period VI, Xuanzang's time) agrees with Mizutani's "early medieval" against Marshall's 3rd century, so the stūpa
Xuanzang saw there would have been recent. With Sirsukh as the city (03.03.01a), Bhallar stands, on the excavator's
word rather than a published argument; Naiki's Islamabad tope stands or falls with his Rawalpindi capital and is
recorded as an alternative.

### 03.03.06 拘浪拏太子窣堵波 — Kunāla stūpa: Marshall's excavated stūpa on the Hathiāl ridge; the bearing from Sirsukh still contested

**Identifications.** Mizutani (disputed): Marshall's stūpa on the N slope of the W end of the Hathiāl ridge S of
Sirkap, beside the "Kunāla monastery". Yamamoto (1940) and others object that Xuanzang's city is Sirsukh, not
Sirkap, so the bearing SE of the city fails. Beal gives no site.

**Survey.** Cunningham first proposed the chief tope of Karmal (ASR II, 1863–64, pp. 149–150), due SE of the Hatial
citadel; he dug it to the rock and found no deposit. In ASR XIV (1878–79) he moved to a stūpa terrace at the N foot
of the Meri hill, admitting "the direction is east instead of south-east".

**Excavations.** Marshall (AR 1914–15, pp. 13–14) excavated "a stupa of imposing dimensions … on a plateau on the
northern side of the Hatial ridge. This is the stupa which Hiuen Tsang describes as being more than 100 feet high":
a three-tiered base 63 × 105 ft, a monastery beside it, a 3rd-century stūpa enclosing a 1st-century one. No
inscription was found, and the bearing is not discussed.

**Inscriptions.** Konow (EI 14, p. 287), editing the silver scroll (03.03): "dhamaraia must denote the Chir stūpa
itself, and this stūpa M. Boyer is inclined to identify with the Kunala stūpa". Boyer takes a *dharmarājikā* for a
stūpa of Aśoka, who built the Kunāla stūpa; Konow allows relics of the Buddha instead and does not decide. The
scroll does not mention Kunāla.

**Gazetteers.** Rawalpindi 1883 and 1895 quote ASR II on Karmal; the topes had been "opened by the villagers".

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji reports Marshall's Kunāla stūpa on the Hathiāl ridge running NE from Sirkap, beside
the Kunāla monastery (Guide, pp. 150–151), but notes that its bearing differs from Faxian's and Xuanzang's and leaves
the identification undecided (p. 307). This is the note's own doubt, reached without Yamamoto's argument from Sirsukh.

**Kuwayama (2002).** The Kunāla stūpa and monastery on the hill S of Sirkap belong to his Period V, built when Sirkap
was "already an old story", and were renovated in Period VI, the period Xuanzang saw (pp. 7–8). That puts the monument
in use in his day; it says nothing of the bearing from Sirsukh.

**Recent scholarship.** Naiki (2021, pp. 221–222, 229–231) restates the bearing objection (Marshall's stūpa lies c. 3
km SSW of Sirsukh, not SE) and, with his Rawalpindi capital (03.03.01a), proposes the vanished tope at Thupi, c. 3.5
km SE of the Rawalpindi cantonment towards the Soan, pulled down for the jail (now Jinnah Park), where lamps with
"Arian" (probably Kharoṣṭhī) inscriptions were dug up (Cunningham, ASR II, pp. 151–152); the name survives in Topi
Rakh to the S. He rejects Boyer's Dharmarājikā (no 7th-century city lies NW of it) and Manikyala (too far, on a
plain).

**Assessment.** The survey's two sites, measured from the Bir mound, yielded nothing (Karmal) or need the bearing
altered (Meri). Marshall's site has a large stūpa with an older core, but from Sirsukh, Mizutani's own capital, the
Hathiāl ridge does not lie SE. Boyer's Dharmarājikā, a third candidate, rests on a debated sense of the word, and
nobody here tests its bearing from the city. Naiki's Thupi tope, a destroyed mound known from Cunningham, depends on
his Rawalpindi capital. Marshall's site remains the best on offer, with low confidence; the place is open.

### 03.04 僧訶補羅國 (siṃhapura) — the Salt Range round Ketas; Cunningham wavered (Malot → Ketas → Malot), Stein returned to Ketas

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Ketās (Ketāksh), N of the Salt Range, 16 miles from Pind Dadan Khan (agreed), after
Cunningham's *Ancient Geography* only. Cunningham (p. 143–145), Beal (p. 143, tentative), Majumdar (p. 682) and Dey
agree. Vivien (p. 321, tentative): Sangohi near Jhelam town, rejected by Beal and Cunningham. Watters (p. 249)
refuses the identification and reports that Cunningham later abandoned it.

**Survey.** Cunningham's view moved twice. In ASR II (1863–64, pp. 191–192) the bearing, distance and fish-tanks
"should be Katas; but the whole description will not apply to Katas", and he chose Malot, the early Janjuha
capital. The *Ancient Geography* (1871) moved to Ketas, with the capital at the Kotera fort. In ASR V (1872–73, p.
85), after marching the routes (114 and 101 miles against the pilgrim's c. 117), he went back to Malot. Ketas, 9
miles E, became the stone stūpa site (03.04.03).

**Gazetteers.** Jhelam 1907 reports the Malot view as "doubted even by himself", and prints Stein's field letters
for "the Kitas locality" (hill forts, the stūpa before the Sat-Ghara temples, the Jaina site at Murti; see
03.04.01a–03), which Bühler accepted "without hesitation". Dehra Dun 1911 (the Lakhamandal praśasti) attests the
kingdom but not its place.

**Inscriptions.** Bühler (EI 1, pp. 11–12) edits the Lakkha Mandal praśasti (Kalsi tahsil, Dehra Dun) of princess
Īśvarā, of the Yādava kings of Singhapura and widow of a Jālandhara prince. From the marriage he puts the kingdom
in the Panjab: "the Singhapura rajya of our inscription may be safely identified with the kingdom of
Sang-ho-pu-lo". He dates it c. 600, before the Kashmiri overlordship Xuanzang reports. It names the kingdom, not
its place, and was found far to the east.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 3 takes Sangohi, 10 mi SW of
Jhelum (Vivien 1858) here, not Mizutani's Ketas (Katas Raj), Salt Range (route/README.md §7).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji accepts Cunningham's Ketās (32°43′N 72°59′E) and rejects St. Martin's Sangohi, a town
in the plain that does not fit 依山據嶺 (pp. 313–314). He argues that Xuanzang did not go there: the Life writes 東南七百餘里聞
("heard of"), the next stage of the Xiyu ji starts again from the northern border of Taxila, which would be a strange
detour after 700 li to the SE, and the Jaina passage reads as hearsay (03.04.x1). He puts the distance mismatch down
to this. The Life does read 聞 (T50:0231a), but 復還 by itself would fit a return from a detour just as well.

**Kuwayama (2002).** In passing, Siṃhapura is in the Salt Range: he argues that Songyun's Hephthalite king camped near
Jhelum to control the Kashmiri salt trade from the Salt Range, "where, as Xuanzang stated …, Singhapura was located"
(p. 112). Agrees.

**Assessment.** The country is the Salt Range round Ketas and Malot, 9 miles apart, on every view; the praśasti
confirms the dynasty but not its location. Watters' "abandoned" refers to ASR V's Malot. Stein's later fieldwork
answers both Watters and Malot, so Mizutani's Ketas is strengthened, though Mizutani cites neither Stein nor
Cunningham's reports. The Life's 聞 (Ji) marks the country as known by report, so its 700 li and bearing are
second-hand figures; that weakens arguments from the distance, Cunningham's march to Malot in ASR V among them, more
than it weakens Stein's remains at Ketas.

### 03.04.01a 僧訶補羅國大都城 — the plain N of Ketas (Stein; Sheranwali Khanqah, Dar and Hameed), Malot (Cunningham, ASR), or the Kotera fort (Ancient Geography)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Kotera, the ruined fort on the hill above Ketās (uncertain). This is Cunningham's
site in the *Ancient Geography* (p. 145, tentative), and it depends on emending the text's 40–50 li to 4–5 li. Beal
gives no site, noting only that a capital ringed by crags rules out the plain of Taki. Watters (p. 253) rejects
Ketas.

**Survey.** In both his reports Cunningham put the capital not at Kotera but at Malot, the hill-fort 9 miles W of
Ketas (ASR II, 1863–64, p. 192; ASR V, 1872–73, p. 85; see 03.04). Malot fulfils, in his words, the text's city
"situated on a high mountain, which was both difficult and dangerous of access"; from it the 40–50 li to
the springs of Ketas stand unemended, with E for SE (p. 90). Cunningham admits one difficulty: there are "no signs
of any occupation beneath the cliffs to the south", where the Aśoka stūpa should be (03.04.02).

**Gazetteers.** Jhelam 1907 prints Stein: "I consider Ketas, or rather the field of ruins lying one or two miles
further north, to be the site of the ancient town", 40–50 li NW of the Jaina temples at Murti; chained Murti–Ketas
is 4½ miles, so "the discrepancy is not great". The forts on the surrounding hills are cited as proof of more than
a pilgrimage site.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji accepts Cunningham's Kotera provisionally, as "at least one view" until a better site
is found, while noting that its ruins are only 3,500 ft (¾ mile) round against the text's 14–15 li (p. 314). He gives
its distance from the Ketās pool as c. 12 miles, which cannot be right for a fort that, as he says himself, overlooks
the pool.

**Recent scholarship.** Dar and Hameed (2018, pp. 51–58) quote a later Stein (1937, p. 57 n. 16) for Dulmial, 3 miles
NW of Katas, chosen from Greek and Indo-Scythian coins bought at Katas and said to come from there; on a visit in
April 2019 they found no ancient mound in or near Dulmial and no antiquities known from it, and Stein seems never to
have gone there. They reject Malot (no stūpa or temple by a pool to its S or SE; its temple later than Xuanzang). They
propose instead the twin round mounds of Sheranwali Khanqah (now Sheranwala Nakka), 6–7 km due N of Katas and E of
Dulmial, with Muslim graves, plain red-slipped pottery and (in 1994) a crouching stone lion on the main grave, Katas
due S and Murti SE of it; the name "of the lions" is read as an echo of Siṃha(pura). Unexcavated; they are "tempted to
relate to, if not to identify" it with the capital, and the 14–15 li circuit is not tested.

**Assessment.** Kotera is the weakest of the three: Cunningham himself abandoned it, it needs an emendation, and its
ruins, 3,500 ft round by Ji's figure, are far short of the 14–15 li. Stein's plain and Cunningham's Malot both keep
the text's 40–50 li, each measured to a different stone stūpa (03.04.03). Malot fits the "high mountain" but has
nothing S of it (Cunningham, and now Dar and Hameed); Stein's city fits his excavated Murti, but Stein himself later
named Dulmial, where Dar and Hameed find nothing. Their Sheranwali Khanqah is a specific site in Stein's direction,
with Katas S and Murti SE, but it rests on two unexcavated mounds, surface pottery and a place-name, and two mounds on
the plain hardly answer a city "resting on the hills, on the ridge" (依山據嶺). The choice is open.

### 03.04.02 僧訶補羅城南窣堵波 — the Aśoka stūpa S of the city: the basement before the Sat-Ghara temples, read as a stūpa or as Cunningham's Jaina temple

**Identifications.** Mizutani: a large base, probably of a stūpa, at the foot of the Kotera hill, with a
Kashmiri-style temple in front (uncertain), after Majumdar (p. 683): "Facing them to the east is the basement of a
great structure probably a Buddhist stupa". Beal gives no site.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR V, 1872–73, p. 90), with his city at Malot, found nothing beneath the cliffs to the
south and put the stūpa on the north side, against the text. At Ketas (p. 92) he took the "large ruined temple,
marked C in my plan", a basement 68½ × 56½ ft with pilasters like those of Buddhist buildings at Shahdheri and
Manikyala, "much older than the group of Brahmanical temples in the Kashmirian style, which stand immediately above
it", to be the Jaina temple of the text (see the final section).

**Gazetteers.** Jhelam 1907 prints Stein: "In the large stupa, situated before the east front of the Sat-Ghara
temples, I believe I recognise the stupa of Asoka, which, Hiuen Tsiang says, lay to the south of the town". The
gazetteer coding marks this as "new"; see the final section.

**Recent scholarship.** Dar and Hameed (2018, pp. 52, 60–63 and n. 43) take the square basement before the Sat-Ghara
temples, drawn on Cunningham's plan in ASR II (p. 190) and "mistaken as Temple", as the Aśoka stūpa and the earliest
building at Katas, later used by Jainas and Hindus. It was excavated and partly restored in 2011 by the Punjab
Directorate of Archaeology (pls. V–VIII), but no report exists; they add that its square plan shows the present
structure to be later than Aśoka. It lies due S of their capital (03.04.01a).

**Assessment.** Stein, Majumdar, Mizutani and now Dar and Hameed take the early basement beside the Sat-Ghara temples
for a stūpa; Cunningham read it as a temple. Dar and Hameed identify Cunningham's planned "temple" with the excavated
base, which answers the question whether the sources describe one structure, though the 2011 excavation is unpublished
and the square base is later than Aśoka in its present form. With any of the northern capitals it lies south of the
city, as the text requires. Mizutani's site is supported by agreement among the authorities; medium confidence,
nothing found names it.

### 03.04.03 僧訶補羅城東南石窣堵波 — the stone stūpa and Jain site: Murti (Stein) vs the Ketas spring (Cunningham)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Ketās with its sacred pools, following Cunningham's reading of 4–5 li (uncertain).
Cunningham (p. 143): "the holy tanks of Ketaksh". Beal implies the same. Watters (p. 253) rejects it but finds
Stein's Jain sculptures from Murti "not conclusive".

**Survey.** Cunningham kept the springs at Ketas in all three versions. In ASR II (1863–64, p. 192) they were the
pools of the Ganiya Nala below Katas. In ASR V (1872–73, pp. 90–92), measuring 9 miles from Malot, he counted "no
less than seven other pools" above the holy pool, some with remains of walls, in a bend of the Ghata Nala; put the
stūpa on the 50-ft rocky spur inside the bend, where a Bairagi's house stands; took a ruined monastery 150 ft
square 400 ft W, then being dug out for stone, as the deserted monastery; and put the Jaina temple at basement C
(03.04.02).

**Gazetteers.** Jhelam 1907 prints Stein: down the Gandhala valley, Murti "seems to agree most remarkably with
Hiuen Tsiang's description" (pools with trees and fish); excavation of the c. 40-ft mound showed "a true stupa";
over 30 camel-loads of Jaina-style sculpture went to Lahore; local memory calls the temple Jaina; Murti is 4½ miles
from Ketas, chained. Stein found "at Ketas no group of tanks such as that described".

**Recent scholarship.** Dar and Hameed (2018, pp. 53–54, 65–66) agree with Stein: Murti, in the Gandhala valley (Rakh
Gandala) c. 3 miles E of Katas and SE of their capital, where Stein (1889, 1930–31) excavated a stūpa, in its last
phase probably Jaina, and a Hindu temple whose Murti red-sandstone sculpture went to Lahore; Stein saw pools of clear
water with fish in the Ghatia nala. They saw the former stūpa site in April 2019; no new excavation.

**Assessment.** Both candidates now have field evidence. Cunningham's walled pools, spur and monastery answer Stein's
denial of tanks at Ketas, but his stūpa was not excavated and his Jaina temple is a reading of a basement. Stein's
Murti has an excavated stūpa with Jaina sculpture and local tradition, and Dar and Hameed agree. Murti remains the
better-supported alternative to Mizutani's Ketas, but with less margin.

### 03.04.05 摩訶薩埵捨身處（大石門） — the body-offering site: Banj (Stein; remains and red soil, AR 1904–05) vs Manikyala, where Court's inscription names the body-offering

**Identifications.** Mizutani (disputed): Banj mountain on the Mahaban spur in Buner, reached by crossing the Indus
from the Taxila side; Majumdar, Petech and Chavannes agree. Cunningham (p. 140): Manikyala, reading 信度 as the
Suhan river; Beal (p. 145) follows him. Watters (p. 255) finds Manikyala "attended with serious difficulties".

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR II, 1863–64, pp. 154–165; ASR XIV, p. 50): 200 li SE of Taxila "is the exact bearing and
distance of Manikyala". The body-offering stūpa is Court's tope No. 2: "The Huta-murta or 'body-offering' is twice
mentioned in the inscription that was found covering the deposit". He adds the red soil and a gateway as the "great
stone gate".

**Excavations.** Marshall's review of Stein's tour (AR 1904–05) reports "on Mount Banj the remains of an important
Buddhist sanctuary, which he identifies, with great probability, as the long sought for sanctuary of the body
offering". The case, first put by Chavannes, is "in the main topographical", with support from the remains and
"that red coloured soil remarked on as such a distinctive feature by Hiuen Thsang".

**Inscriptions.** Konow (CII 2.1, pp. 55–57) edits a Kharoṣṭhī slab "brought to Sir Harold Deane as found at Mount
Banj": "Of the Makadaka scion, in the Vajrastupa, in the year 102, (this) well (is) the gift" (pre-Kushan; AD 18–19
on his era). He takes Stein's ancient well at the head of the Khabal valley, still Banj's water supply, as
"probably the find-place", accepts Stein's identification, and suggests that either of Xuanzang's two stūpas "may
have been the Vajrastupa".

**Gazetteers.** Rawalpindi 1883 and 1895 echo ASR II, with the Raja Rasalu legend.

**Recent scholarship.** Naiki (2021, pp. 226–227, 230–231) and Neelis (2011, p. 201 nn. 54–55) both reject Manikyala.
Its relic deposits (after Errington) run from Kaniṣka and Huviṣka coins to coins of the early 8th century (Abdallah b.
Khazim, Tegin of Kabul, Yaśovarman of Kashmir), so it was active and enlarged then; but it stands on a flat plain
without the mountains and cliffs of the tigress story (Naiki, after Kuwayama 1987), and Xuanzang's "great rocky pass"
cannot be squared with it (Neelis). Neither names an alternative; Naiki thinks Xuanzang did not pass Manikyala.
Fussman (1994, p. 16 and n. 55) adds Songyun's bearing as a check: the site, eight days SE of the Swat capital on a
high mountain with a stūpa and a monastery of 300 monks, lies between the Indus and Swat, since Songyun did not cross
the Indus, which agrees with Xuanzang's "great rocky pass" 200 li SE of Taxila's N border; after Stein, on the
Gandhāran slope near the Banj bridge, not securely identified.

**Assessment.** Court's inscription, if read rightly, makes Manikyala a Kushan body-offering site, but the itinerary
returns to Taxila's north border and crosses the Indus, which Cunningham must explain away. Banj fits the route and
has a sanctuary and the red soil. The well-gift, matching Stein's well, adds a stūpa there by the early 1st century
AD, though its provenance rests on what Deane was told, and it names a "Vajra-stūpa", not the jātaka. Mizutani is
strengthened, with medium confidence.

### 03.04.06 捨身北石窣堵波 — the stone stūpa N of the body-offering: unlocated; Cunningham's two incompatible proposals

**Identifications.** Mizutani (disputed): existence unknown. He notes that Cunningham's Manikiāla stūpa is a
1st–2nd century Gandhāran monument. Watters (p. 255) rejects Manikyala. Beal gives no site.

**Survey.** In ASR II (1863–64, p. 172) Cunningham argued that Ventura's great tope at Manikyala post-dated
Xuanzang, since Sassanian-Arab coins lay 64 ft inside it, so "Hwen Thsang's silence is due to the non-existence of
the tope". In ASR XIV (1878–79, pp. 1–3) he made that same great tope Aśoka's stūpa N of the body-offering, reading
"south" for "north", since it stands S of Court's tope.

**Inscriptions.** Konow (CII 2.1, p. 56) suggests that the "Vajrastupa" of the Mount Banj well inscription (03.04.05)
may be either the body-offering stūpa or this 200-ft stūpa to its north. That is a suggestion from the text, not a
located monument.

**Gazetteers.** Rawalpindi 1883 and 1895 treat Manikiala as a whole without singling out this stūpa.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji reports only Cunningham's Manikyāla stūpa, which he calls an Aśokan Gandhāran
building of the 3rd century BC (p. 318), without Cunningham's own earlier objection or the reversed bearing. This adds
nothing.

**Assessment.** The survey's two proposals contradict each other, and the later one needs the bearing reversed. The
stūpa stands or falls with 03.04.05. If the site is Banj, Konow's reading makes it possible that the Vajra-stūpa was
this one, but no remains north of the sanctuary are described in these sources, so the stūpa is unlocated.

### 03.04.07 孤山伽藍 — the monastery on the isolated hill: unlocated; Cunningham's Mera turned out a Muslim tomb

**Identifications.** Mizutani: "場所も未詳" (unknown). The text puts it 50 li E of the body-offering stūpa. Beal gives
no site.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR XIV, 1878–79, pp. 1–3), measuring from Manikyala, proposed Mera, 9 miles E of the
Manikyala tope: the "exact agreement of both bearing and distance" favoured it. On the ground he found that "the
thup of Mera was the dome of a Muhammadan tomb", built of old cut stones.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** The survey's candidate depends on Manikyala (03.04.05) and yielded no Buddhist remains. The
monastery is unlocated.

### 03.05 烏剌尸國 (uraśā) — Hazara; the capital at Mangali (Cunningham; tested on the ground by Hargreaves 1923) rather than Haripur

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Hazara district, with its capital at Haripur (after Watters) (agreed). Beal (p. 147),
Majumdar (p. 680), Dey (after Stein) and Vivien (p. 321) all say broadly Hazara. Watters (p. 257, tentative) has
Haripur as the capital, after Swynnerton. Cunningham (p. 119–120, tentative): the district of Rash, with the capital
"probably … at Mangali, which is said by the people to have been the ancient capital". Dey also reports Gurez.

**Excavations.** Hargreaves (AR 1923–24, pp. 66–68) tested the Hazara Gazetteer's capital "Mangali on the Mangal
stream" on 9 August 1923. Potsherds cover an area that "coincides pretty closely with the '7 or 8 li' in circuit".
A mound ½ mile SW proved not Buddhist; on a hill (4,219 ft) a furlong SE of it were Kushan diaper basements that
"may mark the sites of the stupa and sangharama … though the site is rather south than south west".

**Inscriptions.** Konow (CII 2.1, pp. 88–89) edits a copper ladle bought c. 1920 by the Deputy Commissioner of
Hazara from a villager who said he found it at Bedadi, on the Siran c. 12 miles NNW of Mansehra: "Gift of
Samgharakshita to the congregation of the four quarters, in the Uraśa kingdom (*Uraśaraje*), of the Kaśyapiya
teachers". The script is pre-Kaniṣka; Stein reports Bedadi as an old site with coins from Azes to the Hindu Shahis.

**Gazetteers.** Punjab 1919 names "the Urash plain" only as a Kashmir feudatory. No Hazara gazetteer appears among
the hits, although Hargreaves quotes one for Mangali.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji prefers Swynnerton's Hazara with its capital at Haripur (IA XX, p. 336) to
Cunningham's Rash in Dhantawar NW of Muzaffarabad, because Cunningham's site lies NE, not SE, of Taxila; he rejects
St. Martin's emendation to 東北, since the Life and the Fangzhi also read 東南 (p. 320). The text, however, measures the
500 li SE from the isolated hill E of the body-offering site (03.04.07), not from Taxila. From the Mahaban–Banj area
(03.04.05) Haripur and Mangali both lie roughly SE and Rash lies E to NE, so Ji's conclusion against Rash holds on the
corrected reading. He does not know Hargreaves' Mangali or the Bedadi ladle, and adds no evidence for Haripur.

**Name.** 尸 is ś (書, 49 of 49 cases), so of Mizutani's three forms only uraśā (Pāṇini 4.3.93) fits; urasā
(Rājataraṅgiṇī) and uragā (Mahābhārata) do not. The Bedadi ladle's Uraśaraje (CII 2.1) has the same ś, so inscription
and transcription agree. Secure (data/names/).

**Assessment.** The country, agreed by all, is now attested by an inscription naming the Uraśa kingdom, found (by
report) in the Siran valley of Hazara; a portable ladle cannot fix the capital. Cunningham's Mangali has a site of
the right size and two Kushan Buddhist foundations near the right place, though the bearing is off and the remains
are scanty. Haripur remains Swynnerton's suggestion, with nothing found. Mizutani's Hazara stands, but Mangali is
the better-supported capital, with medium confidence.

### 03.06.06 佛牙伽藍 — the Tooth monastery: on the S slope of Takht-i-Sulaimān, or near Pandrethan

**Identifications.** Mizutani: name and site unknown, perhaps near the Shankar Acharya temple on Takht-i-Sulaimān;
a later note puts it on the S slope of the hill, at the SE corner of Srinagar (uncertain). Cunningham (p. 108):
near the old capital (Pandrethan), no site. Beal (p. 158, tentative): 10 li SE of the new city, N of the old one, S
of "Hariparvata, now Takht-i-Suliman". Beal conflates the two hills. Watters gives no site.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** Everyone agrees on the area between Srinagar and Pandrethan. No monument is named, and the
gazetteers add nothing.

### 03.06.08 衆賢論師故伽藍 — Saṃghabhadra's monastery: around Avantipur, by distance

### 03.06.10 商林伽藍 — the "merchant's wood" monastery: unlocated

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the reading (商林 or 買林) is disputed and the site is "未詳" (unknown). The text puts it

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji reports Julien's reading 買林 (Vikrītavana), followed by Beal, and Watters' "merchants'

### 03.06.11 大衆部伽藍 — the Mahāsāṃghika monastery: Baramula–Rampur stretch, no site

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Bāramūla–Rāmpur stretch of the Jhelum, but "no monastery site reported on the N
bank" (uncertain). Beal gives no site.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Recent scholarship.** Hassan, Shah and Yatoo (2026, pp. 89, 95), a study of bank erosion, describe the Zehanpore
Buddhist site: three stupas in a line on the right (N) bank of the Jhelum, c. 10 km from Baramula (34°10′43″N
74°15′20″E), with no date, excavation or inscription in their account. They do not connect it with this monastery;
after Ray (1969) they link it with the Jayendra vihāra (p. 90; now 03.06.x2), which the Life puts in the capital
(至都，止闍耶因陀羅寺), so that link does not fit Xuanzang. Yatoo (2012, pp. 173–174, 335–337) surveyed a transect (no. 10,
2009) on the N bank just below Baramula, c. 3–6 km downstream: five sites, all pottery scatters except the excavated
Fathgarh temple (c. AD 550), among them Shalwajin in Sheeri village (34°11.029′N 74°18.596′E) with early- and
later-historic pottery; no Buddhist structure. The transect stops c. 5 km short of Zehanpore.
**Assessment.** The monastery is unlocated beyond the area. Mizutani's "no monastery site on the N bank" no longer
holds: the Zehanpore stupas stand on the N bank inside his stretch, a candidate on position alone, and c. 51 km from
Srinagar in a straight line is long for 140–150 li. Untested; low.
