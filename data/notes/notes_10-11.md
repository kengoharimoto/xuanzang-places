# Notes on disputed places: juan 10–11

Eastern India, the Deccan and the south, Siṃhala, western India, Sindh, Persia. The places follow master-table order.
Sources are `places.tsv`, `places_mizutani.tsv`, `coded/auth_*.tsv`, the gazetteer rows in `coded/gaz_batch*.tsv`
the Archaeological Survey rows in `coded/asr_batch*.tsv` (ASR volume and page), the Annual Report rows in
`coded/ar_batch*.tsv` (AR = Director-General's Annual Report, EC = Eastern Circle report; year and page), the
inscription rows in `coded/epig_batch*.tsv` (EI = Epigraphia Indica, CII = Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum; volume and
page), and the post-1953 excavation rows in `coded/iar_batch*.tsv` (IAR = Indian Archaeology – A Review; year and page).
Gazetteer volumes are counted once, even when two scans exist. The hit numbers (G#) point to `gaz_hits.tsv`.

### 10.01 伊爛拏鉢伐多國 (Hiraṇyaparvata) — Monghyr; secure

**Identifications.** Mizutani: capital Monghyr (Mudgagiri) (agreed). Vivien (p. 386), Cunningham (pp. 545–6),
Beal (p. 186), Watters (p. 179, tentative), Majumdar (p. 722) and Dey all give Monghyr. They differ only over the
Sanskrit form of the name.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR XV, 1879–80, p. 16) keeps Monghyr and proposes a new source for the name: the local
Harana-Parvata, from the Kashta-harana ghat, "is clearly the original of Hwen Thsang's I-lan-na-Po-fa-to", not
Julien's Hiraṇya ("Hill of Gold"). The site does not move.

**Gazetteers.** Monghyr 1909 and Monghyr 1960 accept Monghyr and argue for it from the old names Mudgalapuri,
Mudgalāśrama and Mudgalagiri, and from the Pāla inscriptions' Mudgagiri (G#269). They add that the sage's hermitage
at Kashtaharini ghat "is still a Hindu pilgrimage place" (G#270/1584). Bengal 1902 repeats the identification.

**Assessment.** Nobody contests the site. The survey's Harana etymology and the gazetteers' local names bear only
on the name, and Mizutani stands.

### 10.01.02 伊爛拏山 — the hill at Monghyr; Mizutani's Pirpahar is a guess

**Identifications.** Mizutani: perhaps Pirpahar hill, 3 miles E of Monghyr (uncertain). Cunningham (p. 545) and Beal
(p. 186) give the isolated hill at Monghyr. Vivien (p. 386) gives the range of hills ending at the Ganges at Monghyr,
with a chain of hot springs (the "smoking" mountain).

**Gazetteers.** Monghyr 1909 and Monghyr 1960 follow Waddell. They read the smoke and vapour as "historic testimony
to the former existence in this region of an active volcano", shown by the hot springs of the Kharagpur hills
(Sitakund, Bhimbandh, Rishikund) (G#54/1558). They name no single peak.

**K. Harimoto's notes.** Explains the 含吐煙霞 of the hill by the hot spring Sita Kund (or, doubtfully, volcanism), which
agrees with the hot-spring reading of Vivien and the gazetteers.

**Assessment.** Everyone puts the mountain in the Monghyr hills. Vivien and the gazetteers favour the hot-spring
range over any single hill. Pirpahar (Mizutani's own suggestion) has no support in these sources, but it does not
contradict them. Mizutani's general location stands. The specific hill remains open.

### 10.01.04 小孤山 — Uren hill (Waddell); the survey's Mahadeva has no remains, Uren has inscriptions

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Uren hill, 20 miles SW of Monghyr (agreed, after Waddell). Cunningham (ASR XV, as
reported by Beal p. 190; not in the 1871 Geography): Mahadeva hill, E of the central Monghyr hills. Watters (p. 180)
and Majumdar (p. 722): Uren.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR XV, 1879–80, pp. 18–19) proposed "the hill of Mahadeva and the hot springs of
Bhimband", 11 miles from the Kiyul on the western frontier of Monghyr district: an isolated two-peaked hill whose
springs are "the hottest in the district". On the ground he found that "Nothing in fact now remains save the two
peaked isolated hills and the hot springs".

**Gazetteers.** Monghyr 1909 and Monghyr 1960 give Waddell's answer to the survey:
- Mahadeva is doubtful because its springs lie on the hill itself and it has "no remains but a small brick linga
  shrine" (G#264/1568).
- Uren's position and shape fit; its top is called Lorik ka ghar, and the yakṣa's name Bakula survives in the
  village Bakura and the cult of Ban-Bakura Nath.
- A footprint rock has a brick mound above it "which may mark the remains of a small stupa" (G#278/1580), with
  votive statues, chaityas and carved names. Bloch (ASI) dates the stupa tracings near Xuanzang's time (G#281/1583).

Jaynagar (G#266/1570) and Bateshwar-Sthan (Bhagalpur 1962, G#1432–3) are passing suggestions.

**Inscriptions.** Sircar (EI 28) edits fourteen image inscriptions from Uren, two of the reign of Rāmapāla and two in
Bhaikṣukī script, and notes stūpa engravings on the hill and rock sculptures destroyed by quarrying. He accepts
Waddell's identification "with one of the Buddha's hermitages on the western frontier of the country of
I-lan-na-po-fa-to". The records are Pāla-period (11th–12th c.): they show a Buddhist site, not one of Xuanzang's day,
and name no hermitage.

**Assessment.** Mahadeva rests on the two peaks and the springs; Cunningham's own visit found nothing of the cave,
stupa or footprints. Uren has the footprints, a stupa mound and now a body of Buddhist inscriptions. Mizutani is
strengthened.

### 10.02 瞻波國 (Campā) — Champanagar near Bhagalpur; secure

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Campānagara and Campāpura near Bhagalpur (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 546–7), Beal
(p. 191), Watters (p. 182) and Dey agree. Vivien (p. 386) gives only the distance from Monghyr.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR XV, 1879–80, pp. 32–33) keeps Champa at Bhagalpur. He identifies the herdsman's long
gallery of the Life with the Mayaganj cave at Bhagalpur, where punch-marked silver coins had been found, while
admitting "there are no rocks at the mouth of the Mayaganj cave at the present day" (see the final section).

**Gazetteers.** Bhagalpur 1911, Bhagalpur 1962, Santal Parganas 1910 and Bengal 1902 repeat this and Cunningham's
boundaries of Aṅga (Lakhisarai to Rajmahal). Bhagalpur 1962 puts forward Bateshwar-Sthan (near Colgong) as the
ancient site (G#1432–3). Its reason is that Faxian's 13 yojanas from Pāṭaliputra (about 144 miles) could fit if the
Ganges has changed course. The gazetteer itself says "later researches and excavations may be awaited".

**Assessment.** The Bateshwar suggestion concerns Faxian's Campā and the Vakula hill. It brings no evidence against
Champanagar. Mizutani stands.

### 10.02.02a 孤嶼 — the rocky islets at Kahalgaon (Colgong), with the temple ruins on the summit

**Identifications.** Mizutani identifies only the landscaped pool, perhaps the Gaggarāpokkharaṇī. He leaves the
island itself unidentified (uncertain). Cunningham (1871, p. 546): the rocky island opposite Patharghata, 24 miles E
of Bhagalpur. Vivien (p. 386): the rock of Patharghat. Beal (p. 192) reports Cunningham's later choice, Kahalgaon.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR XV, 1879–80, pp. 34–35) moved a few miles upstream from Patharghata: "Both bearing and
distance point to the rocky hill of Kahalgaon (Kolgong of the maps), which is just 23 miles to the east of
Bhagalpur." There are three rocky islets; the Ganges has since left them, but the old channel survives as the
Marganga ("dead Ganges"), still the main channel when he steamed up it in 1837. On the summit terrace stood "a large
brick temple with white sandstone pilasters and doorway", door jambs still standing, with heaps of large bricks and
carved bricks — "no doubt the ruins of the temple seen by Hwen Thsang". A small rock-cut temple lies below the
summit.

**Gazetteers.** Bhagalpur 1911 echoes the survey: the islet at Colgong has "the only rock-hewn temple in Bengal",
and "On the summit of the same rock there was a large temple in Hiuen Tsiang's time"; it notes the sacred northward
bend of the river (G#16). The 1911 and 1962 volumes repeat the old district gazetteer's remark that the temple
"appears to have been visited" by Xuanzang (G#166/1848).

**Assessment.** Distance, bearing, an island once in the main stream, and a ruined temple on the summit all fit.
This fills the gap Mizutani leaves; his Gaggarā pond lay at Campā itself, not 150 li east.

### 10.03 羯朱嗢祇羅國 (Kajaṅgala) — Rajmahal (Kankjol); the minority sites lack support

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Rajmahal, formerly Kānkjol (agreed). Cunningham (p. 548): the Rajmahal district, named
after Kankjol, 18 miles S of Rajmahal. Majumdar (p. 723): Kajaṅgala, capital at Rajmahal. Vivien (p. 387): the
village of Kadjéri, below Farrakabad, opposite Gaur. Beal (p. 193, tentative): Kajeri on Rennell's map, 92 miles
from Campā. Dey: Kajra station in Monghyr district, which is west of Campā. Watters (p. 183) gives no site.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR XV, 1879–80, pp. 37–39) repeats that the 400 li from Champa point to the neighbourhood
of Rajmahal, then adds that Kankjol itself, "not more than 70 miles from Bhagalpur by the direct route through the
hills", has "at least as good a claim as Rajmahal to represent the capital". He names no remains there.

**Gazetteers.** Santal Parganas 1910 repeats Cunningham, noting that Kankjol is "still extant 18 miles S of
Rajmahal" (G#1). Hooghly 1972 and Howrah 1972 repeat Majumdar's "territory around Rajmahal".

**Assessment.** Dey's Kajra lies in the wrong direction from Campā. The Kajeri of Vivien and Beal rests only on a
name on a map. The survey leaves the choice between Rajmahal and Kankjol open within the same district, and Mizutani
stands.

### 10.03.01a 大臺 — Teliagarhi (Cunningham, ASR XV)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no book identifies the brick-and-stone platform on the northern border near the
Ganges (unknown). None of the coded authorities name it.

**Survey.** The identification is Cunningham's (ASR XV, 1879–80, p. 39). The walls and towers of Teliyagarhi
(Teliya-gali), "built in alternate courses of stones and bricks", recalled the tower built "partly of stone and
partly of brick"; with its place on the northern frontier and the south bank of the Ganges, "this tower was most
probably situated at Teliya-garhi itself". The place, an old military post commanding a pass into Bengal, had held
many Buddhist and Brahmanical statues, most since moved to Kahalgaon and lost. He himself calls the mixed masonry
"the common style of building in this part of the country from time immemorial".

**Gazetteers.** Santal Parganas 1910 (G#2/85) quotes this survey passage. The "panels filled with figures of saints,
Buddhas and Devas" it cites are Cunningham's paraphrase of Xuanzang's tower, not a description of Teliagarhi.

**Assessment.** This is the only candidate in these sources, against Mizutani's "unidentified". It rests on
position and on a building style that Cunningham admits was general; no part of the tower was found. Worth
recording, with low confidence.

### 10.04 奔那伐彈那國 (Puṇḍravardhana) — Mahasthan; found by the survey, settled by the inscription naming Pudanagara

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Rajshahi–Bogra area, capital Mahāsthān (agreed). Vivien (p. 388): Burdwan. Cunningham
(1871, pp. 549–50): Pubna. Fergusson (in Watters p. 184, Beal p. 194): near Rangpur. Westmacott (in Beal): Panjara or
Pandua. Majumdar (p. 724) follows Cunningham's later Mahasthan and rejects Pubna. Dey: North Bengal.

**Survey.** Cunningham gave up his Pubna in ASR XV (1879–80): "I was fortunate enough to discover the site of the
ancient city in Mahasthan on the Karatoya River" (p. v). His grounds (pp. 102–112): Mahasthan lies "just 100 miles to
the east" of Rajmahal (600 li); Bhasu Bihar, 4 miles W, matches Po-shi-po; the city with suburbs is "rather less than
4½ miles" round, against 30 li. He found nothing Buddhist at Mahasthan itself.

**Excavations.** Dikshit (EC 1920–21, p. 23) still found the identification "not universally accepted by scholars".
In 1931 a Mauryan Brāhmī inscription turned up near the site: its "distinct mention of Pudanagara (Sanskrit
Pundranagara) ... confirms the identification of Mahasthan" (Dikshit, AR 1930–34, p. 219; AR 1929–30, p. 142).

**Inscriptions.** Bhandarkar's edition of the Mahasthan plaque (EI 21) — an order to the mahāmātra of Puṇḍranagara to
relieve a famine — "places beyond all doubt the identification of Pundravardhana with Mahasthan"; CII 3 (p. 286)
repeats this and accepts Bhasu Bihar as Po-shi-po. The Damodarpur plates of 443–534 (Basak, EI 15) and the Gupta
records summarised in CII 3 (p. 101) show the Puṇḍravardhana-bhukti as the Gupta province of North Bengal.

**Gazetteers.** Bogra 1910 adds the Karatoyā Māhātmya, the Skanda temple site and a Buddhist temple found in 1907,
with no Buddhist remains at Pabna or Pandua (G#122/127/133). Malda 1918's Pandua concedes the distance problem.

**Assessment.** Distance, Bhasu Bihar and the circuit made Mahasthan probable; the Mauryan inscription naming the
city, found in its ruins, settles it. The plaque is some nine centuries older than Xuanzang, but the Gupta bhukti
records carry the name to his time. Mizutani is strengthened, with high confidence.

### 10.05 迦摩縷波國 (Kāmarūpa) — W Assam with Gauhati; the "great river" crossed was the Karatoyā

**Identifications.** Mizutani: W Assam, capital Gauhati, with the great river taken as the Brahmaputra (disputed).
Cunningham (pp. 573–4): capital Kamatipura in Cooch Behar, with the river taken as the Tista. Watters (p. 186),
Majumdar (p. 729), Beal (p. 195), Vivien (p. 389) and Dey give W Assam/Gauhati or the Karatoyā-to-Gauhati tract.

**Gazetteers.** Bengal 1902 and Bogra 1910 (citing Gait and the Yoginī Tantra) say of the old Karatoyā: "It was no
doubt the great river crossed by Hiuen Tsiang on his way to Kamarupa" (G#123/1920). Their evidence is that the
Karatoyā was the western boundary of Kāmarūpa and that the Bakhtiyar Khilji narrative describes it as three times
the width of the Ganges.

**Inscriptions.** The Nidhanpur plates of Bhāskaravarman, Xuanzang's host, were found in Sylhet and issued "from his
camp at Karnasuvarna" (Padmanatha Bhattacharya, EI 12, pp. 65–66; EI 19): they attest the king and his reach into
Bengal, not his capital. Sircar (EI 32, Howraghat plates) states that Xuanzang "entered the Kamarupa country from the
west after having crossed the Ka-lo-tu or Karatoya", and CII 3 (p. 22) takes Kāmarūpa as W Assam with N Bengal.

**Assessment.** None of these sources touches the capital: Gauhati stands, and Majumdar removes Cunningham's reason
for Kamatipura, since Pubna is not Puṇḍravardhana. The gazetteers and Sircar identify the river crossed at the start
of the 900-li march as the Karatoyā near Mahasthan, not the Brahmaputra or the Tista, which fits Mahasthan for
10.04. Mizutani stands for the capital; the river is corrected.

### 10.06 三摩呾吒國 (Samataṭa) — east of the old Brahmaputra, capital near Comilla; fixed by inscriptions

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Ganges delta, capital Bād-Kāmta near Comilla (disputed). Cunningham (pp. 574–6): the
delta, capital probably Jessore. Fergusson (in Watters, Majumdar): Sonargaon. Watters (p. 188): Faridpur district.
R. D. Banerjee (in Majumdar pp. 729–31): Comilla. Dey: Comilla, Noakhali and Sylhet from the epigraphy. Vivien
(p. 390): Dacca province and the Sundarbans. Beal: Eastern Bengal.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR XV, 1879–80) equates Samataṭa with Bāgdi, the delta between the Jalangi and the Meghna,
with Bikrampur its chief town. He does not name Xuanzang's capital.

**Excavations.** Dikshit (AR 1921–22, p. 65) takes an undug Gupta-brick stūpa mound at Bharat Bhayna, W of Khulna,
as "probably" one of the 30 monasteries; the dating rests on brick size.

**Inscriptions.** Bhattasali (EI 17) edits the Baghaura image inscription (Brahmanbaria, Tippera; reign of Mahīpāla),
which places the donor's village Bilakīndaka — Bilakenduai near Baghaura — in Samataṭa: "it definitely settles the
position of the kingdom". He adds that Kāmarūpa–Samataṭa "is pretty accurately the distance between Gauhati and
Comilla", and that the Khaḍga plates from Asrafpur (on the old Brahmaputra) and Queen Prabhāvatī's image from
Deulbadi (at the foot of the Tippera hills) mark the western and eastern limits of the Khaḍga kingdom of Samataṭa in
the 7th century. In EI 27 he rejects the 24 Parganas, Jessore and Bakarganj. CII 3 (p. 22) accepts his country and
reports his Bad-Kamta capital as his "opinion".

**Gazetteers.** Jessore 1912 and 24 Parganas 1914 reject Jessore on the distances and suggest Dacca or Bikrampur
(G#42/10); Nadia 1910 includes Nadia; Khulna 1908 excludes Khulna; Faridpur 1908 and Bengal 1902 equate Samataṭa with
Vaṅga.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 10 takes Faridpur district
(Watters 1904) here, not Mizutani's Bad-Kamta (Barkamta), W of Comilla (route/README.md §7).

**Assessment.** The gazetteers had already moved the capital east of Jessore. The inscriptions now fix the country
east of the old Brahmaputra, in Tippera, Noakhali, Sylhet and E Dacca, with a Buddhist royal house there in
Xuanzang's century — against Jessore, Faridpur and Khulna. The Baghaura record is c. 10th-century, and no inscription
names the capital, so Bād-Kāmta remains Bhattasali's reasoned choice. Mizutani is strengthened.

### 10.06.02a 室利差呾羅國 (Śrīkṣetra) — Hmawza near Prome; an excavated city of the right age, the wrong bearing

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Thare Khettara near Prome (uncertain). He notes that Prome lies SE, not NE, of
Samataṭa. Beal (p. 200, tentative) and Dey agree. Vivien (p. 391) and Cunningham (p. 576): Sylhet (Śrīhaṭṭa).
Watters (p. 189): roughly the Tipperah district.

**Excavations.** Taw Sein Ko's trial excavations at Hmawza, 1907–10 (quoted by Marshall, AR 1907–08, pp. 41–2; AR
1909–10, pp. 113–14): "The site of Srikshetra is now called Yathemyo ... and is five miles to the east of Prome". He
found ramparts, walled enclosures and pagodas, gold scrolls with the Buddhist creed in a 7th–10th-century script, and
Gupta-style sculpture of the 7th century or earlier. The name Śrīkṣetra comes from the Burmese chronicles; nothing
found on the site names the city.

**Inscriptions.** Two epigraphists offer Bengal candidates. Padmanatha Bhattacharya (EI 12, p. 67) reports that
"our Bengali writers ... have found no difficulty in identifying Shihlichatolo with Srihatta" (Sylhet), adducing a
Śrīhaṭṭa epithet from the Lakkha Mandal praśasti. Bhattasali (EI 17), emending NE to SE from Comilla, reaches
Chittagong, "anciently called Sri-Chattala". Both rest on a name; neither cites a find.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** Prome has the matching name but not the direction; Sylhet has the direction, Chittagong the emended
direction, and both only a looser name. Bhattasali's emendation to SE is the same correction Mizutani's Prome needs.
The excavations give Hmawza a large walled Buddhist city of Xuanzang's time, which no Bengal candidate shows. Mizutani
is strengthened, with medium confidence.

### 10.06.02b 迦摩浪迦國 (Kāmalaṅkā) — Pegu and the Irrawaddy delta

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Pegu and the Irrawaddy delta (uncertain). Beal (p. 200): the same, as Rāmañña.
Watters (p. 189) reports the same. Cunningham (pp. 576–7, tentative): the Comilla district in Tipera.

**Gazetteers.** Tippera 1910 repeats Cunningham's Comilla: "This Kamalanka is generally identified with Comilla"
(G#138), with no evidence beyond Cunningham.

**Inscriptions.** Padmanatha Bhattacharya (EI 12, p. 67) gives Comilla too, attributing it to Watters and Smith; the
coded Watters only reports Pegu. No inscription is adduced.

**Assessment.** The gazetteer's and Bhattacharya's "generally" overstates Cunningham's tentative suggestion. The
equation with Yijing's 郎迦戍 supports Pegu. Mizutani stands.

### 10.06.02c 墮羅鉢底國 (Dvāravatī) — Dvāravatī (Siam)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Ayutthaya, as Dvāravatī (agreed). Dey: Siam. Beal (p. 200): the classic name of
Sandoway (Arakan), also applied to Siam. Watters (p. 189) rejects Dvāravatī and reads Talapati. Cunningham (p. 577):
perhaps Talaingvati, that is, Pegu.

**Gazetteers.** Tippera 1910 says that "perhaps To-lo-po-ti may stand for Tippera" (G#138). This is a guess from the
sound of the name.

**Assessment.** The reading Dvāravatī, the Mon kingdom of Siam, is the best supported. The Tippera guess adds
nothing. Mizutani's equation with Ayutthaya specifically is a precision issue, not a rival site. Mizutani stands.

### 10.06.02d 伊賞那補羅國 (Īśānapura) — Cambodia

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Zhenla/Cambodia, capital perhaps Sambor Prei Kuk (agreed). Watters (p. 189):
Cambodia. Cunningham (p. 577): the Shan country or Laos.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** Īśānapura is Īśānavarman's capital of Zhenla, so Cunningham's Laos is superseded. Mizutani stands.

### 10.06.02e 摩訶瞻波國 (Mahācampā) — Champa (Linyi)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Champa (Linyi), SE Indochina (agreed). Julien (p. 85), Watters (p. 189) and
Cunningham (p. 577): Champa, Cochin-China or Annam. Dey lists "Siam, according to Hiuen Tsiang" (from Beal's Life),
besides Yule's Tonquin–Cambodia.

**Excavations.** Duroiselle (AR 1925–26, p. 117) examined Sampanago (Champanagara) near Bhamo in Upper Burma, which
Vidyavinod had proposed for "Maha-Chan po", and rejected it with Finot, who keeps Annam. The site is an enclosure of
about a square mile with small stūpas. Its wall bricks are no older than the 13th–15th century, the Hmannan
chronicle does not mention it, and "it seems to lack any antiquities that would take us back to the time of Hiuen
Tsang".

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** Dey's Siam is a dictionary cross-reference, not an argued identification, and the only Burmese
rival, Sampanago, fails on its remains. Mizutani stands.

### 10.06.02f 閻摩那洲國 (Yamanadvīpa) — unidentified; Java doubtful

**Identifications.** Mizutani: perhaps Java (Yavadvīpa) (uncertain). Cunningham (p. 577): "almost certainly"
Java. Watters (p. 189): "it cannot possibly have been the island of Java". Beal (p. 200): the Yamadvīpa of the Vāyu
Purāṇa, probably fabulous.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** These sources settle nothing. Java rests on emending the name, so Mizutani's "perhaps" is the right
level of confidence.

### 10.07 耽摩栗底國 (Tāmralipti) — Tamluk; secure

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Tamluk (agreed). All the authorities agree: Vivien p. 392, Cunningham p. 577, Beal
p. 200, Watters p. 189, Majumdar p. 732, Dey. Fergusson's Satgaon is rejected by Watters.

**Gazetteers.** Midnapur–Hugli–Howrah 1875–77 argues that Tamluk, now about 60 miles from the sea, was once a port.
Workers "come upon sea shells at a depth of from ten to twenty feet" in wells and tanks, and the place had the old
name Ratnākar (G#337/359). Midnapore 1911, Howrah 1909, Howrah 1972 (with recent ASI excavations at Tamluk) and the
other Bengal volumes repeat the identification.

**Assessment.** The gazetteers explain why an inland town could be Xuanzang's seaport, and so strengthen Mizutani.

### 10.08 羯羅拏蘇伐剌那國 (Karṇasuvarṇa) — Rangamati: the Raktamṛttikā sealings at Rajbadidanga fix the capital beside them

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Rāṅgāmāṭi, Murshidabad (agreed; no rival reported). Majumdar (p. 733, tentative),
Smith (in Watters p. 192) and Dey: Rangamati; Majumdar also reports Fleet's Rohtasgarh. Cunningham (1871, p. 578): Singhbhum–Barabhum, capital near Bara Bazar. Vivien (p. 392): the Suvarṇarekhā country.

**Survey.** Beglar (ASR VIII, pp. 51, 191): Sapharan near Dulmi in Manbhum, from loose sculptures and the name.

**Excavations.** Dikshit (EC 1920–21, pp. 33–34) inspected Beveridge's site: the Rākṣasa-ḍāṅgā mound, the Rajbari or
"Raja Karna's palace" mounds at Jadupur, later-Gupta gold coins. The city "was in existence at the time of Hieun
Tsang's visit", but whether the capital was here "is however a matter of doubt".

**Inscriptions.** Seventh-century grants name the city as a royal seat — Jayanāga's, issued at Karṇasuvarṇaka
(Barnett, EI 18; find-spot unknown), and Bhāskaravarman's Nidhanpur plates, issued "from his camp at Karnasuvarna"
(EI 12, pp. 65–6) — and Śaśāṅka's Ganjam plates of 619–20 (EI 6) show his reach; none locates it.

**Later excavations.** S. R. Das (University of Calcutta) dug Rajbadidanga, Dikshit's Jadupur mounds, 1962–82. A Phase III (6th–7th c.) sealing named the "Rakta-mrittika-mahavihara" (IAR 1962–63,
p. 46); more sealings named "the illustrious Raktamrittika-vihara and its acharya-bhikshu", dated 6th–10th c., Phase III
by C-14 to 1200 ± 80 BP (IAR 1963–64, p. 63); in 1972–73 a seal read "Sri Raktamrittika Mahavihar[ry]a-bhikshu
Sanghasya" (IAR 1972–73, pp. 37–38). Das draws the consequence: "Karnasuvarna, the exact identification of which has been
hitherto uncertain, is to be located in the neighbourhood of Rajbadidanga" (IAR 1966–67, p. 47; "firmly identified", IAR 1968–69, p. 43).

**Gazetteers.** Murshidabad 1914 doubts the name argument (G#59/284); Manbhum 1911 echoes the Manbhum sites (G#141–2).

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 10's distances and bearings takes Bara
Bazar (Barabhum) (Cunningham 1871) here, not Mizutani's Rangamati (Karnasuvarna), Murshidabad (route/README.md §6).
The figures are one kind of evidence; see the assessment.

**Assessment.** The sealings name the monastery, not the city. But Xuanzang puts Raktamṛttikā "beside the great city"
(大城側), and the sealings span his date, so the capital must lie at Rangamati–Jadupur, where Dikshit saw a city of
his time; the city itself has not been dug in these sources. The 1914 name objection and Beglar's Sapharan lapse.
Mizutani is strengthened, now with high confidence.

### 10.08.02 絡多末知僧伽藍 (Raktamṛttikā) — Rajbadidanga at Rangamati; named on its own sealings

**Identifications.** Mizutani: at Rāṅgāmāṭi, whose name derives from it (agreed). Julien (in Beal p. 202) gives the
Sanskrit form. Majumdar (p. 733) places it near the capital, identified with Rangamati.

**Gazetteers.** Murshidabad 1914 (G#59/283–4) attacks the name argument: "the name Rangamati is not uncommon, being
derived from the red laterite soil".

**Excavations.** Dikshit's 1920–21 inspection (EC 1920–21, pp. 33–34; see 10.08) showed a city of Xuanzang's time at
Rangamati.

**Later excavations.** S. R. Das's excavation of Rajbadidanga, Jadupur, "over 1 km from Chiruti station" (IAR 1962–63
to 1981–82), found a monastery of five or six structural phases from the 3rd–4th to the 12th century, with stūpa
basements, a granary and a pañcāyatana temple-complex. Sealings name the house: "Rakta-mrittika-mahavihara" from
Phase III, 6th–7th c. (IAR 1962–63, p. 46); "the illustrious Raktamrittika-vihara and its acharya-bhikshu" (IAR
1963–64, p. 63); "Sri Raktamrittika Mahavihar[ry]a-bhikshu Sanghasya", with "several inscribed seals bearing
Raktamrittika legends" (IAR 1972–73, pp. 37–38). The seal-bearing levels run c. 5th–6th to 9th–10th c. (IAR 1968–69,
p. 43), covering Xuanzang's visit. Das: "the site has been identified with the illustrious Raktamrittikasangharama
described by the Chinese pilgrim" (IAR 1966–67, p. 47).

**Assessment.** A monastery named on its own sealings, in levels of Xuanzang's century, on the site Mizutani gives:
the identification is settled, and the 1914 objection to the place-name no longer matters. Mizutani is strengthened,
with high confidence.

### 10.09 烏荼國 (Oḍra) — northern Orissa, north of the Mahānadī; capital most often Jajpur

**Identifications.** Mizutani: N Orissa, capital undecided (disputed). He lists Cuttack, Midnapore (Fergusson),
Jajpur, Yajapur (Watters) and Dhauli/Tosalī (高桑). Cunningham (pp. 584–6), Beal (p. 204) and Vivien (p. 394,
tentative): Jajpur on the Baitaraṇī. Watters (p. 194) and Dey: Orissa only.

**Gazetteers.** Balasore 1907 and Cuttack 1906 repeat "The capital, which has been identified with Jajpur ... lay
700 li south-west of Tamralipti" (G#11/182). Puri 1977 traces the route from Karṇasuvarṇa (G#1528).

**Inscriptions.** Chakravarti (EI 21), from the find-spots of the Śailodbhava plates naming Koṅgoda-maṇḍala (Cuttack,
Khurda, Ganjam), concludes that Koṅgoda "extended from the southern bank of the Mahanadi" — which leaves Oḍra north of
the river.

**Assessment.** The country is not in doubt; the capital remains open, with Jajpur, north of the Mahānadī, the most
widely held candidate. If Chakravarti's boundary held in Xuanzang's time, Dhauli/Tosalī and Bhubaneswar on the south
bank fall into Koṅgoda, which tells against 高桑's Dhauli (and raises the same question for Khandagiri, 10.09.02).
Mizutani stands.

### 10.09.02 補澁波祇釐僧伽藍 (Puṣpagiri) — Khandagiri/Udayagiri

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Khandagiri, 4 miles W of Bhubaneswar (agreed). Cunningham (p. 587): the Udayagiri and
Khandagiri hills. Dey (tentative): Udayagiri. Beal (p. 205, tentative): perhaps a stupa found near Aska, or else
Cunningham's hills.

**Gazetteers.** The only coded row (Puri 1977, G#1528) concerns the route from Karṇasuvarṇa and says nothing of
Puṣpagiri. It is miscoded here.

**Later excavations.** G. C. Chauley (ASI, IAR 1985–86, pp. 62–63) found in a hill-top stūpa at Lalitgiri (Cuttack),
N of the Mahānadī, three relic caskets, two holding gold caskets with bone relics, and suggested that "it might be
the same stupa noticed by Hiuen-Tsang", without naming which; later Lalitgiri seasons (IAR 1986–87, 1989–90) do not
repeat it.

**Assessment.** The difference between Udayagiri and Khandagiri is one of precision. Beal's Aska is a passing
alternative. Lalitgiri is a passing suggestion, though it lies N of the Mahānadī, inside Oḍra on Chakravarti's
boundary (10.09), where Khandagiri may not. Mizutani stands.

### 10.09.03 折利呾羅城 (Caritra) — port unidentified; Puri doubted, the Prāchī mouth suggested

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Puri or Nendra on the Mahānadī (disputed). Cunningham (pp. 584–5, tentative): Puri.
Waddell (in Watters p. 195, provisionally accepted) and Majumdar (p. 734): Chitratola at Nendra, 15 miles below
Cuttack. Vivien (p. 394): perhaps the Tchatta of the chronicles. Beal (p. 205): Ptolemy's port of departure, with no
site given.

**Gazetteers.**
- Puri 1977 doubts Puri: "the restoration of the word Che-li-ta-lo as Charitra and its identification with the town
  of Puri are open to doubt" (G#1525). Puri 1908 says that Puri cannot be shown to be one of Kongoda's towns (G#308).
- Puri 1908 also proposes a new site. "Tradition speaks of an old port at its mouth", the mouth of the Prāchī, once
  navigable and lined with temples, brick houses and mounds, "which, if true, might, with some reason, be identified
  with the port of Che-li-ta-lo" (G#307).

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 10's distances and bearings takes
Nendra (Chitratola), 15 mi below Cuttack (Waddell (via Watters 1904, Majumdar 1924)) here, not Mizutani's Puri
(route/README.md §6). The figures are one kind of evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 10 takes Nendra (Chitratola),
15 mi below Cuttack (Waddell (via Watters 1904, Majumdar 1924)) here, not Mizutani's Puri (route/README.md §7).

**Assessment.** The gazetteers weaken Puri. They add the Prāchī-mouth port near Konarak, a candidate no authority
records, though it rests on tradition only. Nendra (Waddell) remains the better-argued alternative to Puri.

### 10.10 恭御陀國 (Koṅgoda) — the Śailodbhava Koṅgoda-maṇḍala, from the Mahānadī into N Ganjam; capital on the Sālimā

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Ganjam district, capital Jaugada (agreed). Cunningham (p. 587): Ganjam, the bay being
the Chilka. Fergusson (in Beal p. 206, Watters p. 196): Khordhagarh near Bhubaneswar, or between Cuttack and Aska.
Majumdar (p. 734): the Koṅgoda-maṇḍala of the inscriptions. Vivien (p. 395): near the Chilka.

**Gazetteers.** Puri 1908 argues for Puri district, capital "probably old Tosali or medieval Bhubaneswar", from the
name Kongoda in "copper-plate inscriptions found in Khurda subdivision and the Ganjam Tributary States" (G#74/303).
Puri 1977 repeats Ganjam (G#1529).

**Inscriptions.** Kielhorn (EI 6, pp. 136–7) first equated the Koṅgoda-maṇḍala of two Ganjam grants with Kong-yu-t'o,
noting that their northern alphabet matches Xuanzang's remark that the script is that of Mid-India; the find-spots
support both Cunningham and Fergusson. Hultzsch's Ganjam plates of 619–20, under Śaśāṅka, were issued from
"victorious Kongoda", a town "on the bank of the Salima river" (EI 6, p. 144). Chakravarti (EI 21, Nivina plate):
since plates naming Koṅgoda-maṇḍala come from Cuttack, Khurda and Ganjam, the province "extended from the southern
bank of the Mahanadi and included within it the northern portion of Ganjam", its capital perhaps on the Salia near the
Chilka. Hultzsch (CII 1) gives Jaugada's Aśokan name as Samāpa.

**Assessment.** The plates fix the country: a coastal province from the Mahānadī across the Chilka into N Ganjam,
ruled by Śaśāṅka's Śailodbhava feudatories at Xuanzang's date. Mizutani's region is confirmed but too narrow. His
capital, Jaugada, has no 7th-century claim in these sources; the inscriptions point to the Śailodbhava town of
Koṅgoda on the Sālimā, perhaps the Salia near the SW Chilka (unexcavated, and the river equation is Chakravarti's
guess). Alternative, medium confidence for the region, low for the capital.

### 10.11 羯𩜁伽國 (Kaliṅga) — region clear; capital Kalingapatnam (Salihundam) or Mukhalingam

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Kaliṅga S of Ganjam, capital undecided (disputed). Cunningham (pp. 590–1): Rajamahendri
or Koringa. Fergusson (in Watters p. 198): near Kalingapatam. Vivien (p. 395): Kalingapatnam. Smith (in Watters) and
Majumdar (p. 735): Kaliṅganagara, that is, Mukhalingam. Beal (p. 207): probably Rajamahendri. Dey lists Kaliṅga's
capitals at different periods.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Inscriptions.** Ramachandran (EI 28) publishes the Salihundam hill, 3 miles from Kalingapatnam: an apsidal caitya and
a mahācaitya with crystal reliquaries, and a 2nd-century Brāhmī pot inscription naming the monastery the
Kaṭṭahārāma. He concludes that "Fergusson was right in placing the capital city of Kalinga near modern Kalingapatam"
and fits the Salihundam stupas to Xuanzang's stone stupa. Sircar (EI 35, in a footnote) prefers Mukhalingam.

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 10's distances and bearings takes
Rajahmundry (Cunningham 1871; Beal 1884) here, not Mizutani's Kalinga (coast S of Ganjam) (route/README.md §6). The
figures are one kind of evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 10 takes Rajahmundry
(Cunningham 1871; Beal 1884) here, not Mizutani's Kalinga (coast S of Ganjam) (route/README.md §7).

**Assessment.** Salihundam gives Kalingapatnam a large Buddhist establishment, but one named in the 2nd century, not
the capital; and the stone stupa Ramachandran matches stands on a ridge "in the north of the country" (10.11.02a),
which a coastal hill near Kalingapatnam does not obviously suit. Mukhalingam keeps the attested name Kaliṅganagara.
Kalingapatnam and Mukhalingam are the two serious candidates; Rajamahendri falls away. Mizutani's "undecided" is fair.

### 10.11.02a 大山嶺上石窣堵波 — Mahendragiri

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Mahendragiri (uncertain, after Beal p. 209, "perhaps"). Cunningham (p. 592): the
Mahendra Male range at the head of the Rishikulya, Ganjam.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** The two identifications agree in substance on the Mahendragiri massif on the northern border of

### 10.12 憍薩羅國 (Dakṣiṇa Kosala) — capital Sirpur (Śrīpura), confirmed by the Somavaṃśī plates and excavation

**Identifications.** Mizutani: upper Mahānadī, capital Sirpur (disputed). Cunningham (1871, pp. 595–6): Vidarbha,
capital Chanda. Fergusson (in Beal p. 209, Watters p. 199): Wairagarh or Bhandak. Beal: Nagpur or Amaravati.
Majumdar (pp. 735–6): Sirpur. Vivien (p. 396): near the Wainganga–Wardha confluence. Dey: Gondwana.

**Survey.** Cunningham abandoned Vidarbha in ASR XVII (p. 69) for Mahā-Kosala (Chhattisgarh), naming no capital.

**Gazetteers.** Bhandara 1908 calls Chanda "in the last degree hypothetical" (G#394); Drug, Raipur and Bilaspur
volumes connect Bhandak with Sirpur (G#382/380/392); Raipur 1973 reports the Saugar excavation (G#1467–8).

**Inscriptions.** Hira Lal (EI 10, p. 28) asserts Bhandak as the capital "which Hiuen Tsiang visited". Mirashi and
Pandeya answer it: the Mallar plates of Mahāśivagupta (first half of the 7th c.) show Śrīpura as the Somavaṃśī capital
from Tīvaradeva to Mahāśivagupta Bālārjuna, while Bhandak lay in Vidarbha (EI 23); the Bonda plates (EI 35) make
Mahāśivagupta the king of Kosala to whose court "evidently" Xuanzang came. CII 3 (pp. 13–14) puts Dakṣiṇa Kosala in
Raipur and Bilaspur, and Śrīpura its capital "certainly" by c. 800.

**Later excavations.** Dikshit (Saugar University) found two monasteries at Sirpur, one built by the monk Ānandaprabhu
under Bālārjuna (IAR 1954–55, pp. 24–25; he dates the king to the 8th century), and in the habitation mound a Period II
of "about the seventh century" with "large-scale structural activity" (IAR 1955–56, p. 26). Joshi and Sharma (IAR
1999–2000, pp. 96–97) exposed inside the fortified area a Buddha temple with an attached vihāra (a nunnery, from the
glass bangles), with 5th-century Brāhmī mason's marks. Sudevarāja's plates from Mahasamund, in box-headed Brāhmī,
were "issued from Sirpura" (IAR 1979–80, Epigraphy), so Śrīpura was a royal seat before the Somavaṃśīs.

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 10's distances and bearings takes
Chanda (Cunningham 1871) here, not Mizutani's Sirpur, Chhattisgarh (route/README.md §6). The figures are one kind of
evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 10 takes Chanda (Cunningham
1871) here, not Mizutani's Sirpur, Chhattisgarh (route/README.md §7).

**Assessment.** Bhandak and Chanda rest on assertions; Sirpur has the royal plates of Xuanzang's date naming Śrīpura
as capital, a city with major 7th-century building, and Buddhist foundations from the 5th century on. Mizutani is
strengthened, with high confidence.

### 10.12.05 跋邏末羅耆釐山 (Bhramaragiri) — Bhaṃgara-pavata at Arang, SW of Sirpur, against Śrīparvata at Nāgārjunakoṇḍa

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Faxian's Pārāvata, perhaps Nāgārjunakoṇḍa (uncertain). Dey: Śrīśailam. Burgess (in
Watters p. 208): Śrīparvata/Śrīśailam. Cunningham (1871, p. 601): a rock monastery W or SW of Chanda. Beal (p. 214),
assuming Bhandak: the Winjhasani and Dewala hills.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR XXI, pp. 93–94) withdrew his Chanda site: the position "has not yet been ascertained".

**Gazetteers.** The coded rows concern Faxian's monastery only (Thana 1882, Ratnagiri 1962, Salem 1883).

**Inscriptions.** Two inscriptional names compete. Vogel (EI 20) reads in the Ikṣvāku inscriptions of Nāgārjunikoṇḍa a
vihāra on Siripavata (Śrīparvata), which he takes for Nāgārjuna's; a 3rd-century pillar there calls Vīra-Puriṣadata
"Siripavatadhipati" (IAR 1967–68, p. 52). Mirashi and Pandeya (EI 23) note that a Brāhmī inscription at Arang names a
"Bhaṃgara-pavata" (Skt. Bhṛṅgāra-parvata), and that "Arang lies south-west of Sirpur as required by the Chinese
pilgrim's description".

**K. Harimoto's notes.** Faxian's 達嚫 cave monastery 波羅越 ("pigeon") is described almost exactly as Xuanzang's 跋邏末羅耆釐; 鴿
may come from parvata taken as pārāvata; and 黑蜂 might be a corruption of 黑峰. Against the last point, 跋邏末羅 transcribes
bhramara independently of the gloss, 黑蜂 is its standard gloss, and Arang's Bhaṃgara-pavata is also 'bee'.

**Nagasawa.** Nagasawa takes Faxian's 達嚫 as Dakṣiṇa, location unknown, 200 yojanas S of Kauśāmbī (p. 127), and
compares Xuanzang's Bhramaragiri, which he glosses 黒峰山 'black peak' (p. 128). Taishō and CBETA read 黑蜂 with no variant
noted, so 黒峰 is his rendering, not a reading of the text.

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 10's distances and bearings takes
Winjhasani/Dewala hills near Bhandak (Beal 1884) here, not Mizutani's Nagarjunakonda (route/README.md §6). The figures
are one kind of evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 10 takes Pantlu-kuri, 45 mi W
of Chanda (Cunningham 1871) here, not Mizutani's Nagarjunakonda (route/README.md §7).

**Assessment.** Xuanzang puts Bhramaragiri 300 li SW of the Kosala capital. With Sirpur now secure (10.12), Arang has
the bearing and a hill-name close to Bhramara (both bhramara and bhṛṅga mean "bee", glossed 黑蜂 in the text; this
point is not made in these sources). Śrīparvata at Nāgārjunakoṇḍa has the Nāgārjuna association and the name of
Faxian's monastery, but lies far to the south, outside Kosala. Arang is the better-supported candidate for Xuanzang's
mountain, though nothing Buddhist is reported on its hill and the Arang record is known here only from Mirashi's mention:
alternative, low confidence.

### 10.13 案達羅國 (Andhra) — capital Veṅgī at Peddavegi, excavated 1984–87

**Identifications.** Mizutani: capital Veṅgī, that is, Pedda and Cinna Vegi near Ellore (agreed). Dey, Beal (p. 217)
and Majumdar (p. 737), after Fergusson: Veṅgī. Cunningham (p. 604): Elgandel, between Bhimgal and Warangal. Vivien
(p. 395): Warangal. Watters (p. 210): perhaps Vinjir.

**Gazetteers.** Salem 1883 repeats Burnell: P'ing-ch'i-lo is Vengi "with the addition of the locative suffix of
Telugu nouns" (G#471).

**Later excavations.** Bopardikar (ASI, IAR 1984–85, p. 7) began at Peddavegi, 12 km NNW of Ellore, "historically known
as Vengi", citing Xuanzang's Ping-ki-lo: an ashlar wall with moonstone steps, two circular structures (probably
stūpas), a brick shrine, and a fortification round the village. I. K. Sarma (IAR 1985–86, pp. 4–6) dug the
Dhanam-Dibba stūpa, recalling the "Stupa caused by Asoka-Maurya at Ping-Ki-Pu-lo", but dated it to the 4th century
(Sālaṅkāyana); in Phase IB (late 5th–7th c.) "The stupa was no more found to be important" and Brahmanical temples
were built, which fits Xuanzang's Andhra. Later seasons (IAR 1986–87, pp. 15–21) added a gravel rampart with moat, Viṣṇukuṇḍin coins in Phase IB, and in Phase IC
early Eastern Chalukya Viṣamasiddhi coins (AD 600–625) with "large scale temple activity", that is, the city of
Xuanzang's day. Reused pillars carry 3rd-century Brāhmī records of a monastery's pupil. Nothing found names Veṅgī,
and no stūpa is shown to be the one Xuanzang saw.

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 10's distances and bearings takes
Elgandal (Cunningham 1871) here, not Mizutani's Pedavegi near Eluru (route/README.md §6). The figures are one kind of
evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 10 takes Elgandal (Cunningham
1871) here, not Mizutani's Pedavegi near Eluru (route/README.md §7).

**Assessment.** Elgandel and Warangal are Telingana guesses from distance. Veṅgī matches the name, Burnell's Telugu
locative explains the last syllable, and the excavation shows a fortified city with stūpas, occupied under the first
Eastern Chalukyas exactly when Xuanzang passed. Mizutani is strengthened.

### 10.13.03 孤山 — Dignāga's hill; a Puri claim outside Andhra

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no identification (unknown). He gives only the translation, "20 li SW of the
monastery". None of the coded authorities name it.

**Gazetteers.** Puri 1977 (G#1533) proposes the Biswanath hill near Jagadalpur, N of Delang station: "supposed to be
the same as the Bhorasaila ... the seat of the activities of the Buddhist logician Dignaga". It adds that "the name
of the village Delang is considered to be a prakrit variant of the name Dignaga".

**Assessment.** This is a new candidate, but it lies in Oḍra, not in Andhra where Xuanzang puts Dignāga's hill. Its
only evidence is an etymology. "Bhorasaila" may also echo Bhramaragiri (10.12.05). The question stays open, and the
Puri claim should be recorded as low confidence.

### 10.14 駄那羯磔迦國 (Dhānyakaṭaka) — capital at Dharanikota: the name on the spot, a fortified river town

**Identifications.** Mizutani: capital near Dhāraṇikoṭa, S bank of the Krishna (disputed; Mizutani's choice).
Cunningham (p. 611), Beal (p. 220) and Dey: Dharanikota. Fergusson, Burgess and Sewell (in Watters p. 216 and
Majumdar p. 737): Bezwada. Watters prefers Bezwada; Majumdar says Dharanikota "does not suit". Vivien (p. 397):
Rajahmundry.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Inscriptions.** The name belongs to the Amaravati–Dharanikota pair. Amaravati inscriptions name the town
Dhānyakaṭaka/Dhānyaghaṭa, and records of 1182 and 1234 still call it Śrī-Dhānyakaṭaka with its lofty caitya (Chanda,
EI 15). Seshadri Sastri (EI 24), editing a dharmacakra pillar inscription from Dharanikota, discusses the forms
Dhaṃñakaḍa/Dhaṃñakaṭaka and reads in a second record there "ayirana Puvaseliyana"; Sircar (EI 35) gives Dhanyakataka
as "Dharanikota near Amaravati". Vogel (EI 20) raised Nāgārjunikoṇḍa, where Puvasela is named, as a possible capital; Seshadri Sastri reports the suggestion only.

**Later excavations.** Venkataramayya and Raghavachari (ASI, IAR 1962–63 to 1964–65) cut across the rectangular fort at
Dharanikota on the right bank of the Krishna: an embankment-cum-wharf on a navigation channel cut through laterite,
later a defence wall, a reservoir (Hamsala-Gundam), NBP and Rouletted wares, and a habitation sequence from megalithic
Black-and-red Ware to a grey-ware period. No 7th-century level is reported, and the reports do not mention Xuanzang.

**Assessment.** Bezwada rested on its setting (10.14.03); Dharanikota now has the city's name in inscriptions on the
spot and a fortified river town, which Bezwada lacks in these sources. Nāgārjunikoṇḍa was a 3rd-century Ikṣvāku
capital and has no claim on the name. Mizutani is strengthened, with medium confidence, since the town is not shown
to have stood in the 7th century.

### 10.14.02a 弗婆勢羅僧伽藍 (Pūrvaśaila) — the Amaravati stupa site, most likely

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Amarāvatī great stupa site (agreed). Burgess (in Dey): the same. Watters
(p. 221): "Neither Amaravati nor Vaikunthapur can be said to have any claim". He reports Rea's Pedda Madur.

**Excavations.** Krishna Sastri (AR 1923–24, p. 93): a c. 2nd-century inscription from Allur, Kistna district,
records gifts to the Pūrvaśailiyas, "evidently to be connected with the convent Purvasila ... mentioned by Yuan
Chwang".

**Inscriptions.** The Dharanikota pillar mentions "ayirana Puvaseliyana", Pūrvaśaila monks at the capital itself
(Seshadri Sastri, EI 24). The Nāgārjunikoṇḍa inscriptions name a locality Puvasela and the Pubba- and Apara-selika
sects, which Vogel (EI 20) and Sircar (EI 34) derive from Xuanzang's two convents E and W of the capital. Sircar
(EI 35) takes a fragment of an Aśokan-type pillar at Amaravati to make the great stupa Aśokan.

**Later excavations.** The base of the Amaravati stupa was traced and relic caskets found (IAR 1958–59); inscriptions
of the 2nd–3rd c. BC suggest a Mauryan foundation (IAR 1959–60, p. 53); a clearance on the W side found two
Sātavāhana phases and one early medieval (IAR 1971–72, p. 1). None names Pūrvaśaila.

**Assessment.** With the capital at Dharanikota (10.14), the great stupa a mile to the east answers to the eastern
convent, and Pūrvaśaila monks are attested at Dharanikota. The Nāgārjunikoṇḍa Puvasela shows the name was not
unique, so the identification remains inferred. Mizutani is strengthened, with medium confidence.

### 10.14.02b 阿伐羅勢羅僧伽藍 (Avaraśaila) — on a hill W of Dharanikota; not Dharanikota itself

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Dhāraṇikoṭa, 1 mile W of Amarāvatī, with the capital between the two monasteries
(agreed). Fergusson (in Beal p. 221 and Watters p. 216): the Amaravati tope itself. Beal finds the position a
difficulty. Watters rejects both Amaravati and Vaikunthapur.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Inscriptions.** Vogel (EI 20) and Sircar (EI 34) connect the Apara-selika sect of the Nāgārjunikoṇḍa inscriptions
with Xuanzang's western convent, "on the hills ... to the west ... of Dhanyakataka (modern Amaravati)"; neither
locates it.

**Later excavations.** The IAR excavations of 1962–65 show Dharanikota as a fortified town with wharf and reservoir
(see 10.14), not a monastery.

**Assessment.** The text puts Avaraśaila "west of the city, resting on a mountain". If the fortified town at
Dharanikota is the city, as the inscriptions and excavations now suggest, Avaraśaila lay on high ground west of it,
not at Dharanikota itself; Mizutani's own arrangement, with the capital between the two convents, implies the same.
Fergusson's Amaravati tope is ruled out by the bearing. Mizutani is weakened in detail; site unlocated, low
confidence.

### 10.14.03 大山 (阿素洛宮) — the isolated rock S of Bezwada

**Identifications.** Mizutani: none (unknown). Watters (p. 221): "the isolated steep mountain to the south of
Bezwada". Beal (p. 223): the isolated rock with caves immediately S of Bejwada, from Boswell's report via Fergusson.
Sewell (in Watters): the rock-cut temple at Undavalli.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** The only candidate in these sources is the Bezwada hill, which presupposes a capital at Bezwada.
Recorded as an alternative to Mizutani's blank, with low confidence.

### 10.15 珠利耶國 (Coḷa) — the Telugu Coḷa country (Renāṇḍu) of Cuddapah

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Penner basin with Nellore (disputed; "諸種の異説"). The authorities disagree:
Fergusson (in Watters and Beal) Nellore; Cunningham (p. 624) Karnul; Smith (in Watters p. 225) Cuddapah district;
Majumdar (p. 738) Coḷa feudatories in Cuddapah, Kurnool and Bellary; Vivien (p. 399) midway between the Krishna and
the Penner; Dey the Coromandel coast.

**Gazetteers.** Cuddapah 1915: "The doubt has now been cleared up. The Choolya State referred to by Hiuen Tsiang was
the Telugu Chola kingdom occupying most of the black cotton country of this district", from inscriptions naming the
country Renāṇḍu "(the 7000)" (G#457–8).

**Excavations.** Konow (AR 1904–05, pp. 128–9) announced a hitherto unnoticed Coḷa dynasty in Cuddapah that "might
well correspond" to Chu-li-ye. Venkayya (AR 1905–06, p. 175) argued it from the bearing SW of Dhānyakaṭaka and
8th-century Coḷa inscriptions in N Cuddapah and round Hemāvatī. Marshall (AR 1913–14, p. 28) read the Malepāḍu plates
of Puṇyakumāra as records of rulers of Chuliye.

**Inscriptions.** Krishna Sastri's edition of the Malepāḍu plates (c. end of the 8th c.) and related Telugu-Coḷa
records (EI 11, p. 344) takes them to prove "the existence of a kingdom called Chu-li-ye in the time of the Chinese
traveller".

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 10's distances and bearings takes
Kurnool (Jorampur) (Cunningham 1871) here, not Mizutani's Nellore (route/README.md §6). The figures are one kind of
evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 10 takes Kurnool (Jorampur)
(Cunningham 1871) here, not Mizutani's Nellore (route/README.md §7).

**Assessment.** Bearing and inscriptions agree on a Telugu Coḷa country in Cuddapah and S Kurnool, with Smith,
Majumdar and in part Cunningham. The plates postdate Xuanzang by over a century, and none names Chuliye or a capital.
This is a better-supported alternative to Nellore for the core of the country, though not a capital.

### 10.16 達羅毘荼國 (Drāviḍa) — Kāñcīpura; secure

**Identifications.** Mizutani: capital Kāñcīpura (Conjeeveram) (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 627–8), Beal (p. 228),
Vivien (p. 397), Majumdar (p. 739) and Dey agree. Fergusson (in Watters p. 226, who leans to it, and rejected by
Beal): Nagapattinam.

**Excavations.** None. Venkayya (AR 1906–07, p. 238) calls the Pallava country "Ta-lo pi-ch'a or Dravida", the later
Toṇḍai-maṇḍalam, with Kāñcī as capital; a statement, not an argument.

**Gazetteers.** Malabar 1887 repeats "the Pallava kingdom" with Kāñcī (G#447).

**Assessment.** Kien-chi-pu-lo is phonetically Kāñcīpura, the Pallava capital. Fergusson's Nagapattinam rests only
on his reconstruction of the route. Mizutani stands.

### 10.17 秣羅矩吒國 (Malakūṭa) — far south; capital near Madurai

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Kumārī area, capital Madurai (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 629–30, tentative):

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 10 takes Quilon (Cunningham
1871 (alternative)) here, not Mizutani's Madurai (route/README.md §7).

**Assessment.** Chi-mo-lo (枳秣羅) is Xuanzang's other name for Malakūṭa at the southern tip, so Chaul on the Konkan

### 10.17.03 秣剌耶山 (Malaya) — southern Western Ghats

### 10.17.04 布呾洛迦山 (Potalaka) — the Podiyil/Pāpanāsam hills, or legendary

**Identifications.** Mizutani: possibly legendary, or the Pāpanāsam hill (高桑) (disputed). Majumdar (p. 741, after
Hultzsch): the Podiyamalai hill. Watters (p. 232): "not to be identified with any one of the mountains by the
sea-side". Beal (p. 233): no site.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**K. Harimoto's notes.** Suggests that the port NE of the mountain on the route to Siṃhala could be at Pondicherry
(Arikamedu). Arikamedu is a Roman-period port not shown to be active in the 7th century; recorded as a lead only.

**Bakker.** On Avalokiteśvara appearing as 自在天 or an ash-smearer: Bakker (Gonda Lecture 2016, publ. 2017, p. 15),
after Eltschinger, cites the Kāraṇḍavyūha, where Avalokiteśvara tells Maheśvara that he will be the creator in the
Kali age.

**Assessment.** Hultzsch's Podiyamalai and 高桑's Pāpanāsam probably name the same range above Pāpanāsam in
Tinnevelly (a geographical point not stated in these sources). If so, two independent sources point to one hill,
against Watters' scepticism. Mizutani stands. The
real hill is the better-supported of his two options.

### 10.17.04a 海畔城 — Nāgapaṭṭinam (probable)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: an unnamed port, with Nāgapaṭṭinam the crossing to Ceylon (disputed). Beal (p. 233):
probably Nāgapaṭṭinam, Yijing's Nāgavadana. Burnell: Kāveripaṭṭinam. Cunningham (p. 630): Negapatam if the capital
was Madurai, Ramnad if it was Quilon. Watters (p. 232): "the old Seng-ka-lo" of the Fangzhi.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Inscriptions.** Hultzsch (EI 10), editing a grant of the Chalukya Vikramāditya I issued from a camp at Uragapura on
the S bank of the Kāverī, equates Uragapura with Negapatam and finds it "very probable that Negapatam is intended" by
Xuanzang's hearsay port, with Yijing's Nāgapaṭṭana.

**Assessment.** With Madurai as capital (10.17), Nāgapaṭṭinam is the most consistent choice; Beal, Cunningham and
Hultzsch converge on it, though Hultzsch's Uragapura (uraga = nāga) is a translation of the name, not a find at the
port. Mizutani is strengthened, with medium confidence.

### 11.02 恭建那補羅國 (Koṅkaṇapura) — capital unresolved; Annagundi or Banavasi

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Tungabhadrā–Varadā basins, capital Annagundi (disputed). Yazdani's Kuntalapura
(Kubattūr) is also reported. Cunningham (p. 632) and Dey: Annagundi. Vivien (p. 401): Banavasi. Burgess (in Majumdar
p. 745): Kopal or Kokanur. Beal (p. 254): near Golkonda. Fergusson: the Mysore plateau. Da Cunha (in Dey): Bassein.
Watters (p. 238): "all beset with difficulties".

**Gazetteers.** Rice's Mysore and Coorg 1876 reports Burnell's Kankanhalli ("Konkanahalli") and undercuts it with
Buchanan's local derivation (G#1915–8). Ratnagiri 1962 discusses only the route to Mahārāṣṭra (G#1549/1552).

**Later excavations.** The University of Mysore with the State Department (IAR 1970–71, p. 29) dug at Banavasi on the
Varadā, "probably visited by Hiuen Tsang": a brick fortification with moat, an apsidal brick caitya in the Donigudda
mound — "Traditionally, the mound contained a chaitya, the existence of which was mentioned by Hiuen Tsang" — and a
Kadamba pillar inscription. Poonacha (ASI, IAR 1995–96, p. 44) found Sātavāhana-level terracottas of Hārītī, a
Buddha face and a Bodhisattva, "establishing for the first time the Buddhist antiquity of Banavasi", and states that
Xuanzang "had visited Banavasi (ancient Vaijayanti)".

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 11's distances and bearings takes
Golkonda (Beal 1884) here, not Mizutani's Anegundi on the Tungabhadra (route/README.md §6). The figures are one kind
of evidence; see the assessment.

**Assessment.** Banavasi now has a fortified early city with Buddhist remains, on the Varadā that Mizutani includes
in the country, and it was the Kadamba capital. But the IAR reports assert the Xuanzang link from tradition, without
argument from the route, and the Buddhist finds are Sātavāhana, not 7th-century; nothing names Koṅkaṇapura.
Annagundi has no excavated evidence in these sources. Vivien's Banavasi is therefore an equally supported alternative
to Annagundi, with low confidence for either.

### 11.03 摩訶剌侘國 (Mahārāṣṭra) — capital Nasik, argued by the gazetteers and Mirashi

**Identifications.** Mizutani: capital disputed, inclining to the Nasik area (Pulakeśin II having moved north).
Cunningham (p. 634): Kalyāṇī. Fleet and Burgess (in Watters p. 238) and Majumdar (p. 746): Nasik. Vivien (p. 401):
Paithan or Devagiri. Fergusson: Toka or Paithan. Beal (p. 255): near the Tapti or Girna.

**Gazetteers.**
- Nasik 1977 and Akola 1977 argue for Nasik, following Fleet, Burgess and Mirashi: the cave monastery "seems to suit
  the caves at Ajanta and as this monastery lay to the east of the capital, the latter appears to be Nasik". They add
  Pulakeśin's grant of 630 found at Lohaner in Baglan taluka (G#1553–4/1544).
- Bombay 1909 and Kanara 1883 repeat Burgess' earlier Bādāmi (G#1955/1911).

**Inscriptions.** Mirashi (CII 6) accepts that Xuanzang "seems to have met him at Nasik. As Fleet has shown, this
city answers to the description of Pulakesin's capital", adducing the grant of Jayasiṃha Dharāśraya (653) from Nasik
district.

**Assessment.** The bearing to Ajanta and the Chalukya grants from Nasik district support Nasik. Bādāmi was the
dynastic capital, but nobody argues for it from Xuanzang's data. Mizutani's preference is strengthened.

### 11.03.03 阿折羅伽藍 — Ajanta, not Kanheri

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Ajanta caves (agreed). Beal (p. 257), Dey, Burgess and Watters (p. 238, "This may
be so"): Ajanta. Cunningham (p. 636): Kanheri on Salsette, reading "west"; Ajanta only if the eastern bearing is right.

**Gazetteers.** Thana 1882 rejects Kanheri: Xuanzang "seems not to have heard of Kanheri" (G#1872–3). Nasik 1977 and
Akola 1977 take Ajanta as fixed and use it to place the capital (G#1554).

**Later excavations.** Venkateshaiah (ASI, IAR 1999–2000, p. 102; 2000–01, pp. 92–97) cleared the right bank of the
Waghora opposite Caves 4 and 17: a votive stūpa on a brick platform and a U-shaped brick vihāra of cells opening to
the river, dated 4th–5th c. by pottery and mouldings, with a gold coin of Theodosius in its disuse phase. The reports
do not mention Xuanzang.

**K. Harimoto's notes.** If Faxian's monastery (some take it as Ajanta) is Xuanzang's Kosala Bhramaragiri (10.12.05),
the Ajanta identification needs rethinking. It does not depend on Faxian: Xuanzang's own description, the bearing from
Nasik and the Cave 26 inscription naming the sthavira Acala (known from Burgess and Indraji 1881, not coded here)
carry it.

**Assessment.** The gazetteers dispose of Kanheri, and Ajanta, east of Nasik, fits the bearing. The structural
monastery shows the site's monastic establishment beyond the caves, though its disuse may precede Xuanzang. Mizutani
is strengthened.

### 11.05 摩臘婆國 (Mālava) — central Gujarat, SE of the Mahī; the epigraphists against Malwa

**Identifications.** Mizutani gives two views, central Gujarat (Kaira–Ahmadabad) or Malwa, and leans to Malwa from
the Valabhī inscriptions (disputed). Cunningham (pp. 561–2) and Vivien (p. 403): W Malwa, capital Dhār. Beal
(p. 260): near Dungarpur. Smith (in Watters p. 243): Kaira–Ahmadabad. Majumdar (p. 728) cites the Valabhī record of
639, naming a Mālavaka within Valabhī, as proving "the thesis of Smith". Watters: "not ... the modern district of
Malwa".

**Gazetteers.** Chittaurgarh 1977, Jhalawar 1964 and Sirohi 1967 only repeat (W Malwa; west of Ujjain, "possibly in
Gujerat"; east of the Mahī).

**Inscriptions.** Bhandarkar (CII 3, p. 69) objects to Mālava = Ujjain: "It is forgotten, however, that this Malava
was situated on the south-eastern side of the Mo-ko" (Mahī). Sircar (EI 34) notes that Xuanzang "locates Mo-la-p'o
(Malava) near the valley of the river Mahi", that "some scholars" identify it with the Mālavaka-āhāra of Dhruvasena
II's grants within Maitraka territory, and that Ujjayinī (11.12) was distinct from Mālava.

**Route test (the figures).** From Bharukaccha the text goes 西北 2000+ li to Mālava. Central Gujarat (Kheda) lies 121
km away on a bearing of 345°: close to the stated direction but far short of the distance (about 480 km predicted).
Mizutani's Malwa (Ujjain region) lies 352 km away on a bearing of 61°: near the distance but opposite in direction.
Neither fits both. A Mālava further north-west in Gujarat would (route/README.md §8).

**Assessment.** Xuanzang describes Ujjayana separately, and the Mahī bearing and the Maitraka Mālavaka-āhāra put his
Mālava in central Gujarat, as Smith, Majumdar and now Bhandarkar and Sircar hold. Mizutani's reading of the Valabhī
evidence runs the other way. Central Gujarat is the better-supported view, though no record fixes a capital. The
bearing favours Gujarat, but the stated distance points further north-west than Kheda: alternative, medium confidence.

### 11.09 阿難陀補羅國 (Ānandapura) — Vadnagar; the city's own praśasti names it Ānandapura

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Vadnagar (disputed against the older "near Ahmadabad"). Vivien (p. 406), Majumdar
(p. 728), Smith (in Watters) and Dey: Vadnagar. Dey also notes an Anandpur NW of Valabhī. Cunningham (p. 565): Barnagar

**Gazetteers.** Sirohi 1967: "Anandpura (Ahmedabad district)" (G#1946).

**Inscriptions.** Bühler and Ojha (EI 1, p. 296) edit the Vadnagar praśasti of Kumārapāla's reign (V.S. 1208), which praises

Vriddhanagara". Five Maitraka grants name Ānandapura (Sircar and Sundaram, EI 35), and Sircar (EI 35) and the
Gwalior inscription of Alla (EI 1, p. 154, "Anandapura in Latamandala") take it as Vadnagar. Sircar's Anand in Kaira

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 11 takes near Ahmadabad (St.
Martin; Cunningham; Watters (older view, via Mizutani)) here, not Mizutani's Vadnagar (route/README.md §7).

**Assessment.** An inscription on the site names it Ānandapura, and Maitraka grants show a town of that name in
Valabhī's sphere, as Xuanzang's dependency requires. The praśasti is 12th-century, but the name continuity is clear.
Mizutani is strengthened, with high confidence.

### 11.10 蘇剌侘國 (Surāṣṭra) — Kathiawar, capital Junāgaḍh (Girinagara); named on the Girnar rock

**Identifications.** Mizutani: S Kathiawar, capital Junāgaḍh (Girinagara) at the W foot of Girnār (agreed).
Cunningham (pp. 372–4) and Majumdar (pp. 697–8): capital Junagadh. Vivien (p. 405) and Watters (p. 249): Junagadh,

**Assessment.** The province, the hill and the town are named together on the rock beside the town two centuries

### 11.11 瞿折羅國 (Gurjara) — capital Bhinmal (Bhillamāla/Śrīmāla); R. C. Majumdar's Mandor rests on distance

**Identifications.** Mizutani: S Rajasthan, capital Bhinmāl (majority) against Bārmer (disputed). Majumdar (p. 697)
and Dey: Bhinmal. Vivien (p. 407), Cunningham (1871, p. 357) and Beal (p. 270): Barmer (Balmer). Watters (p. 250):
perhaps Bhilmala.

**Survey.** Cunningham repeats Balmer in passing in ASR II (cf. p. 34); he visited neither place.

**Gazetteers.** Western Rajputana States 1909: "The old name of the place was Bhinmal or Bhillamala (the Pi-lo-mi-lo
of the Chinese pilgrim)", with a ruined gate, mounds, tanks and Sanskrit inscriptions (G#1949). Pali 1976, Sirohi
1967 and the Bombay volumes repeat Bhinmal; only two 1870s volumes echo Balmer.

**Inscriptions.** R. C. Majumdar (EI 18; not the Majumdar of the 1924 Geography) rejects Bhillamāla, which he gives
to the Cāpa kingdom, as "far from satisfactory in view of its distance from Valabhi", and proposes Gurjaratrā, "the
country round about Mandor", 300 miles N of Valabhī, matching the pilgrim's pious young king to Tāta of Bauka's Jodhpur
genealogy. Sircar (EI 32, 34) keeps Pi-lo-mo-lo = Bhillamāla, Bhinmal.

**Later excavations.** Bhinmal, "the ancient Srimala ... the capital of the Gurjaras in early medieval times", was
dug by the Rajasthan department (medieval brick structures; IAR 1953–54, p. 12). A Songira Cāhamāna inscription of
V.S. 1328 found there records a king "ruling from Srimala" (IAR 1968–69, p. 49).

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 11's distances and bearings takes
Barmer (Balmer) (Vivien 1858; Cunningham 1871; Beal 1884) here, not Mizutani's Bhinmal (route/README.md §6). The
figures are one kind of evidence; see the assessment.

**Assessment.** Majumdar's Mandor is argued from a distance and a dynastic list, without a name answering to
Pi-lo-mo-lo. Bhinmal has the transcribed name in its old forms, ruins, and an inscription on the site naming it
Śrīmāla, though late. Mizutani is strengthened, with high confidence.

### 11.13 擲枳陀國 (Jejākabhukti) — Bundelkhand, named in inscriptions; capital Khajuraho or Eran

**Identifications.** Mizutani: undecided between Bundelkhand (capital Khajurāho) and Chitor (disputed). Cunningham
(1871, pp. 551, 554), Vivien (p. 408) and Dey: Jajhoti/Bundelkhand, capital Khajuraho (or Mahoba). Watters (p. 251, in
Majumdar p. 725): Chitor.

**Survey.** Cunningham's view moved twice:
- ASR II (1864–65, pp. 412–428): Jajhoti is Bundelkhand, from the Jajhotiya Brahmans; the capital Khajuraho, whose
  ruins correspond "almost exactly" with the 15–16 li, with a pedestal inscribed *ye dharmā hetu*.
- ASR IX (1873–74, p. 65): following Hall, Chi-ke-da = Chedi on the upper Narmadā.
- ASR XXI (1883–84, pp. 58, 72): back to Bundelkhand: Mahoba and Madanpur inscriptions name the country
  Jejākabhukti/Jejākasukti; he reads the 1,000 li from Ujjain as 84 kos.

**Gazetteers.** Hamirpur 1909 and Jhansi 1909 keep Bundelkhand (G#555) but prefer Eran on the Bina for the capital on
bearings and distance (G#554/713); Jalaun 1909 repeats Eran; Banda 1909, Saugor 1905 and the 1874 Bundelkhand volume
repeat Khajuraho.

**Inscriptions.** Kielhorn (EI 1, pp. 33–34) reads "the prince of Jejabhuktika (or Bundelkhand)" in the Ratnapur
inscription of Jājalladeva. The editor of a Chandella plate of Devavarman (V.S. 1108, from Kālañjara; EI 20) accepts
Chih-chi-to = Jajhauti and objects only to deriving the name from a king Jejaka.

**Assessment.** The inscriptions and caste names fix the country in Bundelkhand, against Chitor and Chedi; the
epigraphic names are 11th–12th-century. The capital stays open: Khajuraho has the circuit and a Buddhist pedestal but
needs the kos reading; Eran fits the distances.

### 11.14 摩醯濕伐羅補羅國 (Maheśvarapura) — Gwalior; Mandla and Seronj lapse, Maheswar is a name only

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Gwalior region (majority) (disputed). Smith (in Watters) and Majumdar (p. 725):
Gwalior. Cunningham (1871, pp. 559–60): Mandla on the upper Narmadā, reading S for N, which Watters (p. 252) rejects.
Vivien (p. 408): Macheri, W of Agra.

**Survey.** Cunningham dropped Mandla in ASR IX (1873–74, p. 65). Having made 11.13 Chedi, he sought Maheśvarapura
in Central India 900 li NE of Ujjain, as the Life has it: "Seronj, to the north-east of Bhilsa, would suit these
conditions almost exactly" (Ujjain to Besnagar measured at 142 miles by perambulator). The Travels' 900 li north
from Chedi would land "in the neighbourhood of Narwar", which he rejected. He cites no remains at Seronj, and once he
returned 11.13 to Bundelkhand (ASR XXI) the Seronj arrangement lost its frame.

**Excavations.** Srivastava (AR 1935–36, pp. 81–2) says that Maheswar, on the north bank of the Narmadā 28 miles W
of Barwaha (Indore State), "was visited by Hiuen Tsang" and by al-Bīrūnī. He reports old deposits c. 30 ft below the
top near Mandel Khoh, with bricks of 20 × 11 × 3 in. that he takes back to the 6th century. He gives no argument from
the route; the link is the name alone, and Maheswar lies south of Ujjain, against the Life's north-east and the
Travels' north.

**Gazetteers.** Hamirpur 1909 takes Gwalior as Maheśvarapura in its reckoning for Eran (G#554). It argues only from
bearings.

**K. Harimoto's notes.** Suggests Shivpuri or Mandla. From the Eran capital of 11.13, Gwalior lies due north at c. 235
km (≈ 900 li); Shivpuri lies NNW at c. 155 km, short of the distance.

**Assessment.** Seronj fits the Life's distance from Ujjain, but not the Travels' 900 li north of a Bundelkhand
Jejākabhukti. Maheswar fits neither text's direction. Gwalior agrees with the text's "north" without emendation and
with the Eran reconstruction of 11.13. Mizutani stands.

### 11.15 信度國 (Sindhu) — capital Alor

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Upper Sind, capital Alor (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 284–8) and Vivien (p. 409): Alor.
Majumdar (pp. 690–1) reports Branfill's Vijnot, a ruined town in Ubauro taluk, read as "Vijnavapura". Dey:
Minagar. Haig (in Watters p. 253): Xuanzang's Sindh is "not the Sindh of any period".

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Mirchandani.** Mirchandani (ABORI 57, 1976, pp. 87–90) argues that Xuanzang's 信度 lay west of the Indus opposite
Multan, ruled by a Śūdra king, and puts its capital on the Indus near Mithankot (Dera Ghazi Khan district), not at
Alor, from the bearings from Multan and Gurjara and the *Life*'s 700 li from Avaṇḍa (= Kachhi, capital Gandāva).

**Mirchandani 1967.** In "On Hiuen Tsiang's travels in Baluchistan" (JIH 45, 1967, pp. 309–c. 356) Mirchandani takes
the li as c. 329 m (about 5 to the mile) and reads all distances capital to capital (p. 311). He puts 信度 west of the
Indus in the Derajat, its capital near Mithankot, site untraced (pp. 314–21): 1900 li north of Bhinmal, Multan across
the river, the rock salt of the Salt Range, and the riverine strip of Dera Ghazi Khan still called "Sindh". He argues
against Alor (Cunningham, Haig, Smith, Lambrick).

**Gazetteers (site search).** The Sukkur gazetteer (1919) describes the Aror ruins over the old river bed, with very
large "Hindu" bricks and coins (G#1971); the Bombay Imperial Gazetteer (1909) notes carved slabs and brick foundations
at the old Hindu city of Vijnot (G#1969), Banfill's candidate for the capital.

**Route test.** Measured from Gurjara (now corrected; see route/README.md §5), Alor, Vijnot and Mirchandani's
Mithankot score alike (mean |z| 0.42, 0.37, 0.46). The 1900 li north from Bhinmal fit all three.

**Lambrick (Sind, 1964).** Lambrick takes the country as Upper Sind, the Chachnāma's Rai kingdom reaching the Salt
Range. He doubts Alor as the capital: the Indus probably did not then flow east of Alor, and the transcription would
be "A-lo-lo". He puts the capital on the right bank between Pano Akil and Kandhkot, since eroded by the river (pp.
147–48, 166). His li is elastic, about 5 to the mile, 50 li to a day's march of 10 miles.

**Assessment.** Branfill's Vijnot gives a closer sound for Pi-chen-po-pu-lo, but these sources offer no evidence
beyond the name. Alor, the historical capital of Upper Sindh, stands.

### 11.16 茂羅三部盧國 (Mūlasthānapura) — Multan

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Multan (agreed). He notes that the direction is NE, not E. Cunningham (pp. 265, 268),
Vivien (p. 410), Beal (p. 274), Dey and Smith: Multan. Watters (p. 253) calls Mūlasthānapura "an impossible
restoration" because Multan lies far N of Sindh. Majumdar (p. 689) prefers a Mūlasthāna on the Narmadā in Broach
province.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR V, 1872–73, pp. 114–124) traces the name and the sun temple from Xuanzang through
Biladuri, Abu Zaid, Masudi and Istakhri, and measures the city. The fort is 1¼ miles round with 46 bastions; "the
walled circuit of Multan, including both city and citadel, is 15,000 feet, or very nearly three miles; and the whole
circuit ... including the unwalled suburbs, is from four and half to five miles", agreeing with the 30 li. He knew
the walls closely, having dismantled them in 1854; they are Mughal work on older lines.

**Gazetteers.** Mooltan 1884, Mooltan 1902, Punjab 1919 and Puri 1977 repeat Multan. The 1884 volume's golden Sun
image and 30-li circuit (G#1170/1173) echo the survey.

**Assessment.** The sun temple is diagnostic, and the circuit fits, though it was measured on later walls. The
objection from bearing is real but weaker. Mizutani stands.

### 11.17 鉢伐多國 (Parvata) — Harappa; Shorkot abandoned by its proposer

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Harappa (disputed). Cunningham (1871, pp. 233–4): Shorkot. Cunningham later (ASR V, in
Majumdar pp. 687–8) and Fleet: Harappa. Smith (in Watters p. 255): Jammu. Vivien (p. 410): perhaps Fatehpur on the
Ravi.

**Survey.** Cunningham (ASR V, 1872–73, p. 107) withdrew Shorkot after visiting it: "it never could have reached the
size of 20 li", whereas "the size agrees almost exactly with that of Harapa" (about 3 miles round), which "must be the
very city visited by the pilgrim". His digging produced scrapers, pottery and Major Clark's black stone seal.

**Gazetteers.** Jhang 1884 repeats Shorkot, with "a huge mound of ruins about 100 feet in height" (G#1237/1337);
Punjab 1919 puts Po-fa-to NE of Multan.

**Inscriptions.** Chakravarti (EI 31) suggests that the Pavata of the Bandhogarh (Rewa) Brāhmī inscriptions, home of
a merchant's family, is "possibly identical with Po-fa-to". It is a name match, and Rewa lies far from the Panjab
where Xuanzang places Parvata.

**Bakker.** Bakker (in Balogh ed., *Hunnic Peoples in Central and South Asia*, 2020, pp. 277–78) takes the
Kuvalayamālā's Pavvaïyā (Parvatikā), Toramāṇa's residence on the Chenab, to be at Akhnūr–Ambaran (Jammu; ASI
excavations in IAR 1961–63, 1973–74, 1999–2001), the capital of a country Parvata, and links it with Xuanzang's 鉢伐多,
subject to Ṭakka, "roughly the region around Sialkot". Xuanzang's 700 li NE of Multan (c. 170–220 km at the route
model's scale) falls well short of Sialkot or Akhnūr (c. 350–400 km).

**Later excavations.** The Akhnūr sites Bakker relies on were found in 1961–62 (Pande and Sardari Lal, IAR p. 16:
terracotta heads of the 4th–5th and 8th centuries at Ambaran) and 1962–63 (Khazanchi, p. 9). Pambarwan was tested in
1973–74 (Srinivasan, p. 14). Mani dug it in 1999–2001 (IAR 1999–2000, pp. 52–63; 2000–01, pp. 47 ff.). He found a
Buddhist monastery with a Kushan relic stūpa, "believed to be the only early Buddhist site in Jammu region", running
from pre-Kushan to post-Gupta (6th–7th c.). It was flood-prone and "abandoned sometimes around the seventh century
AD". He declines to blame a burnt layer on the Hūṇas. None of the reports mentions Xuanzang or Parvata.

**Assessment.** Shorkot fails on the measured circuit and needs an emended name; Harappa rests on size and position.
The Bandhogarh Pavata does not bear on the site. Bakker's Parvatikā at Akhnūr revives Smith's Jammu with a real
Buddhist monastery of the right period and a Hūṇa-era name, and it fits "subject to Ṭakka"; but Akhnūr is c. 430 km
and Sialkot c. 380 km from Multan, about twice the 700 li. Mizutani's Harappa stands on the distance; Akhnūr is
recorded as an alternative, low confidence (later work, not in these sources, assigns Clark's seal to the Indus
civilization).

### 11.18 阿點婆翅羅國 (Atyanabakela) — lower Sindh/Cutch; capital Karachi, Banbhore (Debal) or Kotesar

**Identifications.** Mizutani: lower Sind, capital Karachi (agreed). Vivien (p. 411) and Lassen: Karachi. Cunningham
(pp. 346–8) and Dey: Cutch, capital Koṭeśvara on the Kori mouth. Majumdar (p. 696): Lower Sindh, not Cutch, since the
capital lay on the Indus.

**Gazetteers.** None in the main gazetteer set (for the Baluchistan volumes see below).

**K. Harimoto's notes.** Argues the capital is Koṭīśvara (Koteshwar, Kutch); takes 翅羅 as -kila (cf. 倶翅羅 = kokila);
notes Lambrick's Debal and Bhambore.

**Other sources.** Watters calls the Koṭīśvara restoration "quite impossible" (II p. 257); Lambrick (Sind, 1964, p.
148) prefers the delta, with Debal as the capital. Al-Bīrūnī puts the eastern Indus mouth "in the province of Kacch at
a place called Sindhu-sāgara" (Sachau I p. 260). The Skandapurāṇa puts the liṅga Sthūleśvara at Śaṅkukarṇa, "at the
confluence of the Sindhu and the ocean" (SP 73.57; SP IV p. 57), which Bakker locates at Banbhore, perhaps Debal (SP
IV p. 55 n. 133). Mirchandani (JIH 47, 1969, pp. 237–51) discusses this capital's name.

**Mirchandani.** Mirchandani (JIH 47, 1969, p. 250, after his JIH 1967 article) puts Atyanabakela in Las Bela,
Baluchistan, and reads the capital as Kiteśvara < Kitaveśvara, = the Arab writers' Armābel near Bela.

**Mirchandani 1967.** Mirchandani puts the country in Las Bela (the Arabs' Armābel) and the capital at Gondakahar, 11
miles NW of Bela (pp. 322–30), reached 1500–1600 li SW from his Sin-tu via Kachhi. He argues against Cutch/Koṭeśvara
(Cutch lies south, not south-west, of Sin-tu and borders neither Makran nor the Indus), against the delta and Debal
(Smith, Lambrick), and against Karachi (Vivien). The Chachnāma's Armābel, with a Buddhist chief subject to Sind in
631, is his main support; he admits the name does not match the transcription. In 1969 (p. 250) he read the capital's
name as Kiteśvara.

**Gazetteers (site search).** Aitken's Sind gazetteer (1907) has Chach, who came to the throne in 631, subdue Las Bela
(G#1967): Las Bela, like the delta, was then subject to Sind, as Xuanzang says of this country.

**Baluchistan gazetteers.** The Las Bela volume (1907, p. 23) and the Imperial Gazetteer (1908, p. 188), after the
Chachnāma, make the 7th-century ruler of Armabel (Bela) a Buddhist Samani subject to Sind, whom Chach visited c. 635
(B001, B009). The same volume doubts that Armabel was Bela itself, suggesting Khairo Kot or a site near the Hor (pp.
198, 207–8; B003–B004). The Gondrani cave city N of Bela is "possibly of Buddhist origin" only in the Imperial
Gazetteer (p. 189; B010); the district volume describes c. 1,500 rock-cut dwellings without religious features (pp.
37–40; B005). Gondakahar is not mentioned.

**Route test.** Gondakahar scores worst when measured from Mizutani's Sindhu (mean |z| 0.86), but Mirchandani's whole
route from Mithankot fits the text best (route/README.md §5).

**Lambrick (Sind, 1964).** Lambrick puts the country in the Indus delta, with Debal as its capital, and rejects
Cutch/Koṭeśvara on the transcription (p. 148).

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 11 takes Kotesar (Koteshwar)
(Cunningham 1871; Dey 1927) here, not Mizutani's Karachi (route/README.md §7).

**Assessment.** These sources split between Karachi or the delta (Debal/Banbhore) and Koṭeśvara in Kutch. 朅 (*khjat)
suits Kacch- better than Koṭi-. A great Śiva shrine at the Indus mouth served by ash-smearers fits this country's
大自在天祠, and the Skandapurāṇa's Śaṅkukarṇa–Sthūleśvara lies there. Mizutani's lower Sindh stands, low to medium
confidence; Banbhore joins Karachi and Koṭeśvara as a candidate for the capital, and Mirchandani's Las Bela (capital
Gondakahar) as an alternative for the country, resting on the Chachnāma's Armābel rather than on the name.

### 11.19 狼揭羅國 (Laṅgala) — Makran; capital 窣菟黎濕伐羅 = Sthūleśvara by name, site open (western Makran by the Xiyu ji's figures, nearer Las Bela by the Life's); Lākoriān weakened

**Identifications.** Mizutani: E Mekran or SE Baluchistan, the capital the great ruined city of "Lākorian" (agreed).
Cunningham reaches "Lakūra" only by changing the bearing from W to NW (AGI p. 356). Watters suggests the capital's
name begins "sthul or sthur" (II p. 257).

**K. Harimoto's notes.** Asks whether Laṅgala could be Lākula, and proposes the capital 窣菟黎濕伐羅 = Sthūleśvara, known
from the Skandapurāṇa and the Śivadharmaśāstra's lists, perhaps al-Bīrūnī's Tawalleshar; otherwise simply a port on
the coast, as the text's 臨大海濱 suggests.

**Other sources.** 窣菟黎濕伐羅 transcribes sthūleśvara closely (窣 as in 窣堵波 stūpa), and Mirchandani (JIH 47, 1969) already
proposed it (SP IV p. 57 n. 136). The Skandapurāṇa places the liṅga Sthūleśvara at Śaṅkukarṇa, at the confluence of
the Sindhu and the ocean (SP 73.57; SP IV pp. 10, 57); among the Śaiva tīrtha lists, only the Śivadharmaśāstra's adds
Sthūleśvara (Kafle, Niśvāsamukha pp. 99, 276). Al-Bīrūnī's coast list has Tawalleshar, 50 farsakh from Daibal towards
Kacch (Sachau I p. 208; cf. Taneshar = Sthāneśvara, I p. 199); his Loharānī is the port at the western Indus mouth (I
pp. 205, 260). Lākula is unsupported: lakula is Lakulīśa's club (Cecil 2020, p. 14).

**Mirchandani.** Mirchandani's Sthūleśvara (JIH 47, 1969, p. 247) rests only on the Devī Bhāgavata's pīṭha
Sthūlakeśvara, which he thought possibly fictitious; he put the city at Kasarkand in Persian Makran (p. 244) and took
Laṅgala as lāṅgala 'palm', for Makran's dates (p. 246). The Skandapurāṇa and Śivadharmaśāstra attestations are new,
from K. Harimoto's note.

**Mirchandani 1967.** Mirchandani makes Laṅgala "Greater Makran" with its capital at Kasarkand in Persian Makran,
reached along the Kech valley, "less than 2000 li" west of Bela (pp. 330–37), against Cunningham's Lākoriān.

**Baluchistan gazetteers.** Lākoriān appears in the Jhalawan volume only as a tract of land, with no ruined city (p.
80; B038). Makran was claimed by Sind in 635–6 and held by it when the Arabs came (Imperial Gazetteer p. 12; Makran
pp. 42–43; B012, B041–B042); the Arab-period capital was Kiz (Kech) (p. 45; B044). An old pilgrim road ran from Las
Bela up the Kech valley into Persian Makran (p. 298; B045).

**Route test.** Kasarkand lies due west of Karachi or Bela at about the stated distance (z +0.2 to −0.3, bearing off
1–18°). Lākoriān lies north (bearing off 83°).

**Stein (MASI 37, 1929; MASI 43, 1931).** At Lākoriān Stein found only Gabar-band dams and a 348-yard irrigation
barrage, with no ruined city (MASI 43, pp. 181–82; S018). A footnote shows that an earlier traveller, apparently
Masson, took the dams for defences (p. 182; S019), which probably lies behind Cunningham's "great ruined city". Kech
(Turbat) has been Makran's chief centre of population from early times, but the Miri mound has only prehistoric and
medieval material (pp. 63–65; S013–S014). Nothing Buddhist was found in Makran.

**Lambrick (Sind, 1964).** Lambrick puts Laṅgala in Las Bela with parts of Jhalawan and eastern Makran, and names no
capital site (pp. 148–49).

**The Life.** The Life (T2053) goes from Laṅgala north-east 700 li to Pītaśilā, whereas the Xiyu ji counts Pītaśilā
700 li north from Atyanabakela. On the Life's leg Kasarkand is far too distant (492 km against about 167 predicted),
while Las Bela or eastern Makran would fit (route/README.md §5).

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 11 takes Las Bela (Bela), with
parts of Jhalawan and E Makran (Lambrick 1964) here, not Mizutani's Lakorian (Lakura), between Khuzdar and Kalat
(route/README.md §7).

**Assessment.** The capital's name is Sthūleśvara; the Skandapurāṇa locates that liṅga at the Indus mouth. Mizutani's
Lākoriān now fails on three counts: the bearing (north, not west), the site (irrigation dams and no ruined city, per
the Jhalawan gazetteer and Stein), and the political note (Makran east of the Persian border was Sind's c. 635, while
狼揭羅 was subject to Persia). Western, Persian Makran fits the bearing, the distance and the subjection to Persia;
Kasarkand on the Kech valley road is a candidate for the capital (Mirchandani), but the Life's leg to Pītaśilā
(north-east 700 li) suits a Laṅgala nearer Las Bela or eastern Makran (Lambrick). Region: Makran, with the capital
probably in the west; Lākoriān weakened; low to medium confidence.

### 11.20 波剌斯國 (Pārsa) — Persia; capital Persepolis/Istakhr

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sasanian Persia, capital Persepolis (Istakhr) (agreed). Vivien (p. 289) and Beal
(p. 277): Persia. Beal does not locate the capital. Dey: the capital Su-la-sa-t'ang-na is a transcription of
"Satarochana", now Shahrud.

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** Dey's Shahrud is an isolated etymology. Mizutani's Persepolis/Istakhr stands.

### 11.20.01a 鶴秣城 — Hormuz

**Identifications.** Mizutani: old Hormuz near Mināb (uncertain). Beal (p. 278): "Ormus?".

**Gazetteers.** None.

**Assessment.** The only proposal, Hormuz, fits the "eastern border" and the name. Mizutani stands.

### 11.20.02b 西女國 — legendary island of women; Minicoy suggested

**Identifications.** Mizutani: a legend, and he judges identification futile (unknown). Watters (p. 257): the
Strīrājya of the Bṛhatsaṃhitā. Beal (p. 279): refers to Marco Polo and Yule.

**Gazetteers.** Malabar 1887 (G#448), quoting Julien's text: "Reference is probably here made to the Island of

### 11.21 臂多勢羅國 (Pitaśilā) — near Hyderabad (Sind), uncertain

**Identifications.** Mizutani: near Hyderabad (agreed). Cunningham (pp. 318, 323): Haidarabad (Nirankot). Haig (in
Watters p. 257): Nagar Parkar. Majumdar (p. 692): NW of Lower Sindh and SW of Middle Sindh, rejecting the delta.
Vivien (p. 412): no comparison possible.

**Gazetteers.** None in the main gazetteer set (for the Baluchistan volumes see below).

**Mirchandani 1967.** Mirchandani identifies 臂多勢羅 with Turān (Jhalawan), capital Kusdar (Khuzdar), reading the name as
Paṭṭa-śilā; 700 li (c. 143 miles) north from Bela matches Bela–Kusdar, 139 miles (pp. 337–43). He argues against
Hyderabad (Cunningham), Haig, and Lambrick's Sehwan. The Chachnāma's march from Armābel through Turān to Kandabil
supports the sequence.

**Excavations (Annual Reports).** Hargreaves (AR 1925–26, p. 82) visited Mini Butt at Khuzdar, Mirchandani's capital:
stone foundations, a tiled room, glazed ware and a reported silver hoard, "an extensive settlement", undated but
probably Islamic. N. G. Majumdar (AR 1930–34) found only a Muhammadan-period fort in the mound at Sehwan, Lambrick's
candidate. Neither report mentions Xuanzang.

**Gazetteers (site search).** Aitken's Sind gazetteer (1907), after the Chachnāma, says that at the Arab conquest the
principal citizens of Sehwan were Buddhists who would not fight for Dahar (G#1966): Buddhists in Lambrick's candidate
town c. 711.

**Baluchistan gazetteers.** The Arabs' Turan had its capital at Khuzdar (Imperial Gazetteer pp. 12, 171; Jhalawan p.
34; B013, B018, B033). The finds at Khuzdar are Islamic: glazed ware at Miri Butt, a Kufic inscription and Ghaznavid
coins (pp. 59–62; B037). Nothing pre-Arab or Buddhist is reported.

**Route test.** Hyderabad fits best when measured from Karachi (mean |z| 0.15); Khuzdar fits only within Mirchandani's
route, measured from Bela (z 0.0).

**Stein (MASI 37, 1929; MASI 43, 1931).** Khozdar is attested in the 7th-century Arab campaigns, but its remains are
Islamic (MASI 43, pp. 13, 171–78; S015–S017).

**Lambrick (Sind, 1964).** Lambrick places Pītaśilā round Sehwan, with Jhalawan as his second choice. Xuanzang
mentions no Indus crossing, so the country lay west of the river. He rejects Hyderabad (Cunningham) and Nagar
Parkar/Umarkot (Haig) (p. 149).

**Assessment.** The three placements differ materially, and none cites remains. Mizutani follows Cunningham, and
confidence is low.

### 11.22 阿軬荼國 (Avaṇḍa) — Middle Sindh (Brahmanabad/Khairpur)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Brāhmanābād or Khairpur (uncertain). Cunningham (pp. 300–1, 310): Middle Sindh,
capital Brahmanabad. Majumdar (p. 691): Middle Sindh. Beal (p. 280): "corresponded with Avanti". Watters (p. 260):
not the Avanti of the canon.

**Gazetteers.** None in the main gazetteer set (for the Baluchistan volumes see below).

**K. Harimoto's notes.** Asks whether Avaṇḍa is al-Mansūra. Al-Bīrūnī says al-Mansūra is Bahmanwa renamed (Sachau I p.
21); it was founded c. 744–74, a century after Xuanzang (Cunningham pp. 310–11), on the site Cunningham already gave
Avaṇḍa. Lambrick puts Avaṇḍa at Badah, S of Larkana (Sind, pp. 149–50).

**Mirchandani.** Mirchandani (ABORI 57, 1976, p. 86 n. 17, p. 90; JIH 45, 1967, pp. 343–49) puts Avaṇḍa in Kachhi
(Kachh Gandāva), NW of Sindh, with its capital at Gandāva.

**Mirchandani 1967.** Mirchandani identifies Avaṇḍa with Budha/Budhiya = Kachhi, capital Kandabil = Gandāva (pp.
343–47), but must emend Xuanzang's 300 li to 500.

**Baluchistan gazetteers.** The Kachhi volume (pp. 24–28) identifies the Arabs' Kandabil with Gandāva, capital of
Budha or Nudha, forced into tribute by Chach c. 635–6 (B015, B017, B025–B029); it prefers the reading Nudha (B026).
Pre-Muhammadan mounds stand at Chhalgari (pp. 25–26; B030). Two Buddhist clay sealings from Tambu in Bhag, with the ye
dharmā formula over five stūpas, are dated by Stein to the 8th–9th century (p. 26; B031): Buddhism in Kachhi close to
Xuanzang's time.

**Route test.** Brahmanabad fits the 300 li from Hyderabad (z −0.1), but its onward leg to Bannu is 810 km against
about 215 km predicted. Gandāva fits within Mirchandani's route: from Khuzdar the unemended 300 li give z +1.1, and
its onward leg to Duki z −0.1.

**Lambrick (Sind, 1964).** Lambrick places Avaṇḍa south of Larkana, with its capital at Badah, and takes its Aśoka
stūpa to be the stūpa at Mohenjo-daro (p. 150).

**Assessment.** Middle Sindh (Brahmanabad/Khairpur) fits the 300 li from Hyderabad but breaks the route onward to
Varṇa. Kachhi has the Arab-period capital Kandabil = Gandāva, tributary to Sind c. 635, pre-Islamic mounds and
Buddhist sealings of the 8th–9th century, and it fits the whole route from Bela through Khuzdar to Duki. It needs no
emendation if the 300 li are taken loosely (z +1.1). Kachhi (capital Gandāva) is now an alternative of equal weight to
Mizutani's Middle Sindh; low to medium confidence for either.

### 11.23 伐剌拏國 (Varaṇa) — Bannu

**Identifications.** Mizutani: probably Bannu (uncertain). Cunningham (p. 97), Majumdar (p. 679, with Stein's
endorsement) and Dey: Bannu. Vivien (p. 414) and Smith (in Watters p. 262): Vaneh or the Gomal valley. Watters:
Cunningham's view is open to "serious exceptions".

**Gazetteers.** None in the main gazetteer set (for the Baluchistan volumes see below).

**Mirchandani 1967.** Mirchandani puts 伐剌拏 in the Bolan–Loralai region, capital at or near Duki (pp. 350–53), against
Bannu (Cunningham, Stein) and Vaneh (Vivien, Smith, Lambrick); the argument depends on his Gandāva for 11.22.

**Excavations (Annual Reports).** Stein (AR 1926–27, p. 250) cleared a Kushan stūpa near Ṭabar-kōt at the entrance of
the Thal plain, with an undisturbed relic deposit, Graeco-Buddhist carvings and Kharoṣṭhī and Brāhmī sherds, "the
first Buddhist ruin discovered in Baluchistan", and surveyed the Duki–Sanjawi mounds of Loralai. This shows Buddhism
in Mirchandani's region, though for the Kushan period, not Xuanzang's.

**Baluchistan gazetteers.** The Loralai volume begins its history only c. 1383 (p. 32; B047). Mounds near Duki are
called old Hindu castles, but undated (p. 44; B048). Stein found that "Monastery Hill" near Loralai was two refuge
forts, with nothing religious (pp. 45–46; B049). Stein's Kushan stūpa near Ṭabar-kōt (AR 1926–27) is the one Buddhist
site in the district. The Imperial Gazetteer, after Cunningham, puts the Life's Opokien (阿薄健, the Afghans) in Zhob (p.
102; B021), on a Gomal–Zhob route.

**Route test.** Bannu is 810 km from Brahmanabad against 900 li (about 215 km); it fits only if Avaṇḍa lay much
further north. Duki fits from Gandāva (z −0.1).

**Stein (MASI 37, 1929; MASI 43, 1931).** Stein restates Bannu for 伐剌拏, citing his 1904–05 report and Cunningham (MASI
37, p. 14; S001). He records that Marquart had once put Banna at Gandāva and then withdrawn it (p. 30 n.; S007).
Buddhist remains on the northern route are Kushan: a small stūpa at Ark-ghundai in the Tochi valley, dated by a
Kanishka coin (p. 15; S002), and possible undated stūpas at Khore-narai and Spin in South Waziristan (pp. 24, 26;
S003–S004). On the southern route, a Sasanian silver coin from Dabar-kot in the Thal plain near Duki (p. 62; S010) is
the only find of about Xuanzang's period. The Kushan stūpa of Tor-dherai lies nearby (S011); the OCR breaks off before
Stein's description of it and Konow's appendix on its inscriptions.

**Lambrick (Sind, 1964).** Lambrick puts Varṇa near Barkhan, rejecting the Gomal/Vaneh view, based on the Life,
because it makes both stage distances wrong (pp. 150, 167).

**Assessment.** Bannu breaks the route from Avaṇḍa unless Avaṇḍa lay far to the north. Duki fits the distance from
Gandāva but has no 7th-century record. The choice follows 11.22: Middle Sindh leaves Varṇa unfixed; Kachhi points to
the Bolan–Loralai country. Low confidence for either.

### 11.23.02 稽疆那國 (Kikāna) — Waziristan (Stein, after his 1926–27 tour); Pishin less likely

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Waziristan, W of Bannu (agreed, after Stein). Majumdar (p. 679): Stein's Waziristan.
Cunningham (p. 99): the Pishin valley near Quetta, the Kīkān of the Arab historians. Vivien (p. 414): Kīkān, near
Shal (Quetta). Watters (p. 262): rejects Cunningham.

**Excavations.** Stein's 1926–27 tour through Waziristan (Tank, Draband, Spinwam, Kurram, the Gumal, Zhob), as
summarised in AR 1926–27 (p. 223), surveyed mounds and forts and matched Xuanzang's description of "Chichiang-na
corresponding to the present Waziristan, and the Qiqan of early Arab history": separate chiefs without a supreme
ruler, sheep and fine horses. The sites he surveyed are prehistoric or late, so the identification rests on text
and terrain, not on a find.

**Gazetteers.** None in the main gazetteer set (for the Baluchistan volumes see below).

**Mirchandani 1967.** Mirchandani makes 稽疆那 the Kīkānān of the Arab writers, the Quetta–Pishin region (p. 354).

**Gazetteers (site search).** The Mooltan gazetteer (1884), after Ibn Haukal, makes the Arabs' Kaikanan "not
identified", probably including Dera Ghazi Khan (G#1970); Mirchandani puts it at Quetta–Pishin.

**Baluchistan gazetteers.** All the Baluchistan volumes put the Arabs' Kīkān or Kaikanan at Nal in Jhalawan, near
Khuzdar (Imperial Gazetteer p. 12; Jhalawan pp. 34–35, 221; Makran p. 44; B014, B023, B034–B035, B043), not at
Quetta–Pishin (B051–B053, except a village Kikani, B052). The name matches 稽疆那 well. Nal's mounds are prehistoric
(B036). Nal lies SW of Duki and far S of Bannu, while the text puts 稽疆那 west of 伐剌拏.

**Stein (MASI 37, 1929; MASI 43, 1931).** Stein bases Waziristan on Xuanzang's description (separate chiefs, fine
horses), the bearing west from Bannu and the Arabs' Qīqān. Al-Balādhurī's raid of 664–65 names Banna together with
Qīqān (MASI 37, pp. 29–30; S005–S006). He has no find in support and does not discuss the Kīkān-at-Nal reading of the
Baluchistan gazetteers.

**Lambrick (Sind, 1964).** Lambrick takes 稽疆那 as the Arabs' Kaikanan in Loralai or Zhob, and sets aside Kaikan near
Nal as not fitting the itinerary (pp. 150–51).

**Assessment.** Cunningham's Pishin rested on the Arab Kīkān. Stein places that Qīqān in Waziristan, and his field
tour fits the description, as Bannu (11.23) does. The Baluchistan gazetteers, however, put the Arab Kīkān at Nal in
Jhalawan, which would follow Mirchandani's southern route rather than Bannu, though Nal is south-west rather than west
of Duki. Mizutani's Waziristan stands with Stein, now with low to medium confidence; Nal is an alternative tied to
11.22–11.23.

## Gazetteer passages coded `new` in this range

- **The herdsman's cave near Campā.** Bhagalpur 1911 (G#17/18, second scan G#165) places at the caves of Barari near
  Patharghatta a legend that "Hiuen Tsiang tells" and calls "The remnants of the petrified man were shown to Hiuen
  Tsiang". The caves have yielded punch-marked coins, and there are rock carvings of 84 sages. The legend (the
  cowherd, the radiant bull, the paradise wood, the swallowed fruit) is in the Life, Book IV, under Campā (Beal 1911,
  pp. 129ff). It is not in the Xiyu ji, which is why the place is missing from the master table. The gazetteer's
  location rests only on the caves' antiquity and their nearness to the Colgong–Patharghata rock (10.02.02a).
- **The great river between Puṇḍravardhana and Kāmarūpa.** Bengal 1902 (G#1920) and Bogra 1910 (G#123, scan 316;
  coded under 10.05) identify it as the old Karatoyā at Mahasthan. See 10.05.
- **The herdsman's cave near Campā (survey).** Cunningham (ASR XV, 1879–80, pp. 32–33) puts the Life's long gallery
  at the Mayaganj cave at Bhagalpur, where punch-marked silver coins were found, because "there is no other now known
  at Bhagalpur", though he admits there are no rocks at its mouth. This differs from Bhagalpur 1911's Barari
  caves near Patharghata (above).
