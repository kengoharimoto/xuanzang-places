# Notes on disputed places: juan 1 and juan 12

Range: ids `01.*` (Gaochang to Kapiśa: the Tarim route out, the Tianshan, Sogdiana, Tukhāra, Bamiyan, Kapiśa) and
`12.*` (the return: Zābulistān, the Kabul basin, Tukhāra, Badakhshan, Wakhan, the Pamirs, Kashgar to Lop).
71 places are noted: 44 because Mizutani marks them `disputed`, `uncertain` or `unknown`, and 27 because the
authorities disagree although Mizutani gives the place as agreed. No gazetteer passage in this range `argues`,
`doubts` or proposes an `alternative`.

**Gazetteer coverage.** British Indian gazetteers barely reach these places. Only one volume deals with the range
in any substance: Atkinson, *Himalayan Districts of the North-Western Provinces* II (1884; vol. AT11). The same text
also turns up as a second scan (ATcu3, Cornell `cu31924023947751`, catalogued as "unidentified 1874"). Its
ethnographic chapter mentions Kapiśa, Hu-phi-na/Kipin and Khie-pan-to, but only in passing. Two Peshawar volumes,
1883 (PA27) and 1899 (PB18), call Kapiśa "Kabul" in a single phrase. None of these passages offers local evidence.
In the notes below, "No gazetteer passage" means the place has no coded hit at all; the authorities carry these
notes.

### 01.01d 徙多河 (Skt. sītā) — the Yarkand river; Dey's Jaxartes is a cosmological reading, not a rival

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Yarkand river (agreed), based on juan 12's own descriptions (烏鎩, 斫句迦). Vivien:
an upper branch of the Yarkand river at Kartchou (p. 426). Beal: probably the Yarkand river, which he glosses as the
Zarafshan (p. 12, tentative). Watters: none. Dey: the Jaxartes (Syr Darya) following Csoma, also the Yarkand river
(tentative).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji: the Yarkand river and the Tarim (p. 42). The addition follows Xuanzang's own juan
12, where the Pamir lake's eastern stream joins the 徙多 and flows on east (12.17.02a); it lengthens the river, not the
identification.

**Assessment.** The Sītā here is one of the four rivers flowing out of the cosmological Anavatapta lake. Xuanzang
names it again in juan 12, where it can only be the Yarkand river. Dey's Jaxartes comes from Csoma's Tibetan
cosmology, and Beal's "Zarafshan" gloss is a slip. Neither is a competing site. Mizutani stands. Confidence medium:
Sītā is a cosmological name, not one continuously attested for the Yarkand river, and the identification rests on juan
12 and the authorities' agreement.

### 01.01e 香山 (Skt. gandhamādana-giri) — cosmological mountain; Kailāsa is a conventional equation

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Mount Kailāsa on the north shore of Lake Mānasa; 大雪山 here means the Himalaya
(uncertain). Watters gives no identification (p. 33). The others are silent.

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji: the Pamirs (葱嶺), rather than the old Kunlun or Mizutani's Mānasa/Kailāsa, without
argument (p. 41). The text supports him more than he says: juan 12 puts the great lake of the Pamir valley "within the
great Congling, at the centre of Jambudvīpa, the highest ground" (據大葱嶺內，當贍部洲中，其地最高也), the place the Anavatapta holds
here, and derives the 縛芻 and the 徙多 from it (12.17.02a).

**Assessment.** This mountain belongs to the cosmological preface, not the itinerary. Kailāsa is the usual
rationalisation. Ji's Pamirs has a footing in Xuanzang's own text: juan 12 puts the centre of Jambudvīpa in the Pamir,
and two of this passage's four rivers, the 縛芻 and the 徙多, are there derived from the Pamir lake (12.17.02a). Neither
equation can be tested; the two are alternatives, low confidence.

### 01.01f 積石山 — the Chinese "Jishi" of the Yellow River source; Amne Machin vs Koko-nor spurs

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Amne Machin range, Qinghai (uncertain), after Zhou Zhenhe. Beal: near the Koko-nor,
possibly the Khadatu-bulak or Tsaghan Ashi-bantu spurs of the Altyn-Tagh (p. 13, tentative). Watters: the Chi-shih of
the *Shujing*, a district in western Gansu (p. 34).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji does not locate the mountain. He says only that the Yellow River rises in the Bayan
Har range in Qinghai, and that the Jishi account follows the erroneous old view of the *Yugong* and the *Shuijing zhu*
(p. 42).

**Assessment.** The name comes from Chinese classical geography (the source of the Yellow River). Xuanzang does
not observe the place himself. All three readings put it on the upper Yellow River in Qinghai/Gansu. They differ
only in how they map a traditional name onto modern relief. Mizutani's Amne Machin is the most specific and is not
contradicted.

### 01.04a 黑嶺 (*Siyāh-kōh, a translation) — the "Black Range" between Bamiyan and Kapiśa; a general range name

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the range crossing central Afghanistan east–west and joining the Hindu Kush to the
ENE, crossed between 梵衍那 and 迦畢試; perhaps Greek Paropamisos (uncertain). "Black" means not snow-capped in
summer (Takata). Beal: the Hindu Kush (p. 54). Watters: the non-snowy ranges bounding Kapiśa on the E, W and S
(native Siah-koh; also Kara Tagh) (p. 37).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji: the Hindu Kush, as his gloss on Hori's "Persian Siyahkoh range" (p. 46). His own
note at Kapiśa (p. 137; see 01.37.03a) instead describes Mizutani's E–W range of central Afghanistan joining the Hindu
Kush to the NE, and again calls it Siyah-Koh, so the gloss at p. 46 is contradicted by Ji himself and has the weakness
of Beal's "Hindu Kush".

**Name.** 黑嶺 is not a transcription: 黑 and 嶺 are the Chinese words "black range", so the key has nothing to test, and
Mizutani's Greek Paropamisos, which is no equivalent, should go. The meaning-equivalent is Persian *Siyāh-kōh "black
mountain" (Hori, via Ji p. 46; Watters' native Siah-koh). In the files, however, Siyāh-kōh also names other ranges
(the range S of the Herat river in Minorsky's Ḥudūd commentary on §5; one in Mangyshlaq), so it is a generic range
name and cannot fix this one. Low confidence (data/names/).

**Assessment.** All three describe the same belt of mountains. Watters' "Siah-koh" (= black mountain) is the closest
to the Chinese name. Beal's flat "Hindu Kush" is too broad. Mizutani stands; for the crossing itself see 01.37.03a.

### 01.04b 高昌 (Qočo) — Kara-khoja; Vivien's Pichan is a capital-site variant

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Kara-khoja, ESE of Turfan (agreed). Watters: chief city of the Turfan district,
represented by Huo-chow = Karakhojo (p. 43). Beal: the Uigur country, capital Turfan (p. 17). Vivien: Turfan
province, with the royal residence probably at Pidjan (Pichan) (p. 263, tentative).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the old Gaochang city c. 60 li SE of Turfan, the district now Qara-hoja, the
city Idiqut-shähri (p. 47).

**Assessment.** All agree on the Turfan oasis. Only the capital is in question, and there Watters and Mizutani
agree on Kara-khoja against Vivien's tentative Pichan. Mizutani stands.

### 01.06.01 國東境城 (大龍池) — "city on the eastern border" of Kucha; position only inferred

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the same as the "deserted city" (荒城) of the next section, slightly east of modern
Kucha (uncertain); the dragon lake is not located. Watters: none (p. 62).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Assessment.** No authority proposes a site. Mizutani's equation rests on reading the text, not on any ruin. It
stands as an inference.

### 01.07 跋祿迦國 (Bāluka; Han 姑墨) — the Aksu region; the capital site is undecided

**Identifications.** Mizutani: old Gumo in the Aksu region; capital undecided (disputed). He lists Aksu (St. Martin,
Watters, V.A. Smith, Pelliot), Bai (Xu Song), Qara-yurgen (*Xinjiang tuzhi*), Yaka-aryk (Chavannes) and the Qarabag
ruins (Huang Wenbi). Vivien: Aksu province (p. 265). Beal: Aksu, 41°12′N 79°30′E (p. 24). Watters: Aksu district,
capital at Khara-yurgun (p. 65).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 1's distances and bearings takes
Kara-yulgun (Karayalghan) (Watters 1904; Xinjiang tuzhi (via Mizutani)) here, not Mizutani's Aksu region (old Gumo),
Xinjiang (route/README.md §6). The figures are one kind of evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 1 takes Kara-yulgun
(Karayalghan) (Watters 1904; Xinjiang tuzhi (via Mizutani)) here, not Mizutani's Aksu region (old Gumo), Xinjiang
(route/README.md §7).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji lists five capital sites (Wensu, i.e. old Aksu, after Feng Chengjun; Huang Wenbi's
Halabake; the *Xiyu tuzhi*'s Yaha-arik; Li Guangting's Kara-yulgun; Ding Qian's Baicheng) and prefers Wensu, on the
*Xin Tang shu*'s and Xuanzang's distances from Kucha and on Aksu's importance (pp. 66–67). He measures nothing; the
route tests here prefer Kara-yulgun.

**Assessment.** The country is securely the Aksu oasis. The dispute is only about which ruin was the capital, and
the sources assembled here cannot settle it. Mizutani stands.

### 01.07.01 凌山 — the Tianshan crossing: Muzart pass vs Bedel pass

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the main range of the Tianshan north of Aksu (Khan-Tengri, Pobeda); the pass is
disputed (Muzart: St. Martin, Beal, Watters, Adachi; Bedel: Hori, Takata). Julien: the Musart dabaghan (p. 11).
Vivien: Mousour-aola ("ice mountain"), c. 20 leagues N of Aksu (p. 266). Beal: the northern plateau of the Congling,
Mongol Musur-aola (p. 25). Watters: the Muzart (Ice Pass) (p. 66, tentative).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji leaves the pass open between the Muzart and the Bedel, but gives the Bedel its Tang
names: Du Huan's 勃達嶺 and the 拔達嶺 of the *Xin Tang shu* geography, and he quotes the *Xin Tang shu* that "north from
the 勃達嶺 a little over a thousand li" lies the Suyab valley with the Hot Sea to its east (pp. 68–69).

**Assessment.** The Western authorities here favour the Muzart; Hori and Takata, whom Mizutani reports, favour the
Bedel. Ji adds that the Bedel is the 勃達嶺/拔達嶺 of the Tang itineraries, and the *Xin Tang shu* road from it runs north
to the Suyab valley with the Hot Sea to its east, the same sequence as Xuanzang's 凌山, 大清池, 素葉水城. The range is certain;
the pass is open between the two, as Mizutani leaves it.

### 01.07.03 素葉水城 (Suyab) — near Tokmak on the Chu; Watters' Susamir position is an outlier

**Identifications.** Mizutani: near Tokmak, c. 40 km E of Frunze (agreed). Vivien: a lost town on the Chu not far W
of Issyk-kul; site not found (p. 271). Beal: on the Chu, perhaps Constantinovsk or Balasagun (p. 26). Watters: W of
Issyk-kul, S of Tokmak, near the Son-kul and the Susamir river (p. 69).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji: Ak-Beshim, 8 km SW of Tokmak, identified "on the Soviet excavation material", first
by Clauson (JRAS 1961) and accepted by Klyashtornyi and Mori Masao (*Kodai yūboku teikoku*, 1976); he refers to Zhang
Guangda, 碎葉考 (1979) (p. 72).

**Assessment.** Vivien and Beal put Suyab on the Chu, as Mizutani does; Watters moves it south over the Kirgiz range,
a materially different area. Ji supplies the site: the excavated town of Ak-Beshim, 8 km SW of Tokmak, identified with
Suyab from the Soviet excavations (Clauson 1961; Zhang Guangda 1979). Mizutani stands, made precise. Confidence high
on the excavated site; the map point should move from Tokmak to Ak-Beshim.

### 01.07.05 千泉 (bing gyul / ming bulaq) — Merke vs a Ming-bulak near Talas

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Merke at the north foot of the Kirgiz range, c. 120 km W of Frunze; he says there is
no dispute (agreed). Vivien: Ming-boulak in the heights c. 12 leagues E of Taras, taking Taras as the town of
Turkestan (p. 272). Beal: Myn-bulak north of the Aulie-ata–Tersa road under the Urtak-tau (p. 27). Watters: the
district N of the Alexandrofsky range between Aulie-ata and Aksu (p. 73, tentative).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 1's distances and bearings takes
district N of the Alexandrovsky range between Aulie-ata and Aksu (Watters 1904) here, not Mizutani's Merke, Kazakhstan
(route/README.md §6). The figures are one kind of evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 1 takes district N of the
Alexandrovsky range between Aulie-ata and Aksu (Watters 1904) here, not Mizutani's Merke, Kazakhstan (route/README.md
§7).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji names no modern site. He takes Pelliot's 屏聿 for Turkic Bing-yul and cites Ibn
Khurdādhbih's hill ringed by a thousand springs at Abārjāj between Isbījāb and Ṭarāz (pp. 76–77). That place lies W of
Ṭarāz, whereas the text reaches Talas by going W from the Thousand Springs (千泉西行百四五十里至呾邏私城), so it cannot be
Xuanzang's; Mizutani's Merke is not discussed.

**Assessment.** The name "thousand springs" invites matching with any Ming-bulak. Vivien's site depends on his
mistaken Taras (see 01.07.06). Watters' district along the north face of the Alexandrofsky (= Kirgiz) range includes
Merke. Beal's Myn-bulak lies in the same corridor. Mizutani stands.

### 01.07.06 呾邏私城 (Talas) — Aulie-ata/Dzhambul; Vivien's "Turkestan" town is rejected by the later authorities

**Identifications.** Mizutani: ruins near Dzhambul (formerly Aulie-ata) at the bend of the Talas river (agreed).
Beal: on the Talas river, Aulie-ata or Tersa 35 miles W of it (p. 29). Watters: near Aulie-ata, some miles SE of the
modern town (p. 81). Cunningham names Talas in his chronology (p. 645). Vivien: Taras "of the Jaxartes", i.e. the
town now called Turkestan near Otrar, not on the Talas (p. 273).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Dzhambul (formerly Aulie-Ata), and adds site evidence: Bernshtam's
excavations of 1936–38 showed that part of Dzhambul is built over the old city of Talas, whose plan he published
(1941); the present town of Talas lies 80 km ESE (p. 78).

**Assessment.** Vivien confused the Talas of the Talas river with the Taraz/Turkestan of the Syr Darya. Beal and
Watters correct this, and Mizutani follows them. Ji adds the site: Bernshtam's excavations (1936–38) found the old
city of Talas under part of Dzhambul. Mizutani stands; confidence high, on a continuously named town and an excavated
site.

### 01.07.06a 小孤城 (中國人) — small Chinese settlement 10 li S of Talas; unlocated

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no location; he discusses only the settlers' history (unknown). Vivien: a known
Chinese settlement, 10 li S of Taras (p. 273), without a site. Beal and Watters: none.

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji gives no location; he comments only on Chinese captives of the Turks (*Zizhi
tongjian*, 631) (p. 79).

**Assessment.** Nothing more exact than "10 li south of Talas" is available. The place remains unlocated.

### 01.07.07 白水城 (Ispījāb) — Sayram vs Watters' Mankent

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Sayram, c. 15 km E of Chimkent, the majority view; he doubts the basis for Watters'
Mankent (disputed). Watters: Mankent (also called Ak-su), c. 15 miles NE of Chimkend (p. 84, tentative). Beal:
Isfijab of the Persian writers (p. 29). Vivien: Isfijab, two days from Taras, apparently destroyed and site unknown
(p. 274). Julien: on the Aksu-gol (p. 15, tentative).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Sayram, c. 15 km E of Chimkent on the N bank of the Aris, citing Kashghari
and Belenitskii et al., *Srednevekovyi gorod Srednei Azii* (1973) (p. 80).

**Assessment.** Everyone accepts the equation with Isfijab ("white water"). The disagreement is between Sayram, the
later name of Isfijab, and Watters' Mankent, which he chose for its alternative name Ak-su. Mizutani's Sayram is
better grounded because it rests on the Islamic geographers (Minorsky, Le Strange). It stands.

### 01.07.08 恭御城 — unidentified town between Ispījāb and Nujkath

**Identifications.** Mizutani: not identifiable; Xuanzang's directions and distances here contradict the map
(unknown). Vivien: undetermined, perhaps a "Yenghi" a few leagues N of Sayram (p. 275, tentative). Beal: not
ascertained (p. 29). Watters: none.

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji: one of the c. 40 towns of al-Iṣṭakhrī's Chach-Ilak district (the Chirchik and Angren
basins), but "the excavations do not yet suffice to fix" it (Belenitskii et al. 1973) (pp. 80–81). This narrows the
region without a site.

**Name.** 恭御 is Koṅgo by the key (as in 恭御陀 Koṅgoda), so the town was *Koṅgo or *Kuŋgu. The only name of that shape in
the town lists of Shāsh and Īlāq is Kankiyāq/Kankrāq, on the left bank of the Chirchik one farsakh from Khudaynkath
(Barthold, *Turkestan* p. 173; Iṣṭakhrī's K.nk.rak, Minorsky, Ḥudūd comm. p. 357), in the right district near Nujkath.
But the vowels differ, the -rāq is not written, two characters match by chance easily, and Kankiyāq is itself not
located. An alternative lead, low confidence (data/names/).

**Assessment.** No authority offers more than a guess from distance, and Mizutani shows that the distances are
corrupt. Kankiyāq/Kankrāq on the Chirchik is a name lead without a site (see Name). The place remains unidentified.

### 01.08 笯赤建國 (Nujkath) — Nujkath of the Islamic geographers; exact site probable only

**Identifications.** Mizutani: probably Nujkath = Chichik (formerly Kosh-kurgan) on the upper Chirchik, c. 30 km NE
of Tashkent (uncertain). Vivien: Noudjkend of the *Masālik al-abṣār*, between Taras and Khojend, site undetermined
(p. 276). Beal: Nejkath of Edrisi, on the Chatkal (upper Chirchik) E of Tashkent (p. 29, tentative). Watters:
Noudjkend after St. Martin (p. 85, tentative).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji gives the name equation (Nujakath, "new town") and reports that the Soviet
archaeologists, on the excavation evidence, identify the town with Khanabad (Ханабад) in the Tashkent region
(Belenitskii et al., *Srednevekovyi gorod Srednei Azii*, 1973, p. 198); he does not give Mizutani's Chichik (pp.
81–82). The study has not seen Belenitskii and cannot place Khanabad.

**Assessment.** All accept the name equation. Beal points to the upper Chirchik, and Mizutani narrows this to Chichik
on Barthold's and Minorsky's evidence. Ji reports a different ruin, Khanabad, which the Soviet archaeologists identify
from excavation (Belenitskii et al. 1973), a source the study has not seen. Nujkath stands, but the site is now open
between Chichik and Khanabad, and the map point stays at Chirchik until Belenitskii can be checked. Low confidence for
the point, with the uncertainty that Xuanzang's distances cause (see 01.07.08).

### 01.11a 大沙磧 — the Kizil Kum, crossed between Ura-tyube and Samarkand

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Kizil Kum, with the route probably N of the Turkestan range via Ura-tyube,

of Ura-Tube; route perhaps NW to Jizak, then SW to Samarkand (p. 91, tentative).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Assessment.** The three agree on both the desert and the route; Mizutani's route is Watters' made precise.
Mizutani stands.

### 01.13 弭秣賀國 (Maymurgh) — Maymurgh near Samarkand vs Vivien's/Beal's distant Maghian

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Maymurgh, one of the southern satellite towns of Samarkand in the Zarafshan valley
(agreed). Vivien: Moughian (Maghîn), 38 leagues E by S of Samarkand; he rejects Meimorg (p. 280, tentative). Beal:
Maghian, 50 miles SE of Samarkand (p. 33, tentative). Watters: about 100 li (20 miles) from Samarkand; Maghian is too
far (p. 94).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji: Maymurgh, but SW of Samarkand. The *Xin Tang shu* puts 米 100 li S of 康, and the
Islamic geographers put the town of Maymurgh a stage from Nasaf on the Bukhara–Nasaf road; most scholars take it for
Čuma'abazar, but Ji leaves the site unfixed for want of excavation (Belenitskii et al. 1973) (pp. 90–91). He also
argues that the seven countries from 弭秣賀 to 貨利習彌伽 are all introduced with 至, not 行, and so are hearsay: Xuanzang
probably went straight from Samarkand to Kesh, as the Life's omission of 弭秣賀 and 劫布呾那 suggests (p. 91). The text
itself has 東南.

**Assessment.** Watters' objection on distance supports Mizutani. Maymurgh agrees in both sound and position with
Xuanzang's short distance from Samarkand, and the Maghian identification of Vivien and Beal fails on that same
distance. Mizutani stands.

### 01.14 劫布呾那國 (Kabūdhanjakath) — Kabudhanjakath N of Samarkand vs Watters' Mitan

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Kabūdhanjakath, one of the northern satellite towns of Samarkand (= Tomaschek's
Gubdan on the Bulanghyr) (uncertain); he notes Watters' Mitan. Vivien: Kéboud, 2 parasangs N (slightly W) of
Samarkand (p. 281). Beal: Kebud of the Arab geographers, position not given (p. 33, tentative). Watters: the Mitan
district, c. 30 miles NW of Samarkand (p. 97, tentative).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees on Kabudhanjakath and adds the royal seat 瑟底痕 = Ishtikhan, W by N of
Samarkand; with the town Kabudhanjkat, which he puts 2 farsakh E of Samarkand, it gives only the country's rough
position (p. 92).

**Assessment.** Vivien, Beal and Mizutani agree on the Kabud/Kabūdhanjakath name and a site close to Samarkand.
Watters' Mitan lies in the same direction but further out. The name equation favours Mizutani.

### 01.15 屈霜你迦國 (Kushāniya) — Kushāniya between Samarkand and Bukhara

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Kushāniya, c. 40 km NW of Samarkand (Le Strange) (disputed, against Watters'
Panj-shamba c. 100 km NW). Vivien: Koshaniya, midway between Samarkand and Bukhara, N of the Zarafshan (p. 281).
Beal: Kashania, half-way between Samarkand and Bokhara, c. 60 miles W of Samarkand (p. 34). Watters: near Panj-shamba,
c. 60 miles W/NW of Samarkand (p. 97, tentative).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 1's distances and bearings takes
Panj-shamba district (Watters 1904) here, not Mizutani's Kushaniya, c. 40 km NW of Samarkand (route/README.md §6). The
figures are one kind of evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 1 takes Panj-shamba district
(Watters 1904) here, not Mizutani's Kushaniya, c. 40 km NW of Samarkand (route/README.md §7).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees on Kushāniya, but gives no position (pp. 92–93).

**Assessment.** All agree on Kushāniya. The figures differ (14 leagues in Le Strange; "half-way to Bukhara" in
Vivien, Beal and Watters). The authorities here place it further west than Mizutani's c. 40 km, and Watters'
Panj-shamba agrees with Beal's 60 miles. The town is not in doubt, but its distance from Samarkand is. Mizutani
stands as to the name.

### 01.16 喝捍國 (Kharghānkath) — Kermine district; Kalkanata or Kermine itself

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Kharghānkath, probably Kalkanata c. 8–9 km N of Kermine, with local tradition in its

Kuan, the Zarafshan territory round Bokhara (p. 34). Watters: Kermineh district (p. 98, tentative).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 1's distances and bearings takes

### 01.18 伐地國 — name unknown; on the Bukhara–Oxus road, near Chardzhou

**Identifications.** Mizutani: name and site unknown, probably near Chardzhou on the only road SW from Bukhara

### 01.26 鑊沙國 (Wakhsh) — the Wakhsh valley vs Julien's/Vivien's Osh

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Wakhsh country on the Kurgan-tyube plain (= Halāward) (agreed). Beal: Wakhsh, N
of Shuman and Khotlan (p. 40). Julien: Och, after Reinaud (p. 26, tentative). Vivien: the territory of Osh in SE
Ferghana (p. 291).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji: the town is Lāwakand on the Wakhsh, 10 km W of Kurgan-Tübe, perhaps Kaun-Tepe
(Belenitskii et al. 1973), where Mizutani has Halāward (p. 110). Both lie in the Kurgan-tyube plain; the difference
concerns the capital, not the country.

**Name.** Bactrian: Waxš (οαχϸο) is the deified river Oxus and its god (BDNA II p. 243); doc. L (year 379 = c. 602) is
written "with the cognizance of the god Wakhsh whom we worship here in Warnu" (BDNA I p. 64). 鑊沙 wax-š renders it

**Assessment.** Osh lies far to the NE, beyond the Alai, and would break the sequence of small states on the northern
Oxus tributaries. Wakhsh fits both the name and the route. Mizutani stands. Confidence medium: a small state,
identified by name and route sequence with the authorities' agreement. No excavation, inscription or document naming
the country is cited (the Bactrian Waxš is the river and its god).

### 01.28a 活國 — Kunduz; Vivien's Ghori and Cunningham's Khulm are slips

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Kunduz (agreed; see 12.05). Beal: Kunduz (p. 39). Watters: Kunduz (p. 106). Vivien:
Ghour (Ghori), at the N foot of the Hindu Kush on the Kunduz–Kabul road (p. 288). Cunningham: "Khulm (Hu-o)", a halt
of one month, in his route chronology (p. 645).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: at the confluence of the Doshi and Talaqan rivers near Kunduz (his note at
12.05, p. 964), with a reference to the Kyoto University surveys of its Buddhist sites (Mizuno).

**Name.** 活 renders *War, which Marquart (Ērānšahr p. 85) makes the base of Warwālīz, the Valvālij of the Ḥudūd
(§23.73), i.e. Kunduz; see 12.05 (data/names/). Bactrian: the city Warnu (οαρνο, *Warnu-), "probably the later
Warwaliz (modern Qunduz)" in Sims-Williams' glossary (BDNA II p. 242), is where doc. L was written in year 379 = c.
602, with its citizens and the cult of the god Wakhsh (BDNA I p. 64). 活 ɣwat writes its first syllable War-, and
Sims-Williams himself reads 活 as War- in 活路 Huolu (EMC ɣwat-lo), a Hephthalite city, which he takes (after Yoshida
2003, p. 158b) for the base *Warl- of the people-name Warlogan (BDNA II p. 242); see 12.05 (data/names/bactrian.tsv). Yoshida (1998, pp. 36–37) independently confirms 活 = war = Kunduz; Kuwayama (2002, 2020) puts the capital town at Qal'a-ye Zāl on the Oxus (see 12.05).

**Assessment.** The country is the lower Kunduz–Surkhāb plain (Beal, Watters, Mizutani), supported by the name: 活
renders *War, the base of Warwālīz, and the Bactrian city Warnu is probably Warwaliz (see Name). The capital, by the
Life (都城在河南岸), lay on the south bank of the Oxus: the town site N of Qal'a-ye Zāl (Kuwayama 2002, 2020), not
Kunduz, 60 km inland; see 12.05, where the route figures, which favour Kunduz, leave the capital open. Cunningham's
"Khulm" is a single word in a chronology table and conflicts with 01.31 忽懍, which is Khulm. Confidence low for the
capital: text against figures, no excavation.

### 01.30 紇露悉泯健國 — Rūb + Simingān (Haibak) in the Khulm valley

**Identifications.** Mizutani: probably two names run together, 紇露 = Rūb (Rui) and 悉泯健 = Simingān (Haibak), with
the capital at Haibak (uncertain; his own hypothesis). Beal: the upper Khulm valley including Rui, Kuram and Haibak
(formerly Samangan), c. 42 miles W of Baghlan (p. 43). Vivien: Semenjan between Khulm and Baghlan (p. 288).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Visiting in 1924, Foucher described an unfinished monolithic stūpa (Takht-é-Rustam) and five
caves (chapels, dormitory, assembly hall) 3 km NW of Haibak (I, p. 124): Buddhist remains at the study's capital. He
does not connect them with 紇露悉泯健, and holds that Xuanzang's road went up the Balkh-āb and the Darrah-Yūsuf, not
through Haibak (I, pp. 49, 124).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: two places counted as one, Rub (Rui) and Simingān (Haibak), after Marquart
(p. 114).

**Name.** Bactrian: Rob (ρωβο, "a city, modern Rui", BDNA II p. 260) and Samingan (σαμιγγανο, "a district, modern
Samingan/Siminjan", p. 262) belong to one polity. Documents are dated "in the district of Samingan, at Sandaran, the
court of the khars of Rob" (doc. N, year 407 = c. 630, BDNA I p. 74) and at Marogan, "the market of the khars of Rob"
(P, c. 669; Q, c. 672; BDNA I pp. 82, 88), so in Xuanzang's years the khars (rulers) of Rob held the Samingan
district, which explains a compound name Rōb-Simingān. 露 renders rō (the final -b unwritten) and 悉泯健 Simingān, whose i
suits Islamic Simingān/Siminjān better than Bactrian Samingan; the remaining step is 紇 as a prefix before initial r-,
as 曷 in 曷邏胡 Rāgh (data/names/bactrian.tsv).

**Kuwayama (2002).** Agrees: 紇露悉泯健 is Rui and Samangan in the upper and middle Khulm valley, after Minorsky (p. 133).

**Assessment.** Mizutani's double name (after Marquart; Beal already joins Rui and Samangan/Haibak, Vivien has
Samangan) was a conjecture; the Bactrian documents now make it well supported. About 630 the khars of Rob held court
at Sandaran in the district of Samingan (see Name), so 紇露悉泯健 names one realm, Rōb with Samingān, not two places run
together by error. Foucher's rock-cut stūpa and caves show a Buddhist centre at Haibak; that Xuanzang's road missed it
fits a country he describes without visiting. The documents attest the polity and its district, not a site: the court
was at Sandaran, which is unlocated, so Haibak as the capital remains Mizutani's assumption, and the 紇 prefix is the
open step in the name. Mizutani stands, strengthened. Confidence medium: documentary evidence of the polity, no site
evidence for the capital.

### 01.37.01a 鍮石釋迦佛立像 — the 35 m Buddha at Bamiyan; Beal reads "brass" literally

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the 35 m Buddha, c. 400 m from the 53 m one; the "brass" is either a metal-covered
surface or Xuanzang's error (agreed). Watters: Captain Talbot's 120-ft "female figure" (Shah-mameh), c. ¼ mile from
the larger image (p. 119, tentative). Beal: a cast metal statue, probably brass or bronze (p. 51, tentative).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Agrees on the 35 m Buddha, the older of the two, and explains the "brass" (鍮石) as a metal
sheathing over the stucco-finished stone in 630 (I, p. 134).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the 35 m Buddha, 400 m from the 53 m one, 鍮石 being a brass coating (p. 131).

**Kuwayama (1990, 2002).** The East Buddha (38 m by his measure), on the image with Mizutani. But 鍮石 is an error:
the figure is clay over a carved conglomerate core like the West Buddha, and gold paint survives over its left cheek
and neck, which a brass sheathing would have made needless (東大佛は…金色（黄）を一面に塗った彩色が残っていて、鍮石製というの
は誤りである); 分身別鑄，總合成立 is then a method "imagined at the desk" when the Xiyu ji was written (1990, p. 1438, n. 98
pp. 1611–12; 2002, p. 159: "no trace of metal casting on the 38 m Buddha"). Like the West Buddha it belongs after the
mid-6th-century opening of the Bāmiyān road (see 01.37.01).

**Assessment.** Watters and Mizutani identify the second colossus by the distance from the first. Beal does not
point to a different extant object; he takes the material at face value. Kuwayama's survey evidence (clay surface,
gold paint) tells against Foucher's and Ji's metal sheathing: 鍮石 is probably Xuanzang's (or his editor's) error.
The identification is unaffected. Mizutani stands.

### 01.37.02 佛入涅槃臥像伽藍 — the reclining-Buddha monastery at Bamiyan: E end of the Buddha cliff (Foucher 1942) vs the Ajdahā rock W

**Identifications.** Mizutani: not identified; he reports the text (E 2–3 li), the Song edition (12–13 li) and
Foucher's emendation (W 12 li, a natural rock formation 300 m long resembling a reclining Buddha) (disputed).
Watters: 2–3 li E of the capital; perhaps Yule's Azdaha figure (p. 119, tentative). Beal: the text is probably
corrupt as to length (p. 52).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Foucher withdrew the western site that Mizutani reports in his name. He had first taken the
1,000-ft image for "les 300 mètres bien comptés du rocher qui s'appelle aujourd'hui Ajdahâ", W of Bāmiyān, but after
Pelliot's translation he sought it c. 1 km E of the monastery between the two standing Buddhas, "à l'endroit où
l'extrémité orientale de la « falaise des grands Buddhas » vient mourir" on the mouth of a large ravine from the
north. The image, he thinks, was built of river pebbles bound with clay (I, p. 133).

**Kuwayama (1990, 2002).** The reclining Buddha lay inside the monastery built by the 先王 between the two colossi (E
of the West Buddha, W of the East Buddha), now vanished: the Life's 像東有伽藍，伽藍東有鍮石釋迦立像…伽藍內有佛入涅槃臥像
names it "inside the monastery" just described, and of the three images only this one is said to be indoors
(先代王建立の臥像伽藍は東西二大立佛の中間にあった; 1990, p. 1444; 2002, p. 154: "Between two standing colossi is a monastery
which housed a huge lying Buddha"). Daoxuan's 釋迦方志 has 涅槃臥素佛, 素 for 塑, so the image was of clay (1990, pp.
1436–37), housed in a building as at Tapa Sardar (Ghazni) and Adjina Tepa (pp. 1444–53). The 先王 is the king just
before Xuanzang's host, late 6th c., not "a former king" (p. 1444; 2002, pp. 154–55). He reads the Xiyu ji's 城東二三里
as the distance of this same monastery from the city and does not discuss Foucher's eastern site.

**Assessment.** With Foucher's withdrawal the western rock loses its main support, and Watters' Azdaha contradicts his
own reading "E". Two eastern sites remain. Kuwayama's middle monastery follows the Life's wording, where 伽藍內 most
naturally refers back to the monastery E of the stone Buddha, and the Xiyu ji's 城東二三里 fits it if the city lay SW
of the West Buddha. Foucher's eastern end of the cliff, c. 1 km further E, reads the Xiyu ji's separate sentence as a
separate monastery. Nothing has been found at either. Alternatives, low confidence; the Life's wording slightly
favours Kuwayama's. Foucher still takes the Ajdahā rock for a Buddhist pilgrimage place, but not for Xuanzang's.

### 01.37.03 臥像伽藍東南僧伽藍 — monastery SE of the reclining Buddha: upper Helmand vs Fondukistan

**Identifications.** Mizutani: location unknown (disputed). He reports two views: (1) literally SE, taking 大雪山 as
the Koh-i-Baba, near Gardan-Diwal on the upper Helmand; (2) E along the Ghorband over the Shibar Kotal, at the
Buddhist site of Fondukistan. No other authority gives a site.

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Foucher takes Pelliot's "south-east" literally and measures the road from the reclining-Buddha
site by Topchi and Kalū over the Hajigak pass to Gardan-Divāl on the upper Helmand: 24 krur, "environ 70 kilomètres =
210 li"; the monastery is to be sought E of the S foot of the Hajigak (I, p. 135). He reports Hackin's unpublished
alternative, the pool of sacred fish at Sar-é-Cheshmeh, a source of the Kabul river on the E side of the Unai pass (I,
p. 137 n. 10).

**Assessment.** Foucher's measurement makes the literal south-east work: 200 li by the Hajigak road reach the upper
Helmand, and Hackin's Sar-é-Cheshmeh lies a stage further on the same road. Fondukistan needs the bearing changed to

(Gardan-Divāl or Sar-é-Cheshmeh), low confidence.

### 01.37.03a 黑嶺 (梵衍那–迦畢試間) — the Shibar pass vs the Hajigak/Unai cols

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Shibar pass area, the route running E along the Ghorband (uncertain). Vivien: the
pass between Bamiyan and the head of the Ghorband valley, the Hajigak and Unai cols (c. 11,000 ft), then the Jelraiz
valley at the source of the Kabul river (p. 293). Beal: the Siah Koh (p. 53). Watters: non-snowy ranges W of Kapiśa
(p. 122, tentative). Cunningham (01.37 entry): Bamiyan lies c. 100 miles W of Opian "by the Hajiyak pass and
Ghorband valley".

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Foucher (1942–47).** In 1942 Foucher holds that the highway still went down the Ghorband (Shibar) in the 7th
century, but that Xuanzang left it on leaving Bāmiyān, "de quoi il fut puni en se perdant" (II, p. 229; I, p. 49): his
excursion to 01.37.03 took the Kalū–Hajigak branch. With Hackin he lets the pilgrim cross the Paghman range from
Sar-é-Cheshmeh, where the storm of the Life overtook him (I, p. 137 n. 10).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji's note on Kapiśa's "black ranges on three sides" (三陲黑嶺) describes the E–W range of
central Afghanistan joining the Hindu Kush to the NE, perhaps the Paropamisos, as the boundary of India and the Hu (p.
137). It concerns the range, not the crossing, and gives no route.

**Assessment.** These are two real alternatives. Mizutani and Takata take the Shibar and the Ghorband; Vivien and
Cunningham take the Hajigak/Unai route. Foucher, cited for the Shibar, puts only the highway there: Xuanzang went SE
by the Hajigak to 01.37.03 and lost his way, on Hackin's view in the Paghman range beyond the Unai. If 01.37.03 lay on
the Hajigak side, so did the crossing. The study now leans to Hajigak/Unai, low confidence.

### 01.38 迦畢試國 (Kāpiśī) — the Panjshir–Ghorband basin round Begram; the Annual Reports accept Foucher's Begram, the gazetteers say only "Kabul valley"

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the basin at the Panjshir–Ghorband confluence near Charikar, c. 72 km NE of Kabul
(agreed). Cunningham: Kapisene = all Kafiristan plus the Ghorband and Panjshir valleys (pp. 21–24). Watters follows
Cunningham and suggests Kapis = Kafir (p. 124, tentative). Vivien: the Nijrao and Panjshir districts E of the
Panjshir (p. 298). Beal: Kapisa of Ptolemy and Pliny (p. 54). Dey: Kushan, 10 miles W of Opian, and equates the
country with the Chinese Kipin. Majumdar: the Sanskrit name is Kāpiśī (p. 671).

**Excavations.** The Annual Reports mention Kāpiśī once, in the epigraphy summary (Chakravarti, AR 1934–35). It
reports Konow's article on a Kharoṣṭhī inscription on a bas-relief from Begram, now in the Musée Guimet: "Begram is
situated on the confluence of the Ghorband and Panjshir rivers and has been identified by Prof. Foucher with ancient
Kāpiśī"; the inscription is "the first record in Kharoshthi that has been discovered in the neighbourhood of
Kāpiśī". It does not name the place.

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson, *Himalayan Districts* II (1884; also in the ATcu3 scan) only repeats: "Further east in
Kapisa in the Kabul valley there was a Kshatriya king (630 A.D.)". The Peshawar gazetteers (1883, 1899) say Gandhāra
"was a dependency of Kapisa or Kabul". Neither offers local evidence.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Begram "représente la capitale du Kapiça qu'a vue Hiuan-tsang" (I, p. 140). A coin of Eukratides
read "Kaviçi" shows the city goddess between a hill and an elephant protome, the two sacred sites Xuanzang names near
the city (II, p. 212; see 01.38.07).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Begram (Bāgh-i Ālam), 62 km N of Kabul, Kanishka's summer capital. The French
excavations from 1927 found coins of the 1st–5th c. and many art objects in the Kushan palace, and French and Japanese
work continued into the 1970s (p. 137).

**Kuwayama (1990, 2002).** Kāpiśī, with Mizutani, and the Sui 漕國 is this country, not 漕矩吒 (12.01): Pei Ju's 西域圖記
(606) puts 漕 last on the southern route before 北婆羅門 (…忛延、漕、至北婆羅門), and only Kāpiśī, through Lampāka, touched
North India; the *Suishu* has 帆延 700 li to its N; the *Tongdian* sets it 葱嶺之西南 and the *Tang shu* says 罽賓。隋漕國也;
early Tang usage never shortens 漕矩吒 to 漕; and the Sui 罽賓 that alone sent no envoy (唯罽賓不至) is Kashmir, while 漕
sent one in 616 (1990, pp. 677–711; 2002, pp. 203–5; Kuwayama 1975). The *Suishu* chapter thus describes Kāpiśī c.
580–620: the Zhun cult on 葱嶺山 (01.38.03a) and a king whose crown, read 金牛頭冠 with the *Tongdian* against the
*Suishu*'s 金魚頭冠, is the bull's-head crown of the "Napki Malka" coins (1990, pp. 722–30; 2002, ch. XIII); the
昭武/康國 pedigree is probably a confusion with Sogdian 曹國 (pp. 719–22). Earlier, Jinagupta spent a year in Kāpiśī
(c. 555) at the king's request, and Dharmagupta stayed in a 王寺 built by a king of Kashgar outside the city and then
two years 於國城中 (Xu gaoseng zhuan; 1990, pp. 665–76; see 01.38.05). He finds the Buddhist monuments round and N of
the capital and the Brahmanical ones (Tapa Skandar, Khair Khana, Hindu marbles near Sarai Khwaja) in the S, a division
the archaeology confirms (1990, pp. 1155–64; 2002, pp. 200–1). Of 比羅娑洛山 (01.38.07, no note) he says that since the
mountain god, not the mountain, takes the elephant form, Gaṇeśa was worshipped there (山神の形が象だというのであるから、こ
の山岳にガネーシャがまつられていたとみられる, 1990, p. 1149).

**Assessment.** The authorities differ on the extent of Kapiśa (whether Kafiristan belongs to it), but all centre it
on the Panjshir–Ghorband plain. The gazetteers add nothing. The Annual Report takes Foucher's Begram as established
and adds an early record from the site, but no proof of the name. Kuwayama adds the Sui name 漕國 and 6th-century
visitors, which date Kāpiśī's rise but do not move it. Mizutani stands. For the capital see 01.38a.

### 01.38.01 質子伽藍 (沙落迦) — the hostage monastery: Koh-i-Pahlvan/Shotorak, E of Begram

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the hill Koh-i-Pahlvan/Koh-i-Top E of Begram; which of the ruins it is remains
uncertain, one candidate being Shotorak (uncertain). Beal: under the northern mountain near Charikar, whose name
might derive from Sha-lo-kia (p. 59, tentative). Watters: none.

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Taking the text's "about 3 li E of the city, on the S slope of a mountain", Foucher points to
"une unique fondation bouddhique debout sur l'éperon le plus méridional d'une colline isolée" E of Begram
(Kōh-é-Pahlavān, Kōh-é-Tōp), with a monastery court of c. 30 × 40 m (I, pp. 140, 152). He does not mention Shotorak.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji places the monastery at the hill locally called 力士山 (Koh-i Pahlawan) and, like
Mizutani, names Shotorak at its N foot as one of its three sites, after Higuchi (pp. 139–140). On 沙落迦, the monastery's
name in the Life, he rejects Hatani's and Feng Chengjun's 疏勒 (Kashgar, with the exile Chenpan as the hostage), which
Mizutani follows, for Xiang Da's Luoyang: Pelliot's 娑囉誐 = 洛 of the Sanskrit Thousand-Character text and the Syriac
Sarag of the Nestorian stele, cited via Xia Nai (1965); Xuanzang knew Kashgar as 佉沙 and would not have named it
otherwise (pp. 139, 141–142).

**Kuwayama (1990, 2002).** The Life's 沙落迦寺, "大城の東三、四里の北山の麓にある大伽藍" (1990, pp. 1134–35), on Koh-i Pahlawan
E of Begram; he chooses no ruin among the hill's sites (two ruined stūpas on its W foot; Shotorak, Qol-i Nader and a
third site on its N foot above the Panjshir; p. 1157). With Mizutani, against Ji, the hostage is Kashgarian (沙落迦 =
疏勒, the 臣磐 of the 後漢書 疏勒國傳; 2002, p. 200: "the Shaluojia monastery built in honour of the Kashgarian hostage";
p. 277 n. 8 repeats the Life's "hostage from the Chinese emperor"). His English "at the northern foot of a hill"
renders 北山下 in the sense that would suit Shotorak.

**Assessment.** Beal's site rests only on the Charikar = Shalaka name echo and assumes the capital lay near Charikar
(see 01.38a). The hill E of Begram follows from the Begram capital, and Kuwayama agrees. The text puts the monastery
北山下, at the foot of a mountain to its north, with stone chambers on the ridge N of it (伽藍北嶺上有數石室): Foucher's
stūpa on the S spur fits this better than Shotorak at the N foot, which Ji and Kuwayama, like Mizutani, only list
among the hill's sites. On 沙落迦 the authorities divide (Kashgar: Hatani, Feng, Mizutani, Kuwayama; Luoyang: Xiang
Da, Ji); the Luoyang reading suits the Life's "son of the Han emperor" better, the Kashgar one Dharmagupta's 王寺 built
by a king of Kashgar (01.38.05), and neither bears on the site. Mizutani stands, with Foucher's ruin the better
candidate.

### 01.38.02 曷邏怙羅僧伽藍 (Rāhula) — Kafir Qaleh SE of Begram vs Kuwayama's Borj-e Kafir near Sarai Khwaja

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Kafir Qaleh SE of Begram, which has a stupa base and fits the direction and distance
(uncertain). Kuwayama: Borj-e Kafir E of Sarai Khwaja, a different site (below).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**K. Harimoto's notes.** Asks whether the monastery is at Mes Aynak (sketch, with 30 li ≈ 15 km). At the route model's
scale 30 li is c. 7–10 km; Mes Aynak is c. 82 km from Begram, and Xuanzang names nothing in the Logar.

**Kuwayama.** Kuwayama (2000, p. 26) identifies the Rāhula monastery with "a still standing stupa called Borj‑e
Kafir", "the one and only site" of the Buddhist group south of the capital. He places Kabul and its southern valleys
in 弗栗恃薩儻那, not Kapiśa (p. 57). The thesis and the 2002 collection say where it is: on the hills E of Sarai Khwaja (Mir
Bacha Kot), "極端にベグラームからかけはなれて存在する" (1990, pp. 1157–58), "Borji Kafir near Tapa Skandar" (2002, p. 187), i.e. c. 40–50
km S of Begram. He chose it because Xuanzang sets the Rāhula monastery apart from the northern Buddhist group and
Borj-e Kafir is the only Buddhist site standing in the south (2002, p. 200); he quotes 大城東南三十餘里 without reconciling
the distance. This is not Foucher's and Mizutani's Kafir Qaleh, c. 10 km SE of Begram (below).

**Foucher (1942–47), p. 141 (recovered).** Foucher found at Kâfir-Qaleh, about 10 km from the "Bêgrâm" of Kapiça, a
fortified quadrangle of 170 × 280 m and, 200 m north of it, a monastery with a stūpa mound 30 m across and a cell
court 25 m square, and took it for the Rāhula stūpa, "just in the direction and at the distance" Xuanzang gives. He
writes "au Sud-Ouest", but the Chinese has 大城東南 (south-east); the site is the study's, the stated bearing is not the
text's.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji puts the monastery SE of Begram without naming a ruin (p. 145).

**Assessment.** Two rival sites, not one stūpa under two names. Kafir Qaleh, c. 10 km SE of Begram, fits the text's SE
30-odd li (c. 15 km at Kuwayama's own 10 li ≈ 5 km) and has Foucher's stūpa mound and monastery court. Kuwayama's
Borj-e Kafir has a standing stūpa and suits his north–south division of Kapiśa, but lies c. 40–50 km S, three times
the distance, and he does not explain the figure. Kafir Qaleh is the better fit to the text; Borj-e Kafir is the
rival. Mes Aynak is excluded by distance. Mizutani stands, medium confidence.

### 01.38.03 霫蔽多伐剌祠城 — Tatarānzar S of Begram, or Kuwayama's Tapa Skandar (Śvetāśvatara?)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Tatarānzar, c. 12 km S of Begram, after Foucher (agreed as to site; the Sanskrit name
is disputed). Cunningham: Begram, and the name may survive in Tatarangzar at the SW corner of the Begram ruins
(p. 30, tentative). Beal: unknown, perhaps Pliny's Tetragonis (p. 61). Vivien and Watters: no site.

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson (1884) identifies Cartana/Tetragonis with Begram, 27 miles N of Kabul (hit coded
02.04.18). This is classical geography after Cunningham and is not tied to Xuanzang's town.

**K. Harimoto's notes.** Proposes that 霫蔽多伐剌 is Śvetāśvatara.

**Kuwayama.** Kuwayama reads the name as "Svetavara, probably a corrupt form of Svetasvatara", and makes the town the
base of the Śaiva ash-smearers of Kapiśa (2000, p. 27; 1972, p. 46). He identifies it with Tapa Skandar (Bala Hisar)
near Sarai Khwaja, "about thirty kilometers to the north of Kabul and some fifty kilometers south of Begram" (2000, p.
27; 1972, p. 54), where the Kyoto mission found in 1970 the marble Umā-Maheśvara seated on Nandin with Skanda (2000,
p. 26). The thesis gives the argument for the name: 霫 is 胥立反 (tsiəp, as Mizutani noted), and since the Tapa Skandar
pedestal hymn is in a Siddhamātṛkā with archaic forms and many errors, Xuanzang wrote the local pronunciation; the god
is Śveta[s]va[ta]ra, founder of an ash-smearing Śaiva school (after Hara Minoru 1961), and the town can only be Tapa
Skandar, "城壁で五角形にかこむ町" (1990, pp. 1223–29). He rejects Tatarānzar as too far north, Foucher's identifications
leaning to the north in general (pp. 1152–55), and counts Paitāvā, "ベグラームの南方七キロほど", among the northern
Buddhist sites (p. 1157). His distances do not agree: Begram–Tapa Skandar c. 40 km against the text's 四十餘里 (p.
1152), c. 50 km elsewhere (p. 1230; 2002, p. 201). Tapa Skandar II began by the very early 7th c. at the latest (2002,
p. 181). He also records Marquart and de Groot's *Spen-varas, "white hair" (2002, p. 278 n. 10).

**Foucher (1942–47).** Mizutani's Tatarānzar is Foucher's view of 1923: the ruins on the dasht of Tataranzar, 40 li S
of the city (I, p. 142). After a visit in October 1925 he put the town at "tertres importants de Paitâvâ et de
Bulandi-é-Padshahî", reached by a branch off the Jabal-us-Siraj road after Qarabagh, where Hackin's excavation
(December 1924) had found the square base of a stūpa faced with carved schist (I, p. 145). He reads the first two
syllables of the name as Iranian (Pahlavi *spêt*, "white") rather than Sanskrit *śveta* (II, p. 365).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji follows Mizutani's site c. 12 km S of Begram and doubts Watters' restoration on the
syllable count; the name is probably NW Prakrit (p. 148).

**Name.** By the key the characters give *Spētavarās: 霫 is s- with a -p coda before the labial, 蔽 pē (幫, a p, not v),
then ta-va-ra and 祠 for the shrine. Of the forms proposed only Julien's Sphītavaras fits, and Foucher explains its
first member as Iranian spēt "white"; Marquart and de Groot's *Spen-varas (via Kuwayama 2002, p. 278 n. 10) is of the
same kind. Śveta- would be written 濕吠 with a v, and Śvetāśvatara also lacks a member for -śva-ta-; Cunningham's
Saptavarṣa has no ṣ. The second member (-varās/-vara) is unattested. Better: *Spētavarās, low confidence
(data/names/).

**Assessment.** Three candidates. Tatarānzar fits 40 li from Begram but has nothing Śaiva, and its author moved away
from it. Foucher's Paitāvā/Bulandi mounds, in the Koh-daman beyond Qarabagh, mark a town with an excavated Buddhist
stūpa but no Śaiva evidence either, and Kuwayama counts Paitāvā with the Buddhist north. Tapa Skandar has the Śaiva
sculpture that Kuwayama's reading of the name calls for, but his own figures for it (40 or 50 km) are two or more
times the text's 40 li (c. 20 km at 10 li ≈ 5 km). The name test cuts against his reading as he gives it: the
characters write an Iranian *Spēt-, "white", not Sanskrit Śvetāśvatara. Kuwayama's own argument from 霫 is that
Xuanzang wrote a local pronunciation, and a local Iranian form of a name meaning "white" could still stand for a
Śaiva Śveta- name; but that is an extra step, and the second member does not give -āśvatara. The Śaiva link thus
rests on Tapa Skandar's sculpture and on Xuanzang's ash-smearers, not on the name. The study keeps the map point at
Tatarānzar and leaves the choice open, low confidence.

### 01.38.03a 阿路猱山 (*Arunau?, not aruṇa) — Khair Khana N of Kabul (Kuwayama), against the Chehel Dukhtaran peak (Cunningham, Beal)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no location (unknown); he discusses the name and the legend only. Cunningham: the
north end of the Siah-koh, called Chehel Dukhtaran, 3–6 miles S of Begram (p. 30, tentative). Beal: perhaps the
Chehel Dukhtarin peak (p. 61, tentative). Vivien: no site.

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Kuwayama.** Kuwayama (2000, pp. 27, 29, 36) takes 阿路猱 as Mount Aruṇa, a Sūrya cult place, and identifies it with the
'Trois Sanctuaires' of Khair Khana, the Sūrya temple excavated N of Kabul. On his reading of the 漕矩吒 story, the 䅳那 god
first held the mountain and left for Zābulistān before Xuanzang's visit (12.01). K. Harimoto's sketch notes only the
30 li from 霫蔽多伐剌祠城. The thesis and the 2002 collection spell out the argument (1990, pp. 1229–68; 2002, pp.
200–7). Hackin's excavation at Khair Khana found two superimposed shrines: a lower 'Ancien Sanctuaire' of three rooms
with a large central room and thick white plaster, and above it the 'Trois Sanctuaires', three equal cells of 38 × 38
× 9 cm bricks with a round-bastioned building H, which yielded a Sūrya with Aruṇa as charioteer, a second Sūrya
(found 1980) and sockets for nine images. Since the Sui 漕國 is Kāpiśī (01.38), the *Suishu*'s 葱嶺山有順天神 is this
mountain under its earlier name: the lower shrine was the Zhun (順 = 稿那) temple, the upper the Sūrya temple of Mount
Aruṇa, and the change fell between Pei Ju's 西域圖記 (606) and 629; the upper shrine is of the time of the last Begram
III (2002, pp. 204–6). The Khair Khana pass was Kāpiśī's southern border (1990, pp. 1262–66). The reading Aruṇa comes
from Hori Kentoku (解説西域記, 1912, p. 100) and is not argued phonetically (1990, pp. 1258–61). His distances are
Khair Khana c. 10 km from Kabul and c. 30 km from Tapa Skandar, Tapa Skandar c. 50 km from Begram, which he finds
"ほぼ照合する" with the text's 40 and 30 li as a proportion (p. 1230); it assumes his long Begram–Tapa Skandar stretch
(01.38.03), and the absolute figures are about twice the text's.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Mount Aruṇa was shown to Xuanzang "de loin" at Kāpiśī; Foucher reads Śuna as Śiva and puts the
god's later temple in Jāguḍa, where the Helmand leaves the mountains (II, p. 258). He gives no site for the mountain.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji, after B.C. Law (*Historical Geography of Ancient India*, 1976), takes 阿路猱 for
Aruṇācala, a mountain W of the Kailāsa range (p. 148). That is a Himalayan homonym and cannot be the mountain the text
puts 30 li from the Śaiva town of Kapiśa; it touches neither Khair Khana nor Chehel Dukhtaran.

**Name.** 阿 a and 路 ru/lu fit, but Xuanzang fixes the last character with his own fanqie 奴高反: 猱 is nau, a dental n
with an au/o vowel. Aruṇa would need 拏 for ṇ and the 歌-rhyme 那 for the final a, so aruṇa does not fit. The characters
give *Arunau or *Aruno; a vernacular -o ending (Bactrian or Gāndhārī) would explain 猱, but the key has no -o < -aḥ
case and no such form is attested. The Sanskrit library has no mountain Aruṇa (giri, parvata, śaila, acala) that could
be this one; Ji's Aruṇācala is a Himalayan homonym. Doubtful, low confidence (data/names/).

**Assessment.** Khair Khana is the first candidate with excavated temples, and Kuwayama's two shrines fit the legend
of a god displaced from the mountain. But its cult link (sun worship for 'Aruṇa') rested on reading 阿路猱 as aruṇa,
which Kuwayama took from Hori without a phonetic argument, and the characters do not give aruṇa (see Name). The
Zhun side of the argument (順 = 稿那, Sui 漕 = Kāpiśī) does not depend on the name and stands. Khair Khana also depends
on Kuwayama's Tapa Skandar for 01.38.03, from which it lies south as the text requires but at c. 20 km, long for 30
li (the same stretch as 01.38.03). Alternative, low confidence; Chehel Dukhtaran rests on the bearing alone.

### 01.38.04 大雪山 (龍池・迦膩色迦王伽藍) — Kanishka's monastery: Top-Darrah near Charikar

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Kanishka's monastery/stupa = the great stupa in the Top-Darrah valley near Charikar,
12 km W of Begram, though the direction is W not NW; the lake is not located (uncertain). Cunningham: the "great
snowy mountain" is the Hindu Kush itself, c. 35 miles NW of Charikar and Opian; lake not traced (p. 22).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 1's distances and bearings takes Hindu
Kush c. 35 mi NW of Charikar (Cunningham 1871) here, not Mizutani's Top-Darrah stupa near Charikar (route/README.md
§6). The figures are one kind of evidence; see the assessment.

**Foucher (1942–47).** The source of Mizutani's Top-Darrah: its fine workmanship, a drum over 75 m round and its
position at the mouth of the gorges whose floods the nāga legend explains make it "le seul monument qui réponde aux
descriptions du pèlerin" (I, p. 142).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees on the Top-Darrah valley near Charikar at the foot of the Paghman range,
citing Mizutani, but gives the distance from Begram as 26 km where Mizutani has 12 km (p. 153); Charikar lies c. 12 km
W of Begram, so the figure is probably a slip.

**Kuwayama (1990).** No site. He equates the monastery with the 罽膩吒王伐龍宮沙彌寺 that Wukong lists in Kāpiśī, so
it was still standing after 751 (pp. 1141–42, 1210–18); Top Darra appears only as the southern end of the Buddhist
sites along the E foot of the Paghman (p. 1157).

**Assessment.** Cunningham identifies only the mountain, Mizutani the monastery. The two do not conflict. Top-Darrah
is the only site proposed and fits the distance but not the direction; Kuwayama adds a later record of the monastery,
not a site. Mizutani stands.

### 01.38a 迦畢試國大都城 — the capital of Kapiśa: Begram vs Opian vs Kushan vs Nijrao/Tagao

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Begram (= Bāgh-i Alam), Kanishka's summer capital (agreed; French excavations from
1923). Cunningham: Opian near Charikar at the N end of the Koh-daman plain; Begram, 6–7 miles ENE, is instead
Kiu-lu-sa-pang (pp. 23–24). Beal: on the declivity of the Hindu Kush near Ghorband, or near Kushan 10 miles W of
Opian (p. 56, tentative). Dey: Kushan. Vivien: unknown, to be sought round Nijrao or in the Tagao valley, not at
Begram, whose plain belonged to Hupian (p. 299).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage on the capital. Atkinson (1884) identifies Opian with Alexandria ad Caucasum
and Begram with Cartana/Tetragonis (coded 02.04.18), following Cunningham's classical geography.

**Foucher (1942–47).** The source of the Begram identification. His visit of 1923 found two walled quadrangles
(Burj-é-'Abdullah and a second "royal city"), coins from the Indo-Greeks to the Muslim period, a stūpa parasol and a
door jamb; above all, the hostage monastery 3 li E fixes the site (I, p. 140).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Begram, 62 km N of Kabul (p. 137).

**Kuwayama (1990, 2002).** Begram, the "new royal city" on the terrace S of the Panjshir. The rival he answers is
Ghirshman's: having ended Begram III with the Chionite/Hephthalite invasion (late 4th–5th c.), Ghirshman put
Xuanzang's capital at Gunde Paisa (Ghunde Peisa), a low mound c. 5 km SE of Begram (Ghirshman 1946, p. 41), a view
ignored rather than refuted (1990, pp. 736–44). Kuwayama's answer is from the text: the 舊王伽藍 lies 王城西北大河南岸, with
a second 舊王伽藍 and the 舊王妃伽藍 to its SE, all without distances, so the city stood close to the S bank of the
Panjshir; Gunde Paisa, 5 km (c. 10 li) from the river, would have needed a figure (王城の遺跡としてはベグラーム以外にないの
である, 1990, pp. 745–50; 2002, p. 162, p. 278 n. 9). He then re-dates Begram III by its round bastions and stamped
pottery, linked with Tapa Skandar II and Khair Khana: "Begram III existed in the second quarter of the seventh
century" (2002, chs. X–XI, p. 172).

**Assessment.** None of the older authorities (Vivien, Cunningham, Beal, Dey) chose Begram; they offer four different
sites. Foucher chose it. Mizutani's Begram rests on Foucher's 1923 survey, the French excavations Mizutani notes, and
the consistency of the dependent sites (01.38.01–03a). Ghirshman's Gunde Paisa, the one rival proposed on excavation
grounds, depended on his early end for Begram III; Kuwayama's argument from the riverside monasteries and his
7th-century date for Begram III remove the conflict. The gazetteer passages only echo Cunningham's classical
identifications. Mizutani stands, with the excavated site carrying more weight than the older guesses.

### 12.01 漕矩吒國 (*jaukuta) — Zābulistān (Ghazni region) vs Arachosia vs Sewistan

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Zābulistān, the SE Afghan basin from Kabul to Kandahar, = the 謝䫻 of the *Tang shu*
(uncertain). Vivien: the country of Ghazna, the name perhaps from Arachotoi (p. 415). Cunningham: Arachosia, all of SW
Afghanistan except Kandahar (pp. 45–47). Beal: the same as Sewistan, judging from the return journey (I p. 62,
tentative); vol. II p. 283 adds Cunningham's Arachosia. Watters gives no site of his own (Arachosia region) (p. 265).
Majumdar: no site (p. 673).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**K. Harimoto's notes.** "Nobody appears to know who 䅳那天 is."

**Kuwayama.** Kuwayama (2000, pp. 29–33) equates 䅳那天 with the Zhun 順 whose statue the *Suishu* places on 葱嶺 in 漕國, and
argues that the Zhun cult left Mount Aruṇa (01.38.03a) for Zābulistān between 606 and 629. The *Suishu*'s 漕國 is
Kāpiśī, not this country (01.38): 漕矩吒 lay S of the Hindu Kush beyond 弗栗恃薩儻那, early Tang sources call it 漕矩, 漕利
(Daoxuan Zaoli) but never 漕 alone, and the *Tang shu*'s 謝䫻 (本曰漕矩吒，或曰漕矩。顯慶時謂訶達羅支。武后改今號) is the same country (1990, pp.
686–97; 2002, pp. 203–4, 278 n. 13). So the *Suishu*'s 順天神, 金魚頭冠 and 昭武 king belong to Kāpiśī, not to Zābulistān.
稿那呬羅山 is the Jabal az-Zūr of Yāqūt, whose idol Zūr = Zūn stood in Zamīn Dāwar on the right bank of the Helmand NE of
Girishk (Balādhurī: taken by Ibn Samura); "シュナ神とズーン神とが同一であるかぎり、ザーブリス ターンの南界はギリシク附近にあったものとみてよい" (1990, pp. 1262–66).

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 12's distances and bearings takes
Ghazni (Vivien 1858) here, not Mizutani's Zabulistan (SE Afghanistan) (route/README.md §6). The figures are one kind
of evidence; see the assessment.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 12 takes Ghazni (Vivien 1858)
here, not Mizutani's Zabulistan (SE Afghanistan) (route/README.md §7).

**Foucher (1942–47).** Foucher takes Jāguḍa for Jaghuri, a name "à peine défiguré" still on the maps, i.e. "la haute
vallée de l'Arghand-âb", which carried the 7th-century road from Arachosia to Kapiśa. He reaches it by 2,000 li NW
from the latitude of Dera Ghazi Khan, over the Toba-Kakar range and across the Ghazni–Tarnak valley, perhaps by
Kelat-é-Ghilzai, and doubts that Ghazni belonged to the country (II, pp. 231, 235–6).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Zābulistān, the Kabul–Kandahar region held by the Hephthalites, = 謝䫻 of the
*Xin Tang shu* (pp. 955–956).

**Name.** Jāguḍa is attested both as a north-western people (Mbh 3.48.21, with the Ramaṭhas; Mārkaṇḍeya P. 57.40, with
the Lampākas) and as "saffron" in the kośas (Rājanighaṇṭu 12.39), which suits the text's 宜鬱金香; Ji (p. 148) has the
same. The characters, however, give *Dzaukuṭa: 漕 dz- (從, unattested in the key for Indic j), the au, and the voiceless
ṭ of 吒 (ḍ would be 荼 or 擇) all depart from the key, which suggests a local form behind the Sanskrit one. Marquart's
link with Zābul (Ērānšahr p. 39) suits dz- but not -kuṭa. The name thus points to Foucher's Jaghuri (upper Arghandāb)
as much as to the Ghazni region. Medium confidence for Jāguḍa (data/names/). Bactrian: the country name is not in BDNA
I–II, but the chapter's 計多外道 is: Sims-Williams equates 計多 jiduo, EMC kej-ta, "designation of the worshippers of the
god Zhun in Zabulistan", with Bactrian κηδο kēd, a title "approximately 'priest'" (< *kaita-, MP kēd "magician"; BDNA
II p. 222). 計多 is then a transcription naming the kēd priests of 䅳那天 (Zhun), not a sect name; it bears on the cult,
not on the place (data/names/bactrian.tsv).

**Assessment.** The country is the Ghazni–upper Arghandāb–Helmand region on any view; Vivien, Cunningham and Mizutani
differ only in the label (Ghazna, Arachosia, Zābulistān). Foucher's Jaghuri adds a name match, as good as any the
region offers (see Name), and puts the core in the upper Arghandāb W of Ghazni, where the map point already lies; the
route test's Ghazni and Foucher's doubts about it (12.01a) leave open whether the country reached that far east.
Kuwayama's Zūn of Zamīn Dāwar puts the southern border, which Xuanzang marks by 稿那呬羅山, on the Helmand near
Girishk, where Foucher too put the Śuna temple; it fixes a border, not the core. The *Suishu* 漕國 material belongs to
Kāpiśī and is not evidence for this country. Beal's Sewistan is an outlier he corrects himself. Mizutani stands for
the region; Jaghuri is an alternative core.

### 12.01b 鶴薩羅城 (*ghasala) — Guzar on the Helmand vs Vivien's Hazara localities

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Guzar (Ghazoor, Guzuristan), chief town of the Helmand basin, after Cunningham
(agreed). Cunningham: Guzar/Guzaristan on the Helmand, Ptolemy's Ozola (p. 46). Beal follows Cunningham (II p. 283,
tentative). Vivien: uncertain, perhaps connected with the Hazaras, e.g. Assaïa-Hazareh between Ghazni and Kandahar on
the Tarnak (p. 415, tentative). Watters: none.

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Leaves the second capital unlocated: its spring "devrait permettre de localiser à coup sûr" the
site once the country is explored (II, p. 235). Pelliot's note (II, p. 240 n. 35) records that the *Tang shu* calls
this capital A-so-ni (*Asani).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: in the Helmand basin, perhaps Guzar (p. 956).

**Name.** 鶴 is γa(k), 薩 sa(t) or sar, 羅 ra/la: Mizutani's *ghasala is the literal reading, and with 薩's -t for r the
form could be *γasar(a) or *γasra. Nothing of this shape was found near Zābulistān in the Ḥudūd, Le Strange or
Barthold; Cunningham's Guzar is a vague modern name not in the files, and the *Tang shu* calls this capital A-so-ni.
Doubtful, low confidence (data/names/).

**Assessment.** Cunningham's Guzar has the closer sound, though only loosely (*γasar against Guzar, with s for z and a
for u; see Name), and is followed by Beal and Mizutani. Vivien's suggestion is a guess from the name only. Mizutani
stands, with "Guzar" itself only vaguely localised.

### 12.01c 羅摩印度川 (*ramaindu) — the Helmand

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Helmand (uncertain); he wonders whether it was confused with the Rāmid river
(Kafirnihan). Vivien: the Helmand valley (p. 415, tentative). Cunningham: the Helmand, the transcript having a Ho-
prefix (p. 45). Watters: the Helmand as identified (p. 265, tentative). Beal: none.

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Doubts that the upper Helmand belonged to Jāguḍa, calling the asafoetida argument a "spécieux
prétexte" (II, p. 235), yet he places the Śuna temple of Jāguḍa "vers l'endroit où le fleuve Hélmand sort des
montagnes" (II, p. 258).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: perhaps the Helmand (p. 956).

**Name.** 羅 ra/la, 摩 ma, 印 in, 度 du: a mid-name 印 marks a vowel-initial member, so *Rāma-indu or the like, but no such
form is attested (Sanskrit library and Iranian files searched; Barthold's Rāmīdh is the Kafirnihan, far away). The
Islamic Helmand spellings Hindmand/Hidmand (Le Strange p. 339) share the *ind- but not in this order, and 印度 is also
Xuanzang's own word for India, so the name may be half-translated. Doubtful, low confidence; the Helmand rests on
geography only (data/names/).

**Assessment.** Vivien, Cunningham, Watters and Mizutani give the Helmand on geographical grounds (Vivien and Watters
tentatively, Mizutani uncertain); Beal gives no site, and the name remains unexplained. Mizutani stands.

### 12.02 弗栗恃薩儻那國 — a Kabul-basin country: Kabul (Ortospana) vs the Opian/Charikar area

**Identifications.** Mizutani: a country in the Kabul river basin, exact location open (disputed). He reports Julien
(Vṛjisthāna), Shiratori (Portasthana = Ortospana = Kabul) and Takata/Mizuno (= 護時健 of the *Tang shu*). Vivien:
Vardasthāna, country of the Vardak Afghans near Kabul (= Ortospana), capital Hupian (p. 416). Cunningham: Ortospana =
Kabul with its Bala Hisar; he thinks Xuanzang interchanged the names of country and capital (pp. 39–41). Beal:
Parsusthāna, country of the Parsus, round Hupian/Opian (II p. 285, tentative). Watters rejects Vardasthāna and
Urddhasthāna (p. 267). Majumdar rejects the interchange and compares Pratiṣṭhāna (p. 672).

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson (1884; ATcu3 is the same text) writes of Kipin that it lay "along the upper sources of
the Kabul river and is the Hu-phi-na of Hwen Thsang". This repeats Cunningham's equation of the capital 護苾那 with
Kipin/Kophene and adds no local evidence (see the data note on the coding of this hit).

**Foucher (1942–47).** Foucher places Vṛjisthāna SW of Kabul in the Hazārajāt, "sur le versant méridional de la grande
chaîne du Kôh-é-Bâbâ", where the Helmand, the Kabul-rūd and the left-bank tributaries of the Logar rise; he hears the
name in the Ujaristān of the British map and takes the "Turkish" king for a Hazara (II, pp. 231–2). His route from
Jāguḍa runs c. 500 li N by the Dasht-é-Nāwar and the Bokan pass into the upper Logar or its tributary the Khavat
(Wardak), "toujours réputé pour le nombre de ses antiquités bouddhiques", then NE into the Kabul-rūd valley and E, as
the Life says, to the borders of Kapiśa (II, p. 232).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji gives no location: the meaning of the name is unclear and the position undecided (p.
959).

**Kuwayama (2002).** The district round Kabul, "from Maidan, or even Wardak, in the west to the Lataband Pass in the
east", = the *Tang shu*'s 護時健 (N of Zābulistān, SE of Bāmiyān) (p. 198); the name perhaps *Ūrdhasthāna, "high
region" (p. 258). Its Turkish king was a local minor power under Kāpiśī's hegemony, whose line later took the
Kāpiśī throne at Kabul (Huichao; Bīrūnī) (pp. 198, 264). He sets Kāpiśī's southern border at the Khair Khana pass
(1990, pp. 1262–66, see 01.38.03a), so the Koh-daman belongs to Kāpiśī, not to this country.

**Assessment.** Everyone puts the country in or near the upper Kabul basin. The question is whether it centred on
Kabul (Cunningham, Shiratori, Kuwayama), on the Koh-daman north of it (Vivien's capital, Beal), or SW of Kabul in
Wardak and the upper Logar (Foucher; close to Vivien's Vardak country, and within Kuwayama's district).
Foucher's route weighs: the Life takes Xuanzang from the country NE and then E to the borders of Kapiśa, which a
country in the Koh-daman, itself part of Kapiśa, would not need. The Koh-daman reading rests mainly on Opian (12.02a).
Kuwayama's Kabul district, reaching W to Maidan and Wardak, joins Cunningham's and Foucher's readings rather than
choosing between them, and his southern border of Kāpiśī at Khair Khana tells against the Koh-daman. The gazetteer
repeats Cunningham. Kabul with Wardak/upper Logar is the better reading and the Koh-daman the alternative, low
confidence.

### 12.02a 護苾那 (*hübina) — Hupian/Opian N of Charikar vs Cunningham's Kophene (not a town)

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Hupiān/Opian, a little N of Charikar (agreed). Vivien: Hupian (Opian), near
Alexandria ad Caucasum (p. 415). Beal: Hupian or Opian, 35°2′N 69°1′E (II p. 285). Cunningham: Kophene (Kipin), the
country on the Kabul river, not a town; he rejects Hupian (p. 40). Majumdar rejects Hu-phi-na = Kipin, since
Xuanzang does not render initial K with H (p. 672). Watters: none.

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson (1884; second scan ATcu3) repeats Cunningham: Kipin "along the upper sources of the Kabul
river and is the Hu-phi-na of Hwen Thsang". It gives no local evidence. Atkinson elsewhere also identifies Opian with
Alexandria Opiane, 36 miles N of Kabul (coded 02.04.18), but does not connect it with Xuanzang.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Questions Opian on route grounds: a village Opiān in the north of Kapiśa does not forbid the
pilgrim to find in his Vṛjisthāna, "à trente lieues plus au Sud", a chief town with a name like Hou-pi-na (II, p.
235). He proposes no site.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji: location unknown; "one view" is Hupiān/Opian N of Kabul (p. 959). He is as reserved
as the study.

**Assessment.** The direct sound match Hupian/Opian (Vivien, Beal, Mizutani) and Majumdar's phonetic objection both
count against Cunningham's Kophene. But Opian lies in the north of Kapiśa; if the country lay SW of Kabul (Foucher,
12.02), its capital cannot be there and the name match is a coincidence. Opian stays on the map with medium
confidence, tied to the location of 12.02.

### 12.02b 婆羅犀那大嶺 (varasena) — the Khawak pass

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Khawak pass, c. 3500 m (uncertain). Vivien: the Khawak pass at the head of the
Panjshir (p. 416). Beal: probably the Khawak (II p. 286, tentative). Watters: the Khawak, after St. Martin (p. 268,
tentative).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Khāvak, since the text has the descent NW into the Andarāb; the approach ran N along the E foot
of the Paghman range past Istālif, Opiān, Tutam-Darrah, Parvān and Gul-behār, with the Life's 屈露薩邦, where the king
left Xuanzang, near Paghman (II, p. 234).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: probably the Khawak pass (p. 960).

**Assessment.** The authorities agree, Beal and Watters tentatively, and Foucher adds the NW descent into Andarāb
(12.03). No rival pass is proposed, but nothing on the ground fixes the crossing: agreement only. Mizutani stands
(medium).

### 12.04 闊悉多國 (Xwast) — Khost (Baber's Khost), NE Afghanistan

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Khost, the valley of an upper Oxus tributary (uncertain). Vivien: the territory of
Khost, next to Andarab (p. 417). Beal: Khost, between Talikan and Indarab, after Yule (I p. 43). Watters: the old
Khost of Baber (p. 269).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Khost, on the way NW from the Andarāb to Kunduz; not the Khost W of the Kurram (II, pp. 234,
240 n. 32).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the Khost river valley in NE Afghanistan (p. 962).

**Name.** 闊悉多 is khwa-s-ta: 闊 keeps the w (溪, rounded), and 悉 + 多 write the cluster -st- in the key's way. This is
Persian Xwast/Khwāst (Marquart, Ērānšahr p. 81: Ps.-Moses' West = Pers. Xwast or Xust, the Kʿwoh-si-to of Xuanzang),
modern Khost (Ḥudūd comm. p. 341: the Khost valley); *khasta loses the w. Only the initial departs from Xuanzang's
practice, 溪 where he usually writes Iranian x with 曉. Medium confidence; the identification is unaffected
(data/names/). Bactrian: Khwastu (*χοαστο < *hu-wastu-, "good place", cf. OInd. Suvāstu-), in χοασταοιγο "(inhabitant)
of Khwastu" (BDNA II p. 279); doc. P (year 446 = c. 669, BDNA I p. 82) has inhabitants of Khwastu present in the
district of Samingan. 闊悉多 writes Xwast with the stem vowel -u unwritten, and the link with Samingan suits Khost within
reach of the Rob realm (data/names/bactrian.tsv).

**Kuwayama (2002).** Agrees: 闊悉多 is the Khost–Nahrin districts on the highland E of the middle Surkhāb, one of the
three countries Xuanzang says were subject to the Turks (with 瞢健 and 安呾羅縛); on his return Xuanzang crossed the Khawak
to the upper Andarāb and went 300 li N to Khost–Nahrin, then on to 活 (pp. 132–33, 194).

**Assessment.** The authorities agree unanimously, and the name match is good. Mizutani's hesitation concerns the
exact valley only. Mizutani stands. Confidence medium: a small district identified by name and the authorities'
agreement, and Mizutani himself is uncertain of the valley. Doc. P (c. 669) names inhabitants of Khwastu: documentary
evidence of the district and its name, not of a site. No excavation or inscription is cited.

### 12.05 活國 (*War) — Kunduz; Julien's Ghūr and Vivien's Ghori rejected

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Kunduz near the Oxus (agreed); he rejects Julien's Ghūr. Beal: Kunduz (I p. 39).
Watters: Kunduz, after Yule (p. 271). Vivien: a territory under the Turkish chief of Ghour (Ghori), NE of Andarab,
not the town of Ghour (p. 419, tentative).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Foucher (1942–47).** Kunduz, seat of the Turkish viceroy, the Khagan's son, on both of Xuanzang's visits (II, p.
230).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: near Kunduz at the Doshi–Talaqan confluence (Yule's Warwāliz, the Valvalij of
the *Ḥudūd al-ʿālam* and Bīrūnī, capital of Tukhāristān), with a reference to the Kyoto University surveys of its
Buddhist sites (Mizuno, *Bunmei no jūjiro*) (pp. 963–964).

**Name.** 活 is ɣwat: the ɣw- is Xuanzang's initial for Iranian w (as 和 = wā in 鞠和衍那), and the -t coda his usual
rendering of r (秣 mar, 呾 tar), so the name he heard is *War. Marquart (Ērānšahr p. 85) takes *War/Awar, with the Tang
阿緩 *Awan, as the base of the Arabic-Persian Warwālīz, the Valvālij of the Ḥudūd (§23.73), which is Kunduz. Medium
confidence (data/names/). Bactrian: the city Warnu (οαρνο, a u-stem *Warnu-, cf. OInd. Varnu-; Arrian's Aornos),
"probably the later Warwaliz (modern Qunduz)" (BDNA II p. 242), where doc. L was written in year 379 = c. 602, with
its citizens and the cult of the god Wakhsh (BDNA I p. 64). 活 ɣwat writes War-, the first syllable, as one-character
names do, and Sims-Williams himself reads 活 as War- in 活路 Huolu (EMC ɣwat-lo), a Hephthalite city, which he takes
(after Yoshida 2003, p. 158b) for the base *Warl- of the people-name Warlogan (BDNA II p. 242). His glossary thus
links 活 *War, Warnu and Warwaliz/Qunduz (data/names/bactrian.tsv). Yoshida (1998, pp. 36–37) independently confirms that 活 renders war "accurately" and that Markwart's Kunduz is "basically right"; the 滑國 of the Liangshu is the same name. He notes one difficulty: the Tang 阿緩/遏換城, long equated with 活, and 活路城 both occur in the Tang geography, which he resolves by taking 阿緩 as Kuwayama's Qal'a-ye Zāl, c. 50 km NW, and 活 (with 活路) as Kunduz. Enoki (1992, pp. 415–17) instead puts 活 at Ghar in the middle Surkhāb valley (not in the files); see the next paragraph.

**The capital: Qal'a-ye Zāl rather than Kunduz.** The Life (T50, 0252) says of the return journey: 至活國，居縛芻河側，
即覩貨羅東界，都城在河南岸, "the country lies beside the Oxus …; the capital is on the south bank of the river"; on the
way out Xuanzang reached 活國 after crossing the Oxus (渡縛芻河，至活國). Modern Kunduz lies c. 60 km S of the Amu and
is a town of the 19th–20th centuries; its Bālā Ḥiṣār, test-excavated by the Kyoto team in 1963 (Onoyama 1966), gave
5th-century red ware in its lowest street levels, "probably not ancient 活". Kuwayama (*Across the Hindukush*, 2002,
ch. "Towns in Tokharistan"; 「トハーラの境域と，藍市城と活国都城」, 西南アジア研究 91, 2020, pp. 119–132) therefore puts
the capital at the large town site N of Qal'a-ye Zāl, on the S bank of the Amu at the Kunduz confluence, where sherds
collected after 1967 are of a 7th-century type; he takes it also as Warwālij, the Ḥudūd's capital of Tokharistan, the
seat of the Tang 月支都督府 at 阿緩城 (遏換), and the Han 藍市. The site is unexcavated. Miyamoto (Tōhō gakuhō 90, 2015)
follows him for 阿緩城 and, after Yoshida 2003 and Grenet 2006, puts the Hephthalite seat 活路城 at Bactrian Warlu in
the Baghlān–Ghōrī plain, a different place from 活; Enoki's Ghar on the Surkhāb concerns 活路. Sims-Williams (1997,
2008) derives Warwāliz from *War(a)wā(ng)-liz, "the fortress of Warnu", and places it at Kunduz; the name War/Warnu
fits the district either way. Texts: data/ref/kyoto/.

**Assessment.** The country is the lower Kunduz–Surkhāb plain, as all agree, supported by the name: 活 renders
*War, the base of Warwālīz (Marquart) and the first syllable of the Bactrian city Warnu (Sims-Williams; see Name).
Its capital, however, was on the south bank of the Oxus by the Life's own words (都城在河南岸), which Kunduz, 60 km
inland, does not fit, and the Kyoto test trench at Kunduz found nothing earlier than the 5th century. Proposed: the
town site N of Qal'a-ye Zāl (Kuwayama 2002, 2020), with Kunduz the alternative. But the route figures favour Kunduz:
Khost → 活, 300 li (72 km predicted), is 100 km to Kunduz and 143 km to Qal'a-ye Zāl, and 活 → 瞢健, 東行百餘里 (24 km),
is 23 km from Kunduz and 69 km from Qal'a-ye Zāl (route/README.md §8). The text and the figures pull apart, so the
capital is left open: the map point stays at Qal'a-ye Zāl on the Life's words, confidence low; no excavation or
inscription decides it.

### 12.06 瞢健國 (*mungan) — near Khanabad/Taliqan vs Beal's Munjan

**Identifications.** Mizutani: between Kunduz and Taliqan, probably near Khanabad, with no matching name
(uncertain); Beal's Munjān (Minjān) is too far. Vivien: Moungan, an old town c. 12 leagues S of Jerm (p. 419). Beal:
probably the district from Talikan and Khanabad through the Farkhar valley; his heading restores Munjān (I p. 42,
tentative). Watters: perhaps Taliqan (Yule), not Mungan/Minjan (p. 271, tentative).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Route test (the Life).** On the Life's figures, the best-fitting combination for juan 12 takes Taliqan (Watters 1904
(Yule)) here, not Mizutani's Near Khanabad (route/README.md §7).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: not modern Mungān/Munjān but the Khānābād–Taliqan area, after Markwart (p.
965).

**Name.** 瞢健 is muṅ-gān: 瞢 with its -ŋ before a velar, and 健 the -gān of Iranian names (胡寔健 Gūzgān, 呾剌健 Tālaqān). The
name is Mungān/Munjān (Marquart, Ērānšahr p. 226, with Bīrūnī's Mundajān; Ḥudūd comm. p. 367, Sanglichi Mandežān), so
Mizutani's "no matching place name" is wrong. The modern Munjān lies far up the Kokcha in Badakhshan; Marquart (after
Yule), followed by Ji, extends the old district down the Bangi valley to the Tāliqān and Khānābād plains, which saves
the route. Medium confidence for the name (data/names/).

**Kuwayama (2002).** 瞢健 is "modern Talaqan", one of the three countries subject to the Turks (p. 132; "west of
Kunduz" there is a slip, p. 194 has "Talaqan to the east of Khanabad"); Xuanzang reached it from 活 in two days, as
the Islamic Warwālīz is two days' march from Tāliqān (p. 133). On the Tāliqān side, with Watters and the route test.

**Assessment.** The name is Mungān/Munjān (see Name), so the question is no longer a sound match against the route but
the extent of the district: the Munjān valley itself in S Badakhshan (Vivien, Beal's heading), or Marquart's larger
Mungān district reaching down the Bangi to the Taliqan–Khanabad plain, where distance and sequence point (Beal's text,
Watters, Mizutani, Kuwayama; the route test takes Taliqan). Mizutani's area survives only through Marquart's extension
of the district, which no source here documents for the 7th century. The Taliqan–Khanabad area stays the working
point, low confidence, with the Munjān valley as the alternative.

### 12.07 阿利尼國 (*alini) — the Arhang crossing near Hazrat Imam vs Vivien's Walwalij

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the old Arhang ferry on the Oxus, 26 miles N of Kunduz, near Hazrat Imam (agreed).
Beal: a district on both banks of the Oxus, chief place Ahreng = Hazrat Imam, after Yule (I p. 42). Vivien: N of
Moungan on both banks of the Oxus, perhaps Walwalij, though this seems too far W (p. 421, tentative). Watters: none.

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: the Oxus ferry Ārhan, which Minorsky, from Iṣṭakhrī, puts near, not at,
Hazrat Imam (p. 966).

**Assessment.** The characters give a-ri-ni (or a-li-ni), and Arhan/Arhang fits only the a-r-: there is no character
for the h, and 尼 gives -ni, not -han (data/names/). The identification therefore rests on the ford rather than the
name: Arhang/Ahreng is a known Oxus crossing that suits a country on both banks of the river, and Ji, after Minorsky,
puts it near Hazrat Imam. Vivien doubts his own alternative. Yoshida (1998, p. 37) notes that the Tang 遏換 sounds very close to
Ārhan; but 遏換 (阿緩) is the seat of the Yuezhi commandery, which Kuwayama and Miyamoto put at Qal'a-ye Zāl, the Tokhara
capital, not at the Arhan ford, so the resemblance says nothing about 阿利尼's site. Mizutani stands. Confidence low: a small district placed
by a crossing and the authorities' agreement, with a loose name; no excavation, inscription or document naming the
place is cited.

### 12.08 曷邏胡國 (rāgh) — Rāgh N of Faizabad vs Vivien's Roshan

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Rāgh plateau N of Faizabad at the bend of the Oxus (agreed). Beal: Ragh, a fief in
N Badakhshan between the Kokcha and the Oxus, after Yule (I p. 42). Watters: Ragh, after Yule (p. 273, tentative).
Vivien: perhaps the Roshan valley (p. 421, tentative).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji gives only Markwart's view: Rāwan, N of the Oxus beside Ārhan (p. 967). The text says
that 曷邏胡 "borders the Oxus on the north" (北臨縛芻河), i.e. lies S of it, and juan 1 lists it among the countries reached
by crossing the Oxus southward from Kumedh (南渡縛芻河); Rāwan fails on both counts, Rāgh fits.

**Assessment.** Since Yule, the authorities have accepted Rāgh as an exact name match. Vivien's Roshan predates Yule
and lies well to the NE across the Oxus. Mizutani stands. Confidence medium: a small district identified by name and
the authorities' agreement. No excavation, inscription or document naming the place is cited.

### 12.09 訖栗瑟摩國 (*kṛṣma) — Kishm on the Kokcha vs Vivien's Ishkashim

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Kishm, a village on the Kokcha between Taliqan and Faizabad (agreed). Beal: Kishm, N
of Farkhar, 32 miles E of Talikan (I p. 42). Vivien: Ishkashim at the entrance to Wakhan (p. 419). Watters: none.

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Route test (whole juan).** The combination of candidates that best fits juan 12's distances and bearings takes
Ishkashim (Vivien 1858) here, not Mizutani's Kishm, Badakhshan (route/README.md §6). The figures are one kind of
evidence; see the assessment.

**Text.** The Korean edition (Taishō) gives the country 東西十餘里. The Song, Yuan and Ming editions and the Japanese
manuscript 【甲】 read 千餘里, and so does the Jōō print of 1653, checked on the scan. A thousand li east to west suits a
country; ten does not (route/README.md §9).

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Kishm on the Kokcha between Faizabad and Taliqan, after Markwart; his edition
reads 東西千餘里 (pp. 967–968).

**Assessment.** Kishm fits both the name and the place in the sequence. Ishkashim belongs to the Wakhan/Shighnan
border (cf. 12.16a). Mizutani stands. Confidence medium: a small district identified by name and the authorities'
agreement, and the route test prefers Ishkashim. No excavation, inscription or document naming the place is cited.

### 12.10 鉢利曷國 (Parghar) — unidentified; Parghar (Markwart, Ji) and Farkhar both weak; Bolor and Parika fail

**Identifications.** Mizutani: no identification; he considers and rejects Beal's Bolor/Parika and the place names
Parkhar (N of the Oxus) and Farkhar (SE of Taliqan) (unknown). Beal: near the Varsakh, a tributary of the Kokcha,
perhaps Farkhar = Parika (I p. 42, tentative). Vivien: Bolor (Po-lo-eulh of d'Arocha), up the Oxus NE of Ishkashim
(p. 422, tentative). Watters: none.

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Text.** The Korean edition reads 北至鉢利曷國. The Song, Yuan and Ming editions and the 宮 manuscript read 東北, and so does
the Jōō print of 1653 (route/README.md §9). The text gives no distance, so the route test cannot choose between them.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji: Parghar, after Markwart. The *Ḥudūd al-ʿālam* has Parghar/Parkhar in Khuttal, and
Barthold places it on the N bank of the Oxus due N of Taliqan, where the Parghar (Kchi-Surkhab) river joins it (pp.
968–969). He reads 北至 with the Korean edition.

**Name.** 鉢利曷 fits Parghar in all three characters: 鉢 p (幫), 利 r (as in 貨利習彌伽 Khwārizm), 曷 γar (匣 ɣ- with -t for r).
Parghar (var. Parkhar, Farghar) is in the Ḥudūd (§6.7–8), where the Kulab river "flows into the Jayhun between
Badhakhshan and Parghar"; the village Parkhar (Farkhor) stands on the N bank of the Panj at the mouth of the Kulab
river (Minorsky; Barthold via Ji). Farkhar SE of Taliqan fits worse, since Xuanzang writes Iranian x with 曉 (喝, 貨, 忽),
not 匣. Medium confidence for the form (data/names/).

**Assessment.** Two names have been proposed, and both have a problem of position. Parghar (Markwart, Ji) fits all
three characters (see Name) and lies roughly N of Kishm, as the Korean reading 北 requires, but on the N bank of the
Oxus in Khuttal, whereas juan 1 lists 鉢利曷 among the countries reached by crossing the Oxus southward from Kumedh;
Minorsky (Ḥudūd comm. on §6.8) holds that the *Ḥudūd* "very rightly" places Parghar N of the river. Either the juan 1
grouping is loose and this is the N-bank Parkhar, or a S-bank place of the same name is meant; the name alone cannot
decide. Farkhar fits the characters worse and lies SW of Kishm, against both 北 and 東北; Mizutani rejects both without
reasons in the extract here. Vivien's Bolor is too far east. The place stays unidentified, with Parghar the better
name and Farkhar a weak alternative.

### 12.11 呬摩呾羅國 (himatala) — Darāim near the Khwaja Muhammad massif

**Identifications.** Mizutani: near the N end of the Khwaja Muhammad massif, where the village Darāim (Darah-i-im,
"snow valley") recalls the name (uncertain). Beal: Daraim (Darah-i-aim), after Yule (I p. 42). Vivien: unknown
(p. 423). Watters: none.

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji gives no site. With Markwart he reads the name as perhaps Hematāla, a form of
Hephthal, and takes the women's horned headdress for a Hephthalite custom (pp. 969–970).

**Kuwayama (2002).** No site. He reads Xuanzang's account (a first king of Śākya stock, the people dispersed under
many chiefs in fortified towns, living in felt tents) as the Hephthalites' fate after the Turks took 活: Himatala, "at
the gateway to Badakhshan", became their summer pasture while their winter seat stayed in the Baghlān–Ghōrī plain (pp.
132–33, 138). He rejects Funaki's placing of the Hephthalite capital badiyan in Himatala: royal towns lay in the river
plains, not in the mountains (p. 137). This supports Ji's Hephthalite reading, not a site.

**Assessment.** Beal (after Yule) and Mizutani agree, and the meaning of the name matches. No rival has been
proposed. Mizutani stands as probable.

### 12.12a 鉢鐸創那國大都城 — capital of Badakhshan: Faizabad or an older town on the Kokcha

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Faizabad (uncertain). Watters: on or near Faizabad, after Yule (p. 278, tentative).
Vivien: the old town of Badakhshan on the Kokcha, 7 days from Taliqan; the capital later moved to Faizabad and then
to Jerm (p. 423).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Kuwayama (2002).** In 726 Huichao, who uses 王住城 for royal towns, gives none for Badakhshan although the Tokhara
yabghu had fled there; Kuwayama infers that it had no major royal town (p. 134). That concerns the 8th century and the
term, not Xuanzang's 大都城; no site.

**Assessment.** All three put the capital in the Kokcha valley at or near Faizabad. Vivien distinguishes an older
town but places it in the same area. Mizutani stands.

### 12.13 淫薄健國 (*yambagan) — Yamgān on the upper Kokcha vs Vivien's Wakhan

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Yamgān (Hamakān) on the upper Kokcha above Jurm (agreed). Beal: probably Yamgan,
after Yule (I p. 42, tentative). Watters: Yamgan or Hamakan, after Yule (p. 278, tentative). Vivien: Wakhan, reading
NE for SE (p. 424).

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Yamgān/Hamakān, the Kokcha above Jurm (p. 972).

**Name.** 淫薄健 (yam-bak-gān) renders the Y.nb.qān of Ibn Khurdādhbih's revenue list of 211/827 (p. 37), which Marquart
(Ērānšahr p. 222) restores as *Yanbaqān > Yamgān; the 侵 rhyme of 淫 also allows Minorsky's Yumgān. An attested
9th-century form in the right place fits all three characters (data/names/).

**Assessment.** Yamgān is an exact name match, now with a 9th-century form that 淫薄健 renders closely (see Name), on the
correct bearing from Badakhshan, while Vivien has to emend the direction. Mizutani stands. Confidence medium: a small
district identified by name and bearing with the authorities' agreement. No excavation, inscription or document naming
the place is cited.

### 12.15 達摩悉鐵帝國 (dharmasthiti; 鑊侃 = Wakhan) — Wakhan; Vivien's Matotch rests on the name only

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Wakhan, the Ab-i-Panj valley (agreed); the Sanskrit name is unexplained, and he
doubts Markwart's "gate of Mastuj". Beal: the valley of Wakhan, also the Termistat of the Arab geographers (I

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees on Wakhan, but accepts Markwart's *Dar-i Mastit for the name, which Mizutani

**Assessment.** The alias 鑊侃 settles Wakhan. Vivien's Matotch (= Mastuj?) is an attempt to explain the

### 12.16a 尸棄尼國大都城 — capital of Shighnan: perhaps Ishkashim

### 12.18 朅盤陀國 (*kharbanda) — Sarikol/Tashkurghan; Vivien's Kartchu survives in Atkinson

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Tashkurghan in Sarikol (agreed). Beal: Sarikol with its chief town Tash Kurghan,
after Yule (II p. 299). Dey: Sarikol, capital Tash-kurghan. Watters: the Sarikol district, but "Tashkurghan cannot
represent the capital" (p. 287, tentative). Vivien: Kartchou, on an upper branch of the Yarkand river (p. 426).

**Gazetteers.** Atkinson, *Himalayan Districts* II (1884; also the ATcu3 scan), reports St. Martin: "M. de St. Martin
identifies the chief city of Khie-pan-to with Kartchu on the Yarkand river." He also routes Faxian and Song Yun
through it, and has Xuanzang reach it via a "Po-lo-lo celebrated for its gold washings" (this confuses the return
route). It is all reported from Vivien; there is no local evidence.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji agrees: Tashkurghan, the Han 蒲犁; he cites the Uighur version of the Life (Tugusheva
1980, p. 12) (pp. 983–984).

**Assessment.** The country is Sarikol on everyone's account; Vivien's Kartchu is the name of the district's river
valley. Only Watters doubts that Tashkurghan was the capital. The gazetteer simply transmits Vivien's 1858 view and
weakens nothing. Mizutani stands, and later work (not in these sources), from Stein onward, confirmed the Tashkurghan
fort. Confidence medium for the capital point: the country is certain, but 朅盤陀 is not a continuous name, Watters
doubts Tashkurghan as capital, and the Stein-era confirmation of the fort is not coded here.

### 12.18.04 大石崖 — the great cliff of Kabhanda: the capital itself or a cliff 300 li SE

### 12.18.05 奔穰舍羅 (puṇyaśālā) — the hospice: Shiratori's Kandar Punin vs Stein's Chichiklik

**Identifications.** Mizutani: SE of Tashkurghan, perhaps the Kandar Punin mountain, following Shiratori; he
reports Stein's Chichiklik NE of Tashkurghan (disputed). No other authority gives a site.

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji says only that the hospice is to be sought SE of Tashkurghan, from the text's
bearing, and names neither Kandar Punin nor Chichiklik (p. 990).

**Name.** 奔穰舍羅 is puṇyaśālā in every character, including ś for 舍 (書) and the echo coda of 奔 before ṇy; Mizutani's
Prakritised *pu(m)ñasala breaks the sibilant (s would be 娑 or 薩). The word is attested (Asaṅga, Śrāvakabhūmi, in the
list of deśavastu) and glossed 福舍 in the text, but it is a common noun and does not help locate the hospice. High
confidence for the form (data/names/).

**Assessment.** Stein's Chichiklik lies on the actual route from Tashkurghan to Kashgar/Yarkand. Shiratori's Kandar
Punin follows the text's "SE" and "300 li" (12.18.04). Mizutani prefers the text, while the route favours Stein. The
sources here leave the two equally weighted.

### 12.19 烏鎩國 (*uša) — Yarkand (or upstream) vs Yangi Hissar vs Chihil Gumbaz vs Ighiz Yar

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Yarkand or somewhere upstream, following Shiratori (disputed). He reports Cunningham
(Yangi Hissar), Yule and Hedin (Chihil Gumbaz) and Takata (Ighiz Yar). Vivien: Yangi Hissar (p. 427). Beal: "Och" as
a restoration only (II p. 288). Watters: none.

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji prefers Yarkand (莎車), with Shiratori and Herrmann, against Stein's (and Pelliot's)
site between Yangi Hissar and Yarkand; he adds the Uighur form ušar (Tugusheva 1980, p. 14) but no new argument (pp.
991–992).

**Name.** 烏 is u/o; 鎩 has three readings, and the usual 所八 (with -t) gives ṣar, since -t is Xuanzang's coda for r (秣
mar, 喝 khar). That yields *Ušar or *Oṣar, which the 10th-century Uighur version of the Life writes ušar (Tugusheva,
reported by Ji); but the Uighur translators transcribed the Chinese, so this is not independent evidence. No place
name of this shape was found in the Ḥudūd or *Serindia*. Low confidence; the name does not decide between Yarkand and
Yangi Hissar (data/names/).

**Assessment.** The text says the country lies south of the Sītā (Yarkand river) and produces jade. Mizutani argues
that none of the road stations fits "south of the Sītā", and that Yarkand fits the jade and the farming. Vivien's
and Cunningham's Yangi Hissar fits the route from Tashkurghan to Kashgar better but not the river. The question
remains open on these sources.

**Assessment.** This depends entirely on 12.19 and remains open with it.

### 12.22.06 勃伽夷城 (Bhagai?) — near Piälma W of Khotan

**Identifications.** Mizutani: near Piälma on the main road W of Khotan; Stein suggests it is the 渤海 of the Tang

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

### 12.22.12 媲摩城 (Phimmāṃna; Marco Polo's Pein) — Uzun-Tati vs Ulugh-Ziārat N of Chira; which ruin is which

**Identifications.** Mizutani: the Uzun-Tati/Ulugh-Ziārat area N of Chira Bazar. Stein and Cordier make 媲摩/Pein =
Uzun-Tati and 捍麼 = Ulugh-Ziārat; Mizutani reverses this (disputed). Beal: probably near Keria, = Pein and Song Yun's
Han-mo (II p. 324, tentative). Vivien: perhaps Pialma W of Khotan (p. 428, tentative). Watters: none.

**Gazetteers.** No gazetteer passage.

**Ji Xianlin et al. (1985).** Ji follows Stein for the Uzun-Tati – Ulugh-Ziārat area N of Chira, without dividing 媲摩
and 捍麼 between the two ruins; he puts Song Yun's 捍麼 temple at Ulugh-Ziārat, 3 miles S of Uzun-Tati, after Stein. He
adds that Hedin's Khotanese–Chinese bilingual document no. 24 makes 媲摩 very probably the Tang 坎城 (Bailey, *Khotanese
Texts* IV, 1961, pp. 135–137) (pp. 1028–1029).

**Name.** 媲 is a voiceless aspirate (滂 pʰ-), which fits Khotanese phimmāṃna and Tibetan Phye-ma (Ji, after Thomas and
Konow) exactly and rules out Watters' and Hori's Skt. Bhīmā (bh would be 婆, 毘 or 跋). The name confirms the chain to
Pein but does not decide between the two ruins (data/names/).

**Assessment.** Beal, Stein and Mizutani agree on the area between Khotan and Keria. Vivien's Pialma contradicts the
text's direction (it lies W). Within the area, the allocation of the two ruins is Mizutani's own reversal of Stein,
and the sources here do not settle it. The area stands; the ruin is open.

### 12.22.16 折摩馱那故國 (calmadana) — Charchan (Cherchen) vs Beal's Sorghak

**Identifications.** Mizutani: Charchan Bazar (且末), after Stein (agreed). Beal: Song Yun's Tso-moh, near Sorghak on

**Assessment.** The name chain calmadana = 沮末/且末 = Charchan is direct. Beal's Sorghak was a map-based guess for
