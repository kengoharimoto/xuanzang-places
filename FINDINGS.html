Xuanzang, Da Tang Xiyu ji 大唐西域記 (646) · a study of its places

Locating Xuanzang's Places

Every country and site in Xuanzang's record of the Western Regions, tested against the colonial survey, excavation reports, inscriptions, district gazetteers, the pilgrim's biography, early editions of the text, and his own distances and bearings. The study starts from Mizutani Shinjō's annotated translation (1971), used because it covers every place, and reports where the evidence keeps, strengthens or moves his identifications.

Settled by inscriptions and seals

For these places an inscription or sealing found on the site names the place Xuanzang describes.

  • Kuśinagara at Kasia: the Haribala plate in the Nirvāṇa stūpa.
  • Śrāvastī and the Jetavana: the Govindachandra plate and a Bodhisattva inscribed "in the Jetavana of Śrāvastī".
  • Kauśāmbī at Kosam: the Kara, Meohar and pillar inscriptions, and the excavated Ghoṣitārāma.
  • Puṇḍravardhana at Mahasthan: the inscription naming Pudanagara (Puṇḍranagara).
  • Nālandā: the monastery's sealings and the Bālāditya inscription.
  • Taxila: four inscriptions naming Takṣaśilā.
  • Karṇasuvarṇa: the Raktamṛttikā monastery's own sealings at Rajbadidanga.
  • Srughna at Sugh: a sealing reading "Sugha". Ānandapura at Vadnagar. Kanishka's stūpa at Shah-ji-ki-Dheri.

What Xuanzang's own figures show

Every leg of the route with a distance and bearing was tested: single sites, whole routes as each authority reconstructs them, and the Life's figures beside the Xiyu ji's.

  • The Deccan figures are inflated. South of the Mahānadī the stated li run two to four times too long, even between sites fixed by inscriptions at both ends: Andhra to Dhānakaṭaka is 1000 li for 85 km. The Life's figures show the same. Cunningham's Deccan sites fit the figures better because he chose them to fit; the inscription-fixed sites should not be moved on route grounds.
  • Sindh and Baluchistan. Mirchandani's route of 1967 (Las Bela, Khuzdar, Kachhi, Duki) fits the Xiyu ji's figures best as a whole: mean |z| 0.38 and 18° of bearing error, against 0.62 and 41° for Mizutani's. On the Life's different order the advantage shrinks to the bearings, and the Life puts Laṅgala nearer Las Bela than Persian Makran.
  • Kapilavastu. The Life's 800 li from Śrāvastī and 500 li on to Rāmagrāma pull Kapilavastu north-west of both Tilaurakot and Piprahwa.

The text

A scan of the Southern Song Sixi edition (1132; juans 1–8 survive) was read against the Taishō apparatus. Every Song reading it reaches is as the apparatus records, and the route sentences show no unrecorded variant.

  • Vīrasana (毘羅刪拏): the Song print and the Life read "south, cross the Ganges, south-west", the Korean base text "east, then south". Bilsar lies due south of Ahicchatra, so the Song reading fits.
  • Kanyākubja: here the Song and the Life read north-west and the Korean south-east; Kanauj lies east-south-east of Sankisa, so the Korean is right.
  • Sāṃkāśya's stairs: the monastery is east of the city in every witness except the Korean edition.

What Ji Xianlin's commentary (1985) changed

Ji's notes, coded for 356 places and folded into the notes, mostly agree with Mizutani. Where they report site or documentary evidence the study lacked, ratings rose: Suyab at the excavated Ak-Beshim (now on the map), Talas under Dzhambul, Termez with the Kara-Tepe cave monastery, Īśānapura named as Zhenla's royal city in the Jiu Tangshu, Valabhī from the Vala inscriptions and sealings, and the Vāśibhā monastery from Ahmed's Mahasthan excavation (known only through Ji). He opened questions at Kāmalaṅkā (Langkasuka on the Malay Peninsula, not Pegu), Mālava (Malwa with Dhār), the Lingshan pass (Bedel as well as Muzart), Nujkend, Khuttal's capital, 鉢利曷 (Farkhar or Parghar) and Bhagai. Where his arguments run against the Chinese text (Benares on the left bank, Maymurgh south-west of Samarkand, Rāwan north of the Oxus) the notes say so.

What the scholarship since 1985 changes

A bibliography of 384 later items was built region by region in English, Japanese, Chinese and European languages; 60 open-access items were read and coded (133 relevant passages, every quotation checked), and Indian Archaeology – A Review was extended to 2013–14. The books and paywalled reports that matter most are still to be read.

  • Kapilavastu is open again: the Piprahwa sealings may be later than thought and fix Faxian's city rather than Xuanzang's, while Tilaurakot is now an early walled town that nothing names.
  • Rāmagrāma gains a candidate, the brick stūpa at Parāsi (Ramgram) in Nepal, on Xuanzang's bearing and at his distance.
  • Gandhāra and Taxila: Varuṣapura falls to medium; the Bhīmā shrine is open between Karamar and Kashmir Smast; the Treasure stūpa at Taxila is open between Baoti Pind and the Dharmarājikā (Naiki 2021); a slab names Udabhāṇḍa near Hund.
  • Andhra: Pūrvaśaila at Amarāvatī is weakened, the stūpa standing on a plain where the text has a mountain (Shimada 2013). Vadnagar gains early sealings reading "Anandpur".
  • Khotan: the royal city at Yotkan falls to medium (Talati 2024); Phema's fort is probably the Kanaqin fort (Zhang 2016).
  • Magadha: a BHU survey and field reports give first candidates for Buddhavana (Ayer), Kālapināka (Nanan) and Kolika (Juafardih or Kool), all low; a sealing naming Śakrāditya was found just north of Nālandā.

Max Deeg's annotated translation (one volume per juan) and the Cardiff–Bihar "Xuanzang Trail" project's results are not yet published.

The original names

Many names carry reconstructed original forms that nobody had tested. A key to Xuanzang's own transcription, built from 262 names of certain form, shows what he marks (retroflexes, voiceless aspirates, ś/ṣ/s) and what he does not (vowel length, r/l, v/b/bh). Tested against it and searched in the Sanskrit texts and inscriptions, 17 of 47 forms in India and the North-West improve: Tamasāvana, Toyikā (Kāśyapa's town), Kheṭa(ka) for 契吒 (pointing to Kaira rather than Cutch), Mūla-Sāmbapura for 茂羅三部盧 (Multan's sun-city name), Mahāsāra, Kacaṅgalā, Lāhula, *Palola for Bolor (the Gilgit kings' title, which makes Gilgit an alternative to Baltistan). For twelve more places the key chooses among the forms Mizutani gives, e.g. vakṣu, Uraśā, Goviṣāṇa, Licchava. In Afghanistan and Central Asia 17 of 45 forms improve, read against the Islamic geographers: Khamdādh (Khandūd in Wakhan), Sutrūšna, Parghar, Andrāb, Xwast (Khost), *War for 活 (the base of Warwālīz, strengthening Kunduz), Khaṣa for Kashgar; 瞢健 is Munjān itself, while 鶴悉那 hardly fits Ghazna. Sims-Williams' Bactrian documents confirm several: 活 is the city Warnu, "probably the later Warwaliz (modern Qunduz)" though its capital is open: "on the south bank of the river" by the Life, i.e. the town site by Qal'a-ye Zāl on the Oxus (Kuwayama), while Xuanzang's distances fit Kunduz, and 紇露悉泯健 is Rōb with its district Samingān, the seat of the khars of Rob in documents of Xuanzang's own years. And 揭職 is not the village Gaz but *Garčig, Bactrian "mountain land", the mountain zone south of Balkh (Yoshida, Sims-Williams). All 359 forms have now been tested, including the 267 that built the key: 44 improve, 9 have alternatives, 17 stay doubtful (e.g. Ṭakka, kukkuṭārāma, vakṣu, and an Iranian *Spēt- "white" for 霫蔽多 that weakens its Śaiva reading). A name match is a lead, never site evidence on its own.

Still open

  • The capitals of Siṃhapura, Kaliṅga, Koṅkaṇapura and Jejākabhukti.
  • Śvetavat near Kapiśa: Tatarānzar, Tapa Skandar or Foucher's Paitāvā mounds.
  • Laṅgala's capital and Avaṇḍa: the Sindh–Baluchistan question.
  • Bhramaragiri: Arang or Nāgārjunakoṇḍa.
  • Kapilavastu: Piprahwa–Ganwaria or Tilaurakot. Rāmagrāma: Parāsi (Ramgram) or Dharmauli. The Bhīmā shrine: Karamar or Kashmir Smast.
  • 50 minor sites that the text gives no figures to place; 50 more placed only approximately from the text.

Not yet used: Ball's Archaeological Gazetteer of Afghanistan (1982; 2nd ed. 2019), and the books and paywalled reports listed as wanted in data/LITERATURE.md.

Sources and method

Mizutani's translation and notes; the identifications of Julien, Vivien de Saint-Martin, Cunningham, Beal, Watters, Majumdar and Dey; 季羨林 et al., Da Tang Xiyu ji jiaozhu 大唐西域記校注 (1985); the Archaeological Survey's reports (1862–87), Annual Reports (1903–37) and Indian Archaeology – A Review (1953–2014); Epigraphia Indica and the Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum; 557 British Indian district gazetteers and the Baluchistan gazetteers; Stein's survey memoirs; Foucher's La vieille route de l'Inde; Kuwayama, Mirchandani, Lambrick and the Hunnic Peoples sourcebook; the CBETA text of the Xiyu ji and the Life, with checked translations of every passage.

Language-model agents coded the sources under written instructions; every quotation was checked against the source text by script, and samples of the notes were reread. All assessments are provisional and rest on the coded quotes.
